key — what the evidence permits
The SiCp loading that gives the largest yield-strength gains (5 and 10 wt%, raising YS from ~93 MPa to ~113-124 MPa) is not the loading that improves creep resistance: minimum creep rate is pushed below AZ91 only at 15, 20 and 25 wt% SiCp, while 5 and 10 wt% curves overlap or sit slightly above AZ91. So the two mechanical responses to SiCp content diverge - the composition that helps yield strength most is not the one that helps creep resistance.
limits
  • Yield strength (a single stress value per wt%) is compared against creep behavior expressed as a curve of minimum creep rate versus applied stress at 175 C; these are not the same kind of quantity, so the 'gain' at 5-10 wt% in YS cannot be equated in size to the 'benefit' at 15-25 wt% in creep.
  • obs1 reports only qualitative curve position (below/overlap/above AZ91), not a numeric reduction in creep rate, so the magnitude of the 15-25 wt% creep improvement is not quantified and cannot be ranked against the quantified YS gains from step 1.
  • Creep is evaluated at an elevated temperature (175 C) while the YS values (obs2/step 1) carry no stated test temperature; if YS was measured at a different (e.g., room) temperature, the two properties are being compared across different test conditions, not just different compositions.
  • Only two points (5, 10 wt%) are described for creep as overlapping or slightly above AZ91 and three (15, 20, 25 wt%) as below - this supports a threshold-like split at 15 wt%, not a continuous dose-response curve.

handoff: Carry forward that no single SiCp loading optimizes both properties examined so far: 5-10 wt% is best for yield strength while at least 15 wt% is needed to see a creep-rate benefit relative to AZ91; treat this as a composition-dependent trade-off to be checked against any further property measured on the same wt% series.

— carrying: The creep comparison is only a qualitative curve position (above/overlap/below AZ91), not a quantified rate value, so its size cannot be weighed against the quantified YS gains.

— carrying: Creep was assessed at 175 C; the temperature at which YS was measured is not confirmed to match, so part of the divergence could reflect different test conditions rather than composition alone.

— carrying: Only five composition points are available for each property, and the creep split (15 wt% threshold) rests on qualitative curve position, not a fitted trend.

key — what the evidence permits
Placing the previous step's composition-dependent yield-strength (YS) trend alongside the creep-rate comparison shows that the two properties do not track the same composition dependence. The wt% SiCp levels that gave the largest YS increments (5 and 10 wt%, per the previous step) are exactly the levels whose minimum creep-rate curves merely overlap or sit slightly above the unreinforced AZ91 curve (obs1) -- i.e., no creep benefit. Creep-rate curves fall below AZ91 (better creep resistance) only at 15, 20, and 25 wt% (obs1), the loadings where the per-step YS gain is smaller (YS increments of +3, +9, +6 MPa vs +20, +11 MPa at 5 and 10 wt%, from the 93-142 MPa series in obs2). So the two steps together permit concluding that SiCp loading sufficient to improve creep resistance is higher than the loading that gives the largest tensile-strength gains; tensile strengthening and creep-rate improvement diverge across the same composition series rather than co-varying.
limits
  • obs1 reports only an ordinal comparison (below / overlap / slightly above AZ91), not a quantified size of the creep-rate difference at 5, 10 wt% vs 15-25 wt%
  • obs2 restates the same 93-142 MPa YS series already given in the previous step, so it adds no independent creep information; it only supplies the composition axis used to line up the two properties
  • the creep comparison is drawn from a single test-temperature/stress-range dataset (F7a) and six discrete wt% points (0,5,10,15,20,25); this supports an ordering/threshold statement, not a continuous relationship or generalization to other temperatures or stresses
  • YS (a single-point strength value) and minimum creep rate (a rate under sustained stress) are different kinds of quantities; showing they move differently with wt% SiCp establishes that they diverge, not why, and does not by itself show SiCp causes the creep-rate change rather than some other change in the specimens at higher loading

handoff: The composition needed for improved creep resistance (>=15 wt% SiCp per obs1) is decoupled from the composition that gives the largest tensile-strength gains (5 and 10 wt%, previous step); the next step should look for a microstructural or mechanistic reason for this threshold (e.g., via F3) rather than assuming the same mechanism drives both properties.

— carrying: creep comparison is ordinal (below/overlap/above AZ91), not a quantified magnitude

— carrying: based on discrete wt% points and a single test window shown in F7a; no interpolation or extrapolation is supported

— carrying: the divergence between YS-optimal and creep-optimal composition is established, but its mechanistic cause is not identified by this evidence alone
