The combination supports that x=0.01 has the best power factor over the whole temperature range, and at high temperature (above ~450–500 K) this also corresponds to the highest ZT among the doped samples — consistent with PF driving ZT there. But it does NOT account for the low-temperature (300–400 K) ZT ranking: x=0.005, despite having a lower PF than x=0.01 throughout, shows the highest ZT near room temperature. Since ZT = PF·T/κ, that crossover implies a lower thermal conductivity for x=0.005 that isn't shown in either panel. So the PF evidence explains part of the ZT trend but leaves the low-T ZT lead of x=0.005 — and thus the full PRP→PRF link — open; thermal-conductivity data would be needed to close it.

The upstream resistivity-PF result explains only the coarse split: the badly-doped x=0 sample is lowest in both PF and ZT, consistent with the earlier finding that its high resistivity (and separately its Seebeck behavior) suppresses PF. But it does not account for the ZT ranking among doped compositions: x=0.005 has the lowest PF of the doped samples yet the highest ZT, while x=0.01 has the highest PF but only the second-highest ZT. Since ZT = PF·T/κ, this mismatch shows thermal conductivity κ (not measured in either panel) must be doing real work in setting the ZT ranking — the PF/resistivity story cannot be extended to ZT. What's established: doped-vs-undoped contrast tracks across both quantities. What's left open: the composition-dependence of κ, and thus the mechanism behind the ZT ranking, is undetermined by this evidence.
