This is consistent, directionally, with the downstream YS trend (93→142 MPa, 0→25 wt%): more reinforcement present in the matrix at higher content is compatible with a monotonic strength increase (via mechanisms like load transfer or Orowan-type effects), and the qualitative match (more particles, higher YS, no panel showing a density drop against a YS drop) supports that the two measurements are not contradictory.

What it does not establish: the micrographs show particle presence and visual clustering only — no quantitative area-fraction, no porosity assessment, no matrix grain size, and no interfacial bonding information. Increasing clustering at 20-25% (panels e-f) is visually clear but YS still rises rather than falls, so the images cannot explain why clustering does not degrade strength here, nor can they isolate SiC content from matrix microstructural changes (e.g., grain refinement) as the driver of YS. So the combination supports plausibility/consistency, not a mechanistic or quantitative causal link between the imaged distribution and the specific YS values.

The optical micrographs (F1b-f) show dark SiCp particles distributed across the whole field of view at every composition, with local clusters rather than perfect dispersion; visually, particle density and cluster frequency/size increase progressively from b through f (captions confirm f-panels correspond to 15, 20, 25 wt% for d, e, f). Together, these support the modest claim that as particle loading increases, more reinforcement is visibly present and spread through the matrix, and yield strength increases in step with it — consistent with a particle-reinforcement effect on strength. What the combination does not establish is the mechanism (Orowan strengthening, load transfer, or grain refinement cannot be distinguished from micrographs alone), nor whether the visible clustering at higher wt% is responsible for the diminishing YS increments — that link is only suggestive, not measured. The micrographs also say nothing about interfacial bonding, porosity, or dislocation density, all of which could independently affect YS. So the pairing supports a qualitative dose-response between particle content and strength, but not a mechanistic or quantitative causal account.
