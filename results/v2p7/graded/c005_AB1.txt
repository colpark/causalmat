ANSWER 2
Panels viewed: F3e (spot temperature vs. concentration over 6 min, 200>100>50>25 µg/mL, water cold), F3g (spot temperature vs. laser power 0.5–1.5 W), F5c (tumor temperature vs. time, PBS ~41 °C at 5 min vs. BNs-BSA ~49 °C).

The upstream evidence (A: dose- and power-dependent spot heating in solution) partially accounts for the downstream in vivo observation (B: tumor heating to ~49 °C with BNs-BSA vs. ~41 °C with PBS). Both show the same qualitative logic — presence and amount of the absorbing material, under 808 nm irradiation, drives greater heat generation than the material-free/PBS condition — so the in vitro concentration/power trend is consistent with, and offers a mechanistic basis for, the in vivo PBS-vs-BNs-BSA gap in F5c.

What it does not establish: the in vitro series used defined, controlled concentrations and powers in solution, while the tumor experiment involves unknown intratumoral material concentration, tissue optics (absorption/scattering by tissue itself), blood perfusion, and a single fixed power density (7 W cm⁻²) — none of which were varied or measured against tumor temperature. So the combination cannot quantify how much of the tumor's extra ~8 °C rise is attributable to particle absorption versus tissue-level effects, cannot confirm dose-response in vivo, and says nothing about downstream therapeutic/ablation efficacy — only that the same qualitative "more absorber → more heat" pattern recurs from solution to tumor.
