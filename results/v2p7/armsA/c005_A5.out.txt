All three panels reviewed directly.

F3e/F3g (in-vitro, spot/dish IR imaging) establish that the photothermal response (PRP) is graded: temperature rises with nanomaterial concentration (200 > 100 > 50 > 25 ug/mL, water flat) and with laser power (0.5 -> 1.5 W) at fixed concentration, over the same 6-min window used later in vivo. This is solid dose- and power-dependence evidence for the material's intrinsic photothermal capability in solution.

F5c (in-vivo) shows a downstream service outcome (PRF/service_capability): tumor temperature reaches ~49 C with BNs-BSA vs ~41 C with PBS under 7 W/cm2 for 6 min — i.e., the injected nanomaterial produces a large temperature excess over a non-nanomaterial control in living tissue.

What the combination supports: the upstream dose/power curves make it plausible that the same concentration- and power-dependent photothermal property is the mechanism driving the tumor-vs-PBS temperature gap in F5c — both show monotonic heating attributable to the material rather than to the laser or medium alone.

What it leaves open: F5c uses a different system (solid tumor, not free solution), a different, unstated power density correspondence (7 W/cm2 vs the 0.5-1.5 W tested), and an unknown effective tumor concentration of BNs-BSA after injection/biodistribution. The in-vitro curves therefore support the qualitative mechanism but do not themselves quantify or guarantee the magnitude of the in vivo delta — that magnitude could also be shaped by blood perfusion, tissue optical/thermal properties, or injected dose, none of which the upstream panels measure.
