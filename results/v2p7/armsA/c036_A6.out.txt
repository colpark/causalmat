Both panels confirm the stated numbers. F5c (power factor): x=0.01 (red) sits on top of all curves across the whole 300-623 K range, from ~29 down to ~22 µW cm⁻¹K⁻¹; x=0.005 (black) is the lowest among the doped set (x=0.005/0.01/0.02/0.04), sitting below 0.02 and 0.04 throughout; undoped x=0 (green) is far lower still, rising from ~2 to only ~5. F7c (efficiency, Tc=300K): at Th=623K, x=0.01 (red) reaches ~11.8%, clearly above x=0.005 (~9.5%), x=0.02 (~10.6%), and x=0.04 (~9.5%) — matching the 9.5-10.6% band cited for "other x."

What the combination supports: the ranking is consistent — x=0.01 has the highest power factor across essentially the entire temperature range and also the highest conversion efficiency at the maximum hot-side temperature, and x=0.005 is the weakest performer on both metrics among the doped samples. This is a coherent, non-contradictory pairing: the sample with the best PF trace also converts heat most efficiently.

What it leaves open: efficiency in a thermoelectric device depends on zT (hence also on thermal conductivity), not on power factor alone, and neither panel shown here provides thermal-conductivity or zT curves, so the PF data alone cannot fully explain the magnitude or shape of the efficiency curve — only that the two rank orderings agree. Also, F7c omits x=0 entirely, so the undoped sample's very low PF cannot be cross-checked against a corresponding efficiency value. The evidence therefore supports a correlational/ranking link between PRP(power factor) and PRF(efficiency) for the doped compositions, but not a full causal or quantitative accounting of the efficiency result.
