Both panels were opened and verified against the stated claims.

F5c (power factor vs T) confirms claim A exactly: x=0.01 (red) is the highest curve throughout, ~29 µW cm⁻¹K⁻² at 300 K declining to ~22 at 623 K; x=0.005 (black) is the lowest of the four doped compositions; x=0 (green) sits far below all doped samples, rising from ~2 to only ~5.

F6a (ZT vs T) confirms claim B: x=0.01 (red) traces the highest ZT curve, peaking at roughly 1.25 in the 500–525 K range, again well above x=0.005 and the undoped x=0 sample (which stays below 0.25 throughout).

What the combination supports: the same composition (x=0.01) that gives the highest power factor across the whole 300–623 K range also produces the highest and peak ZT among the samples, with the peak ZT occurring in the upper-middle of the measured temperature range rather than at the PF maximum (which is at 300 K). This is consistent with power factor being a positive contributor to the observed ZT ranking and with x=0.01 being the best-performing composition on both metrics.

What it leaves open: ZT = PF·T/κ, and neither panel reports thermal conductivity. The measurements do not show why ZT peaks near 500–525 K rather than tracking PF's monotonic decline, so a drop in thermal conductivity (or the T-scaling term) at those temperatures is the more likely proximate driver of the ZT peak location — this cannot be confirmed or ruled out from these two panels alone. The evidence therefore supports correlation and consistent composition-ranking between PF and ZT, but does not establish that PF alone accounts for the downstream ZT curve shape or peak location; thermal-conductivity data would be needed to close that gap.
