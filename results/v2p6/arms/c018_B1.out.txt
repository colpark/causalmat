On the actual question:

Static assay (F5a-c: SEM after 2h PRP incubation, platelet counts, activation %) already established that BVLD/NO-PPAmF has the fewest, least-spread, least-activated platelets of the five surfaces. The ex vivo blood-circulation images (F6b cross-sections, F6c longitudinal photos, same figure as panels E-G: occlusion %, thrombus weight, blood flow rate) extend this to a simulated in-service condition: after 2h of actual blood circulation through the sample, 316LSS and PPAmF lumens are visibly filled with dark red thrombus and their longitudinal sections show thick red clot along the whole sample; BVLD-PPAmF and NO-PPAmF alone show partial, patchy thrombus with a partly open lumen; BVLD/NO-PPAmF shows an open, largely clear lumen with little to no visible clot, matching its status as the surface with the least platelet adhesion/activation in the static assay.

Together this justifies: the surface that suppresses platelet adhesion and activation in vitro also visibly resists thrombus buildup when blood actually flows over it — i.e., the antiplatelet effect translates into reduced clot formation under simulated in-service (circulating blood) conditions, not just in a static dish.

What it does not establish: F6b/F6c are single representative gross/cross-sectional photographs with no stated replicate count or statistics, so the visual difference is descriptive, not quantified on its own (the numeric occlusion/weight/flow-rate panels on the same figure would be needed for that, and even those carry no report of raw n or individual data points beyond error bars). Neither pair explains the chemical/biological mechanism of suppression, and the static PRP assay and the circulation model are different experimental setups, so their correspondence across surfaces is an observed pattern, not a tested or controlled link.
