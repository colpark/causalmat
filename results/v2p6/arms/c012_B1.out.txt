Both panels confirmed by direct inspection.

F5c (power factor, 300-623K): x=0.01 (red) is highest at every temperature (~29 to ~22), x=0.005 (black) is lowest among the doped samples, x=0.02 and x=0.04 sit between, and x=0 (green) is far below all doped samples throughout (~2 to ~5).

F7c (efficiency at Th up to 623K, Tc=300K): x=0.01 (red) is highest across essentially the whole temperature range, reaching ~11.8% at 623K. x=0.02 (blue) is next (~10.6%), while x=0.005 (black) and x=0.04 (magenta) are lowest and close together (~9.5-9.6%).

What the combination supports: for the top performer, x=0.01, the upstream chain (lowest resistivity → highest power factor, holding the PF lead from 300K to 623K) co-occurs with the highest downstream conversion efficiency at 623K. This is a coherent, consistent association — the composition that leads on resistivity/PF also leads on efficiency — and is compatible with PF being one contributor to the efficiency advantage.

What it leaves open: (1) Efficiency is computed from a full thermoelectric figure of merit that includes thermal conductivity, which is not shown in either panel here, so PF/resistivity cannot be said to mechanistically account for the efficiency result — only to co-vary with it for x=0.01. (2) The ranking does not fully transfer: x=0.005 has the lowest PF among doped samples, but in efficiency it is roughly tied with x=0.04, not clearly last, so the ordering among the three lower compositions is not preserved. (3) As with the PF/resistivity pairing itself, this remains an association across composition and two temperature endpoints, not a demonstrated causal pathway from resistivity/PF to efficiency.
