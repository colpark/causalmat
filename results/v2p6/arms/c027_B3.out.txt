No, the upstream evidence does not account for the downstream VEGF observation — the two patterns are actually discordant. The established upstream result is monotonic: Co2+ release order is 5Co > 2Co > 0Co at every timepoint (Fig. 5a: ~8.9/17.9/20.8 mg/L for 5Co vs ~4.7/10.5/16.9 mg/L for 2Co, 0 for 0Co), tracking the embedded Co/Ca ratio (0.49 > 0.14 > 0). But Fig. 8 shows VEGF secretion is non-monotonic in Co dose: 2Co-MBG produces far more VEGF (~430 pg/mL) than 5Co-MBG (~115 pg/mL), which is statistically indistinguishable from blank (~60) and MBG (~100). So the group with less released Co2+ (2Co) elicits a much larger biological response than the group with more released Co2+ (5Co).

What the combination supports: Co is released from the scaffolds in a graded, composition-dependent manner, and that released Co2+ is plausibly the stimulus behind elevated VEGF at the 2Co dose (VEGF rises above baseline with Co exposure). What it leaves open: why higher Co2+ exposure (5Co) fails to sustain or exceeds this stimulatory effect — the release data alone cannot explain the drop-off, since release doesn't decrease at 5Co, only VEGF does. This points to an unestablished non-monotonic (e.g., dose-toxicity or saturation) relationship between Co2+ and VEGF that the upstream chemistry data do not address.
