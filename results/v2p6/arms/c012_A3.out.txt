Both panels confirm the stated numbers. F5c (power factor vs T) shows x=0.01 (red) as the top curve across the whole 300–623 K range, running roughly 28–29 µW cm⁻¹K⁻² at low T and declining to ~22 at 623 K; x=0.005 (black squares) sits as the lowest of the doped set, and x=0 (green) is far below all doped curves, topping out near 5. F7c (efficiency vs Th, Tc=300 K) shows the same x=0.01 red curve on top throughout, reaching ~11.8% at Th=623 K, while x=0.005, 0.02, and 0.04 cluster lower, ending near 9.5–10.6% at 623 K.

This upstream-downstream pair is consistent and well-matched: the composition (x=0.01) that produces the highest power factor across the full temperature range is the same composition that produces the highest conversion efficiency across the full Th range, with the ordering of the other three doped compositions in F7c roughly tracking their PF ordering in F5c. So the evidence supports that the PF advantage of x=0.01 plausibly contributes to (is consistent with) its efficiency advantage.

What it leaves open: efficiency also depends on thermal conductivity/ZT and other inputs not shown in these two panels, so the panels alone cannot establish that PF is the dominant or sole driver of the efficiency ranking — only that the two trends co-vary in the same direction for x=0.01. No causal or quantitative (e.g., regression) link between PF and eta is shown; it is a directional correspondence across compositions, not a mechanistic decomposition.
