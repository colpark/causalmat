Both panels confirm the stated numbers. F5c: x=0.01 (red circles) is the highest PF curve at every T, ~29 µW cm⁻¹K⁻² near 300 K declining to ~22 at 623 K; x=0.005 (black squares) sits lowest among the four doped curves (0.005/0.01/0.02/0.04) across the whole range, with x=0.04 and x=0.02 close together above it; x=0 (green) is far below all doped curves, ~2 rising to ~5. F7c: x=0.01 (red) is again the highest efficiency curve, reaching ~11.8% at Th=623K, while x=0.005, 0.02, 0.04 cluster at ~9.5-10.6% at that same Th.

What the combination supports: the ranking is consistent — x=0.01 has the highest power factor at essentially every measured temperature and also delivers the highest conversion efficiency, so the upstream PF advantage is directionally compatible with, and helps account for, the downstream efficiency advantage. This is a real, panel-confirmed correlation, not just a caption match.

What it leaves open: PF alone is not sufficient to determine efficiency — the efficiency model (F7c) integrates over the full Th=300–623K range and depends on the material's full ZT (which also requires thermal conductivity, not shown in either panel), not on power factor in isolation. Neither panel gives thermal conductivity or ZT, so one cannot verify from these two figures alone that PF is the dominant driver of the efficiency gap rather than a favorable thermal conductivity for x=0.01, or how much of the ~11.8% vs ~9.5-10.6% spread is attributable to PF versus to conductivity/ZT differences. The panels establish a consistent, plausible link but not a quantitative or exclusive causal account.
