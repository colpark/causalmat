Both panels plot the same three samples (S1, S2, S3) against the same temperature axis, so the porosity series (A) and flexural-strength series (B) can be compared point-by-point.

The combination partially supports a porosity-limits-strength story, but only for two of three samples. S1's porosity collapses from 8.7% to 0.4% by 1600 °C while its strength jumps from ~165–188 MPa to 465 MPa over the same range — consistent with densification driving strength up. S3's porosity stays elevated (11.2% to 7.7%, never below ~8%) and its strength stays essentially flat (~170–195 MPa) — consistent with retained porosity capping strength. The earlier-established SEM panel (dark cavities on a fracture surface) is compatible with this qualitative picture of residual porosity but, as already noted, cannot be tied to a specific sample or temperature.

What this leaves open, and where it breaks: S2 contradicts a simple porosity-strength link. S2 densifies to 0.7% porosity by 1650–1700 °C — lower than S1's final value — yet its strength never exceeds ~170 MPa, far below S1's 465 MPa. So porosity reduction alone does not account for S2's strength behavior; some other microstructural factor (grain size, phase content, defect distribution) must govern S2's strength, and the evidence here does not identify what it is.
