The two panels justify a partial, sample-dependent link between porosity and strength, not a general mechanism.

For S1, the correspondence is strong: porosity collapses from ~7.5% (1500 C) to 0.4% (1600 C) exactly where flexural strength jumps from ~188 to 465 MPa — densification and strengthening co-occur at the same temperature. For S3, the correspondence is also consistent though undramatic: porosity declines only gradually (11.2 to 7.7%) and strength stays essentially flat (~170-195 MPa) across the same range — little densification, little strength change.

S2 breaks the simple story: its porosity falls substantially (7.9% at 1500 to 0.7% at 1700, lower than S3's 7.7% at 1600), yet its strength (80-170 MPa) remains the lowest of the three samples throughout, never approaching S1's peak and not clearly exceeding S3 despite far less residual porosity. So porosity reduction is necessary-looking for S1 but not sufficient to explain S2's strength, which must be governed by some other microstructural factor (grain size, phase content, flaw population) not captured in these two panels.

This evidence supports porosity as one contributor to strength (clearest for S1, plausible for S3) but leaves open why S2 fails to translate densification into strength, and gives no mechanism — only a temperature-matched correlation for two of three samples.
