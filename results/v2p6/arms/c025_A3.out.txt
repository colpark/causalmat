Panels confirm both upstream claims as stated. F3b (DLS) shows exactly the described contrast: the microfluidic sample is a single narrow intensity mode centered near 300-400 nm, while the hydrothermal sample is a broader mode centered near 1000 nm — clean, non-overlapping distributions. F4f/g/h show three independent scatter comparisons (siRNA, DOX, BSA), each with hydrothermal (green squares) sitting lower and microfluidic (magenta/blue open squares) sitting higher on encapsulation %, each pair marked with a significance star, consistent with the ~53 vs ~37%, ~47 vs ~34%, ~59 vs ~43% values given.

What the combination supports: across three chemically distinct cargos, the synthesis route that yields smaller, more uniform ZIF-8 particles (microfluidic) also yields significantly higher encapsulation efficiency than the route producing larger, broader-distribution particles (hydrothermal). This is a reproducible, cargo-independent association between the size/uniformity phenotype and encapsulation performance, which is suggestive of a real link between microstructure and loading capacity.

What it leaves open: the figures show correlation, not mechanism. Microfluidic and hydrothermal synthesis differ in more than particle size alone (mixing kinetics, precursor concentration, nucleation conditions), so nothing here isolates size/uniformity as the causal variable versus other route-specific differences. There is no intermediate-size data point, no direct structural measurement (pore size, surface area, defect density) linking microstructure to cargo uptake, and each encapsulation panel has only 3-4 replicate points, so effect-size precision is limited even though significance is marked. The evidence justifies "smaller/more uniform particles co-occur with higher encapsulation across cargos" but not "smaller particle size causes higher encapsulation."
