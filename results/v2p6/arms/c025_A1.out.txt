The four panels support the downstream claim, but only as an association tied to synthesis method, not an isolated causal link between size and encapsulation.

F3b (DLS) confirms claim A directly: the microfluidic sample shows one narrow intensity peak centered roughly 300-400 nm (teal), while the hydrothermal sample shows a broader peak centered roughly 800-1200 nm (magenta) — smaller, more monodisperse particles from the microfluidic route.

F4f, F4g, F4h confirm claim B directly: for all three cargos (siRNA, DOX, BSA), the microfluidic group (open squares) sits visibly above the hydrothermal group (filled green squares), each pair marked with a single asterisk indicating a significant difference. Values read roughly siRNA ~53 vs ~37%, DOX ~47 vs ~34%, BSA ~59 vs ~43%, matching the reported numbers.

Combined, the evidence justifies: particles made by the microfluidic method are smaller and more uniform, and — in the same paper's separate assay — they also encapsulate more cargo across three different molecule types, each difference statistically flagged. That is a consistent, reproducible co-occurrence of "smaller/narrower microstructure" with "higher encapsulation value" whenever synthesis route changes.

What it leaves open: none of these four panels test size and encapsulation on the same sample set or across a size gradient — they are two separate experiments (DLS on unloaded particles vs. encapsulation on loaded particles) linked only by sharing the same two synthesis conditions. So the panels cannot rule out that some other method-driven difference (crystallinity, defect density, mixing kinetics, precursor ratio) rather than particle size itself is what drives encapsulation. Causation from feature_size to PRP/value is not demonstrated here, only correlation via a shared upstream cause (synthesis method).
