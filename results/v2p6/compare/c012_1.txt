THE QUESTION
Does the upstream evidence account for the downstream observation about PRP/value / PRF/service_capability? Say what the combination supports and what it leaves open.

ANSWER 1 -- evidence only
Both panels confirm the stated numbers. F5c: x=0.01 (red circles) is the highest PF curve at every T, ~29 µW cm⁻¹K⁻² near 300 K declining to ~22 at 623 K; x=0.005 (black squares) sits lowest among the four doped curves (0.005/0.01/0.02/0.04) across the whole range, with x=0.04 and x=0.02 close together above it; x=0 (green) is far below all doped curves, ~2 rising to ~5. F7c: x=0.01 (red) is again the highest efficiency curve, reaching ~11.8% at Th=623K, while x=0.005, 0.02, 0.04 cluster at ~9.5-10.6% at that same Th.

What the combination supports: the ranking is consistent — x=0.01 has the highest power factor at essentially every measured temperature and also delivers the highest conversion efficiency, so the upstream PF advantage is directionally compatible with, and helps account for, the downstream efficiency advantage. This is a real, panel-confirmed correlation, not just a caption match.

What it leaves open: PF alone is not sufficient to determine efficiency — the efficiency model (F7c) integrates over the full Th=300–623K range and depends on the material's full ZT (which also requires thermal conductivity, not shown in either panel), not on power factor in isolation. Neither panel gives thermal conductivity or ZT, so one cannot verify from these two figures alone that PF is the dominant driver of the efficiency gap rather than a favorable thermal conductivity for x=0.01, or how much of the ~11.8% vs ~9.5-10.6% spread is attributable to PF versus to conductivity/ZT differences. The panels establish a consistent, plausible link but not a quantitative or exclusive causal account.

ANSWER 2 -- the same evidence, plus the earlier result
Both panels confirmed by direct inspection.

F5c (power factor, 300-623K): x=0.01 (red) is highest at every temperature (~29 to ~22), x=0.005 (black) is lowest among the doped samples, x=0.02 and x=0.04 sit between, and x=0 (green) is far below all doped samples throughout (~2 to ~5).

F7c (efficiency at Th up to 623K, Tc=300K): x=0.01 (red) is highest across essentially the whole temperature range, reaching ~11.8% at 623K. x=0.02 (blue) is next (~10.6%), while x=0.005 (black) and x=0.04 (magenta) are lowest and close together (~9.5-9.6%).

What the combination supports: for the top performer, x=0.01, the upstream chain (lowest resistivity → highest power factor, holding the PF lead from 300K to 623K) co-occurs with the highest downstream conversion efficiency at 623K. This is a coherent, consistent association — the composition that leads on resistivity/PF also leads on efficiency — and is compatible with PF being one contributor to the efficiency advantage.

What it leaves open: (1) Efficiency is computed from a full thermoelectric figure of merit that includes thermal conductivity, which is not shown in either panel here, so PF/resistivity cannot be said to mechanistically account for the efficiency result — only to co-vary with it for x=0.01. (2) The ranking does not fully transfer: x=0.005 has the lowest PF among doped samples, but in efficiency it is roughly tied with x=0.04, not clearly last, so the ordering among the three lower compositions is not preserved. (3) As with the PF/resistivity pairing itself, this remains an association across composition and two temperature endpoints, not a demonstrated causal pathway from resistivity/PF to efficiency.
