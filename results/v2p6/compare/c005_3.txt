ANSWER 1 -- evidence only
All three panels confirm the stated measurements. F3e shows spot heating scaling with concentration (200>100>50>25 ug/mL, H2O flat) over 0-6 min, colorbar 25-60C. F3g shows the same 200 ug/mL sample heating with laser power (0.5-1.5 W), 0-6 min. F5c is a line plot of tumor surface temperature vs time (0-5 min) showing BNs-BSA rising to ~49C by ~5 min against PBS reaching ~41C.

This upstream evidence (dose- and power-dependent photothermal conversion in vitro, plus elevated in-vivo tumor heating with the nanomaterial vs control) directly supports the claim that the material's photothermal property (PRP/value: temperature rise under NIR laser) is concentration- and power-tunable and translates in vivo to a therapeutically relevant hyperthermia range (>45-50C, associated with ablation). It justifies saying the material converts light to heat in a dose-dependent way and that this heating is reproduced, and amplified over PBS, in the tumor setting.

It leaves open the causal link to actual service capability/outcome (PRF): none of these three panels show tumor shrinkage, survival, histology, or any therapeutic endpoint — they are thermal-imaging/temperature data only. So the combination supports "material generates tunable, therapeutically relevant heat in vivo" but does not by itself establish that this heat produced tumor ablation or improved treatment efficacy; that inference requires additional (unreviewed) efficacy panels.

ANSWER 2 -- the same evidence, plus the earlier result
Panels viewed: F3e shows the 6-min IR series across 200/100/50/25 ug/mL and water, replicating the concentration-dependent spot heating described in Observation A. Does the upstream evidence account for the downstream tumor observation? Qualitatively, yes: the already-established absorption-mediated photothermal mechanism (concentration- and power-dependent in vitro heating, non-heating water control) predicts that BNs-BSA delivered to a tumor and irradiated will generate excess heat relative to a non-absorbing control, and F5c shows exactly that directional pattern (BNs-BSA >> PBS). That supports the material's photothermal activity translating from solution to tissue and reaching a temperature range (~48-49 C) generally associated with therapeutic photothermal ablation.

What it leaves open: (1) unit/setting mismatch — in vitro dose-response used discrete ug/mL and W values in solution, while F5c uses a single fixed BNs-BSA dose and 7 W/cm2 in tissue, so no quantitative curve links the two; (2) unlike the water control in F3e (which stayed flat), the PBS control in F5c itself rises from ~36 to ~41 C, showing laser/tissue alone produces substantial non-material heating in vivo — so these three panels alone cannot cleanly attribute the BNs-BSA tumor delta purely to the same absorption mechanism versus additional tissue/blood absorption or perfusion effects; (3) the prior caveats (no derivable conversion efficiency, no fitted rate law, scattering/convection not excluded) still apply and are not resolved by F5c.
