ANSWER 1 -- evidence only
Both panels confirm the numeric claims: F4a shows porosity falling with temperature for all three systems, most sharply for S1 (8.7→~0.4% by 1600 °C) and S2 (7.9→~0.7% by 1700 °C), while S3 stays elevated (11.2→7.7%). F6a shows flexural strength: S1 rises steeply to ~465 MPa at 1600 °C, S2 stays low (~80–170 MPa) across 1550–1700 °C, and S3 is essentially flat (~170–195 MPa).

The combination partly supports a porosity-strength link: S1's near-total densification coincides with its strength jump, consistent with lower porosity enabling higher flexural strength. But the pairing is not consistent across systems — S2 reaches porosity as low as S1's (0.7–2.3%) yet its strength stays far below S1's peak, and S3 remains highly porous (7.7–11.2%) yet matches or exceeds S2's strength and tracks close to S1's low-temperature values. So porosity reduction alone does not account for the strength differences between S1, S2, and S3.

What this leaves open: the figures give no microstructural, phase, or grain-size data, so whatever else is driving S2's persistently low strength and S3's porosity-independent strength (composition, phase content, crosslinking/pyrolysis chemistry, grain morphology) is not shown here. No PRP or "value" metric appears in either panel, so that part of the question cannot be addressed from this evidence.

Some factor beyond porosity (e.g., grain size, phase, microcracking) must govern S2's strength, and the SEM record offers no mechanism for it.
