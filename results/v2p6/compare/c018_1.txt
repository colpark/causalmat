Two answers to the same question about one set of measurements. Both answerers saw the same evidence. The second was additionally handed an earlier established result; the first was not.

THE QUESTION
how the material performs in service

ANSWER 1 -- evidence only
The five panels come from two composite figures (both files show a multi-panel figure; the OCR indices given map onto the same two images).

Fig. 5 (static, PRP incubation, 2 h): SEM (A) shows fewer, rounder, less-spread platelets on BVLD-PPAmF, NO-PPAmF, and especially the combined BVLD/NO-PPAmF surface than on bare 316LSS or PPAmF alone, which display dense, spread, activated platelets. Panel B (platelet number) and Panel C (%activation) quantify this: bare/PPAmF surfaces carry ~32-41x10^2 cells/mm^2 at 65-74% activation, versus ~15/11/~5x10^2 cells/mm^2 and 23%/18%/~0% activation for BVLD-PPAmF, NO-PPAmF, and the combined surface respectively (p<0.001 vs controls).

Fig. 6 (ex vivo AV-shunt blood circulation, 2 h): lumen photos (B), cross-sections (C), and SEM of the sample surface (D) all show heavy dark clot/fibrin-platelet coverage on bare 316LSS and PPAmF, versus open lumens and sparse deposits on the functionalized samples. Occlusion (E) drops from ~58-63% to ~3-10%, thrombus weight (F) from ~65-74 mg to ~2-5 mg, and blood flow rate (G) is preserved at 64-90% (vs ~18-20%) on the modified surfaces, with the combined BVLD/NO-PPAmF performing best (~90% flow retained, near-initial value).

Justified conclusion: across a static protein-rich-plasma assay and a dynamic ex vivo blood-circulation model, bivalirudin and NO surface modifications — individually and more so combined — markedly reduce platelet adhesion/activation and thrombus formation and better sustain blood flow than bare 316LSS or PPAmF alone.

Left uncertain: this is a single short-term (2 h) readout in one ex vivo animal shunt model; the images/captions give no information on coating durability, degradation, long-term patency, endothelialization, or clinical/in-vivo implant performance over weeks-to-years, so nothing about long-term in-service durability can be claimed from this evidence.

ANSWER 2 -- the same evidence, plus the earlier result
On the actual question:

Static assay (F5a-c: SEM after 2h PRP incubation, platelet counts, activation %) already established that BVLD/NO-PPAmF has the fewest, least-spread, least-activated platelets of the five surfaces. The ex vivo blood-circulation images (F6b cross-sections, F6c longitudinal photos, same figure as panels E-G: occlusion %, thrombus weight, blood flow rate) extend this to a simulated in-service condition: after 2h of actual blood circulation through the sample, 316LSS and PPAmF lumens are visibly filled with dark red thrombus and their longitudinal sections show thick red clot along the whole sample; BVLD-PPAmF and NO-PPAmF alone show partial, patchy thrombus with a partly open lumen; BVLD/NO-PPAmF shows an open, largely clear lumen with little to no visible clot, matching its status as the surface with the least platelet adhesion/activation in the static assay.

Together this justifies: the surface that suppresses platelet adhesion and activation in vitro also visibly resists thrombus buildup when blood actually flows over it — i.e., the antiplatelet effect translates into reduced clot formation under simulated in-service (circulating blood) conditions, not just in a static dish.

What it does not establish: F6b/F6c are single representative gross/cross-sectional photographs with no stated replicate count or statistics, so the visual difference is descriptive, not quantified on its own (the numeric occlusion/weight/flow-rate panels on the same figure would be needed for that, and even those carry no report of raw n or individual data points beyond error bars). Neither pair explains the chemical/biological mechanism of suppression, and the static PRP assay and the circulation model are different experimental setups, so their correspondence across surfaces is an observed pattern, not a tested or controlled link.
