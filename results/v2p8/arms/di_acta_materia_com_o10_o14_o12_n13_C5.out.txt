The established result settles that the two OM-defined band populations map onto two distinct EBSD-defined orientation behaviors, and that this pairing is consistent across the two morphology classes: bands with straight {10-12}/{01-12}-trace morphology and symmetric-appearing optical contrast correspond to the EBSD class showing a single uniform color (i.e., a discrete, spatially constant orientation, consistent with twin-like behavior), while bands with wavy morphology and the strong/faint (0001)-versus-(1-100)/(-2110) contrast asymmetry correspond to the EBSD class showing continuously shading color (a gradually varying orientation, consistent with kink-band-like behavior). So the conclusion it supports is a classification-level correspondence: "behavior_class" (twin-like uniform-orientation vs kink-band-like graded-orientation) can be assigned to a band once its OM morphology/contrast signature is known, because that OM signature reliably co-occurs with a particular EBSD orientation-shading signature — this was directly illustrated in the [01-10] specimen (uniform red {10-12} twins vs. variably-colored purple kink bands).

What it leaves open: the magnitude of misorientation is not quantified, so nothing about how much orientation changes across a kink band relative to a twin can be stated. The correspondence is documented only for the [11-20] and [01-10] orientations, so it cannot be generalized to other loading orientations or specimens without further evidence. The link is a co-registered imaging correspondence, not a mechanistic or causal demonstration of what produces the continuous shading. Finally, because OM contrast and EBSD color encode physically different quantities (topographic/diffraction contrast vs. crystallographic orientation), their agreement in separating band types should not be read as evidence that they measure the same underlying physical variable.</message>
