The established result settles only a partial, low-loading claim about the PRF/service_capability relationship. It supports the conclusion that from 0 to 4 wt%, the rise in beta-phase content (76.1-76.3% up to a peak of 81.8-83.2%) tracks the early rise in voltage output (7.5 to 32.0 V), so a beta-phase-mediated contribution to the initial output gain is consistent with the data in that narrow regime.

It does not settle, and explicitly leaves open, anything about the 6-10 wt% range: there beta content is non-monotonic and eases back to ~81% while voltage keeps climbing monotonically to 43.0 and 62.0 V, so beta-phase content cannot be the operative driver of output gain at higher loadings, and the two series are shown to decouple there. The result also does not establish causation at any loading — PZT wt% is a shared driver of both the structural (FTIR) and transport (voltage) measurements by construction, so the low-loading correlation is equally consistent with coincidental co-variation rather than a genuine beta-phase causal pathway. Additionally, because beta content is a normalized percentage and voltage is an absolute quantity in different units, no quantitative mapping (e.g., how much voltage gain per unit beta-phase increase) can be derived. Finally, with only 5-6 discrete wt% points per series, the data support only a qualitative trend-shape comparison, not a fitted functional relationship. In short: a beta-phase contribution to the initial (0-4 wt%) output rise is plausible and consistent with the data, but full-range causality, quantitative attribution, and true mechanistic proof remain unestablished.
