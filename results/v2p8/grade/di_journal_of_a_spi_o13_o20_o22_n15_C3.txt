THE ANSWER
The established result settles that beta-phase enhancement is a plausible contributor to service_capability (voltage/PRF output) only in the low-loading regime, roughly 0-4 wt%: over that range beta content rises from ~76% to a peak of 81.8-83.2% while voltage rises from 7.5 to 32.0 V, so the two trends move together and are consistent with a beta-phase-mediated gain in that window.

It does not settle, and in fact rules out, beta-phase content as the driver across the full 0-10 wt% range. Beyond 4 wt%, beta content is non-monotonic and eases back to ~81%, while voltage continues climbing monotonically to 43.0 and 62.0 V. Since one series turns over and the other keeps rising, beta-phase content cannot account for the voltage gain at 6-10 wt%; some other mechanism must be responsible there.

Several things remain open even within the low-loading regime: (1) because beta fraction (%) and voltage (V) are different kinds of quantities, the magnitude of the voltage rise cannot be derived from or calibrated against the magnitude of the beta-phase change; (2) PZT wt% drives both series by construction, so the co-rise at low loading is consistent with a shared common cause rather than proof that beta-phase enhancement causally produces the voltage gain -- coincidence via the shared driver cannot be excluded; (3) with only 5-6 discrete wt% points per series, there is not enough resolution to fit or confirm any shared functional relationship, only to compare coarse trend shapes. So the result supports a partial, low-loading-only, correlational (not confirmed causal) link, and explicitly leaves the 6-10 wt% mechanism and the causal pathway question unresolved.
