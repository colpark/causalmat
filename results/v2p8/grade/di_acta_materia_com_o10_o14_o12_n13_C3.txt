THE ANSWER
This earlier result justifies treating two band populations as a single coherent structural pairing rather than two unlinked observations: straight, {10-12}/{01-12}-plane-trace bands with symmetric, uniform optical contrast correspond to bands that EBSD shows as a single uniform colour (a discrete, twin-like orientation), while wavy bands with asymmetric OM contrast (strong on the (0001) side surface, faint on (1-100)/(-2110)) correspond to bands whose EBSD colour shades continuously with position (a gradually varying orientation). In the [01-10] specimen this concretely pairs uniformly red {10-12} twins against purple kink bands of varying colour. So the result supports linking an optically defined morphology/contrast class to an EBSD-defined orientation-continuity class for these two band types, and supports calling the wavy/asymmetric-contrast bands "kink bands" (graded orientation) as distinct from twins (uniform orientation).

It leaves open several things. It gives no quantitative measure of misorientation magnitude or gradient across a kink band versus a twin — only qualitative uniform-vs-shading, strong-vs-faint contrasts. It is based on only two specimen orientations ([11-20] and [01-10]), so it cannot be generalized to other loading orientations or specimens. The pairing is a correspondence in imaging signature (co-registration by band type), not a mechanistic or causal proof of what produces the continuous EBSD shading. Finally, since OM contrast and EBSD colour are physically different contrast mechanisms (optical/diffraction contrast versus crystallographic orientation coding), their agreement in distinguishing the two band types does not establish that they measure the same underlying physical variable — it only establishes that they consistently co-vary across this specific band classification.
