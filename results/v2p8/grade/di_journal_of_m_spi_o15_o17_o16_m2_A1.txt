THE ANSWER
The two SEM debris images support a morphological contrast between the two processing routes' wear debris, consistent with (but not proof of) a shift in dominant wear mechanism. Panel a (stir casting) shows coarse, blocky, flat flakes on the order of 20-40 um, some elongated/platy, typical of adhesive/delamination-type wear where subsurface cracking releases larger fragments. Panel b (FSP) shows a much finer, granular texture — mostly sub-5 um particles forming loose agglomerated clusters, with only a couple of larger fragments at the image edge — consistent with a finer-scale abrasive or oxidative wear mode, plausibly linked to the finer, more homogeneous grain structure FSP typically produces.

What this settles: the debris morphology genuinely differs between the two samples under whatever wear test generated them — that much is directly visible and quantifiable from particle size/shape alone (flat flakes vs. fine particles), matching the stated measurement.

What it leaves open: this is a single SEM field per condition at one magnification (X750, 20 um scale bar), so there is no debris-size distribution, no replicate/statistical support, and no chemical analysis (EDS/XPS) to confirm whether the fine debris in b is oxidized (supporting oxidative wear) versus simply mechanically comminuted metal. The images alone do not establish wear rate, coefficient of friction, or which mechanism (delamination, abrasion, adhesion, oxidation) is causally dominant — that requires the accompanying text/data. Nor do they control for confounds such as compositional or reinforcement-distribution differences between the stir-cast and FSP specimens that could independently affect debris morphology. So the panels support "different wear debris morphology between routes" but do not by themselves settle mechanism or performance ranking.
