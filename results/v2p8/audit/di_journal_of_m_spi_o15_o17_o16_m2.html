arm A — the measurement alone · combined 0.00, limits 0.40, 0 contradiction(s)
The two SEM debris images support a morphological contrast between the two processing routes' wear debris, consistent with (but not proof of) a shift in dominant wear mechanism. Panel a (stir casting) shows coarse, blocky, flat flakes on the order of 20-40 um, some elongated/platy, typical of adhesive/delamination-type wear where subsurface cracking releases larger fragments. Panel b (FSP) shows a much finer, granular texture — mostly sub-5 um particles forming loose agglomerated clusters, with only a couple of larger fragments at the image edge — consistent with a finer-scale abrasive or oxidative wear mode, plausibly linked to the finer, more homogeneous grain structure FSP typically produces. What this settles: the debris morphology genuinely differs between the two samples under whatever wear test generated them — that much is directly visible and quantifiable from particle size/shape alone (flat flakes vs. fine particles), matching the stated measurement. What it leaves open: this is a single SEM field per condition at one magnification (X750, 20 um scale bar), so there is no debris-size distribution, no replicate/statistical support, and no chemical analysis (EDS/XPS) to confirm whether the fine debris in b is oxidized (supporting oxidative wear) versus simply mechanically comminuted metal. The images alone do not establish wear rate, coefficient of friction, or which mechanism (delamination, abrasion, adhesion, oxidation) is causally dominant — that requires the accompanying text/data. Nor do they control for confounds such as compositional or reinforcement-distribution differences between the stir-cast and FSP specimens that could independently affect debris morphology. So the panels support "different wear debris morphology between routes" but do not by themselves settle mechanism or performance ranking. arm B — measurement + earlier result · combined 1.00, limits 0.40, 2 contradiction(s)
Both panels are ×750 SEM images at the same scale (20 µm bar). Panel a (stir casting) shows debris dominated by flat, plate-like flakes roughly 20–40 µm across with sharp, chipped edges — consistent with larger-scale material removal. Panel b (FSP) shows a field of much finer particles, mostly well under 5 µm, many clumped into loose agglomerates, with only occasional larger fragments at the margin. Read together with the earlier settled finding — that both surfaces abrade (parallel grooves, no craters) but the FA-containing surface additionally shows shallower grooves covered with fine debris — this debris-size measurement corroborates and sharpens that earlier qualitative impression: it is not just that surface b "looks" covered in finer material, the debris particles themselves are measurably an order of magnitude smaller (<5 µm vs 20–40 µm) than the flakes generated on the comparison surface. This strengthens the case that the FA-bearing (FSP) surface wears by a milder, fine-particulate abrasive mode rather than coarse flake delamination, and the fine, loosely agglomerated particle morphology specifically favors the "fine debris rolling as three-body abrasive" sub-mechanism over the "FA blocking asperity penetration" sub-mechanism, since it is direct evidence of loose rolling particles rather than of an intact protective layer. What remains open: the debris composition/origin (FA fragments vs. matrix wear product) is still not analyzed, so whether the fine debris actually derives from FA is unconfirmed; the causal link between fine debris presence and reduced groove depth is still only a spatial correlation, not demonstrated mechanistically; and both measurements remain single-image, unquantified-count observations, so full-surface representativeness and blocking-mechanism involvement are not excluded.

arm C — the earlier result alone · combined 0.25, limits 0.80, 0 contradiction(s)
The established result supports that both composite surfaces (with and without FA) wear predominantly by abrasion, evidenced by parallel sliding-direction grooves and the absence of large craters on both surfaces. It further supports that adding FA changes the character of that abrasive wear in a favorable direction: the FA-containing surface shows shallower grooves with fine debris and still no large craters, which is consistent with a mechanism-of-change (MEC) in which dispersed FA moderates the severity of abrasive cutting -- either by blocking asperity penetration or by generating fine debris that rolls as a third body -- rather than the composite shifting to adhesive wear. What this leaves open is substantial. First, "shallower" and debris presence are qualitative visual impressions from single SEM images, not quantified groove-depth measurements or debris counts, so the magnitude and statistical reliability of the difference between surfaces is not established. Second, the composition of the fine debris was not analyzed, so it is not confirmed that this debris originates from fractured FA rather than ordinary matrix wear debris -- the causal link between FA and the debris is inferred, not verified. Third, only two localized features were examined, not the full worn surface, so the complete absence of adhesive-wear features elsewhere on the samples cannot be claimed from this evidence alone. Finally, the two candidate sub-mechanisms (barrier effect vs. three-body debris rolling) remain equally consistent with the images and are not distinguished; the pathway is supported only at the level of "abrasion severity is moderated," not at the level of which specific micromechanism does the moderating.
