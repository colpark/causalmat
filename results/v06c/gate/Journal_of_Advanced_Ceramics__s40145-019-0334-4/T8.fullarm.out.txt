Panel F8f shows an indentation crack on polished RZSZ-20 (20 vol% excess ZrSi2). The crack path is tortuous rather than straight: it deflects repeatedly along interfaces, several elongated grains are pulled whole out of the matrix (yellow arrows/labels), the crack bridges across gaps left by these pulled-out grains (orange arrows marking bridging ligaments), and on the right side the crack branches and diffuses into a SiC-rich region (dashed outline, red arrows) rather than propagating as a single line.

Mechanistically, this pattern follows directly from the in-situ reaction route and resulting microstructure described: Reaction (1) leaves ZrB2 as elongated, interlocking platelets (most developed at 20 vol% excess ZrSi2) embedded in a SiC matrix formed at the reaction front. The anisotropic, plate-like ZrB2 grains and the ZrB2/SiC interfaces are weaker/more deflecting paths than trans-granular fracture through the grains themselves, so the advancing crack preferentially follows these interfaces (deflection), locally detaches and dislodges whole ZrB2 platelets (grain pull-out), and leaves unbroken elongated grains spanning the crack wake that must be sheared through (crack bridging), all of which absorb energy. When the crack reaches the SiC-rich region formed at the ZrSi2-derived reaction/liquid-transport zone, the finer, differently oriented SiC grains cause the crack to split into multiple paths (branching) instead of continuing as one plane. Together, deflection + bridging + pull-out + branching are the toughening mechanisms enabled by the interlocked, anisotropic ZrB2 platelet microstructure and the SiC-rich reaction zones produced by the in-situ reactive-hot-pressing synthesis — consistent with the paper's claim that this microstructure improves fracture toughness over conventional ZrB2-SiC composites.
