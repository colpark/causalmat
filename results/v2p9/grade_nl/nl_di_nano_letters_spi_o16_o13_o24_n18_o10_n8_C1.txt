THE ANSWER
This settled result supports attributing Na-ion capacity fading in Co3O4 to poor sodiation reactivity rather than to volume-change-driven structural failure. The reasoning is joint: the retained lattice/porosity finding (A) rules out the competing mechanical-failure explanation by showing the electrode survives the first cycle structurally intact (no metallic Co, no pore collapse), while the computed oxidation-degree ranking (B) shows that even in the absence of such damage, the sodiation reaction itself only reaches ~36% completion and stalls after 0.8 ps -- far short of Li-Co3O4's ~91% at 2 ps. Together these indicate the capacity limitation is intrinsic to the reaction chemistry, not a consequence of the electrode breaking down.

What this leaves open is substantial. B is a short-timescale (2 ps) AIMD proxy for reaction extent, not a direct measurement of capacity fade, so no quantitative link between the oxidation degree and actual capacity loss is established, and it's unproven that the same stalling persists over the full electrochemical cycle (down to 0.11/0.02 V). The material comparison in B conflates cation (Li vs Na) and anion/host-lattice (Co3O4 vs Co3S4) changes, so whether the poor Na reactivity is sodium-specific or lattice-specific is undetermined. Neither A nor B distinguishes surface versus bulk origin of the modest structural change or limited reaction. Finally, the pairing establishes only correlation/consistency between intact structure and limited reaction extent -- it does not demonstrate a shared causal mechanism explaining why the two co-occur.
