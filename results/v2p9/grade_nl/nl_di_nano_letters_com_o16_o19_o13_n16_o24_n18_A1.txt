Panel F6d plots degree of oxidation (of the alkali metal, Li or Na) vs MD simulation time (0–2 ps) for three systems: Li-Co3O4 (black), Na-Co3S4 (red), Na-Co3O4 (blue). At 2 ps: Li-Co3O4 ≈ 91% (highest, still rising steadily/near-linearly), Na-Co3S4 ≈ 59% (rising throughout, second highest), Na-Co3O4 ≈ 36% (rises with the others until ~0.8 ps, then flattens/plateaus for the remainder of the simulation).

What this measurement settles: it gives a direct, quantitative, time-resolved ranking of alkali-metal oxidation extent among the three anode/electrode combinations simulated — Li in Co3O4 oxidizes fastest and most completely, Na in Co3S4 oxidizes at an intermediate, still-increasing rate, and Na in Co3O4 oxidizes least and saturates early. This is solid evidence, within the MD simulation, that (a) Li reacts with Co3O4 more readily/extensively than Na does with either host in this timeframe, and (b) the sulfide host (Co3S4) supports continued Na oxidation past the point where the oxide host (Co3O4) stalls — consistent with a mechanistic claim that sulfides are more permissive to sustained Na-ion reaction/insertion than oxides, at least within the first ~2 ps of this particular MD protocol.

What it leaves open: the panel alone does not establish the underlying atomistic mechanism causing the Na-Co3O4 plateau (e.g., whether it reflects a genuine kinetic barrier/passivation layer forming, a diffusion bottleneck, a finite-size or short-timescale artifact of the MD simulation, or the system approaching an equilibrium composition). It also cannot say whether the ranking would hold at longer times, larger system sizes, different temperatures, or under real electrochemical conditions (vs. this specific MD setup), nor does it by itself identify the causal structural feature responsible for the sulfide vs. oxide difference (that requires structural/DOS/bonding analysis, not just the oxidation-time curve). So the figure supports a comparative rate/extent finding but not a full mechanistic identification.
