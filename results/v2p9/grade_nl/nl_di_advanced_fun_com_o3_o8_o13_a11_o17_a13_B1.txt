The panel (F3h) shows a photothermal heating/cooling curve at λex = 808 nm with the cooling-phase data plotted as linear −ln(θ) vs. time, yielding τs = 133.4 s and a photothermal conversion efficiency η = 19.4%.

Read together with the earlier result (TEM+Raman identifying a Bi-like nanoparticle phase in the reduced material, plus a separate bulk optical measurement showing linear, high-R2 absorption at 808 nm with α = 6.4 L g⁻1 cm⁻1), this new measurement closes the gap the earlier result explicitly left open: it converts "strong, linear NIR absorption" into a demonstrated functional outcome — actual heat generation upon 808 nm irradiation, quantified as a 19.4% photothermal conversion efficiency with a well-defined thermal relaxation time constant. So the combination now supports that the reduced, Bi-phase-bearing material is not just an efficient light absorber but a genuine, quantifiably performing photothermal agent — i.e., it has demonstrated photothermal-conversion value at 808 nm, not merely optical-absorption potential.

What this still leaves open: the photothermal measurement, like the absorption measurement before it, is a bulk/ensemble measurement on the sample as a whole. It does not spatially resolve the heating effect to the TEM-imaged Bi-like nanoparticles specifically, so it still cannot rule out that residual oxide or the unreduced sheet substrate contributes to (or dilutes) the photothermal response. It also does not quantify the fraction of material converted to the Bi phase, nor address photothermal stability over repeated heating/cooling cycles, colloidal/photothermal stability over time, or performance in a realistic (e.g., biological) medium. So the mechanistic attribution of both absorption and photothermal conversion specifically to the Bi nanoparticle phase remains unestablished — only that the same bulk sample, identified as bearing that phase, is now shown to both absorb and convert 808 nm light with quantified efficiency.
