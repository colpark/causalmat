Fig. 14 plots average UTS vs. average YS for AZ61 processed by various routes (ECAP variants, squeeze casting, multidirectional forging, friction stir processing, rolling, heat treatment, extrusion+ECAP, forging+heat treatment) alongside "this study" (red circles). The measurement supports: the three "this study" points (~275/380, ~295/355, ~335/448 MPa YS/UTS) sit clearly above and to the right of every other technology's cluster in the plot — all literature points fall within roughly YS 70–240 MPa and UTS 175–320 MPa, so this study's process achieves a genuinely better combined strength (both higher YS and higher UTS simultaneously) than any comparator route shown, not just a shift along one axis. This justifies the qualitative conclusion that the processing route used in "this study" gives the best strength combination among the technologies compared in this figure.

What it leaves open: the plot gives no ductility/elongation axis, so nothing here supports a strength-ductility trade-off claim — a route could be superior in YS/UTS yet worse in elongation, and this figure cannot rule that out. It also doesn't state sample size, scatter/error bars, or testing conditions behind each "this study" point, so statistical significance of the separation from the next-best cluster (squeeze casting/rolling/multidirectional forging around 200-240 YS, 280-320 UTS) is not established. Finally, comparability is only as good as the literature survey underlying Table 4 (referenced in the caption) — it is unclear whether all reasonably competitive AZ61 processing literature was captured, so "above every literature point" is bounded by the comparators actually plotted, not an exhaustive claim about the field.
