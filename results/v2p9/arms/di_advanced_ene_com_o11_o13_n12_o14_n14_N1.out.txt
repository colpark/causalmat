Likely content: papers with this title typically report a new measurement (e.g., DFT binding-energy calculations, XPS/synchrotron site-specific analysis, or nucleation-overpotential/chronopotentiometry data) showing that certain host sites (Cu, Au, oxide, or heteroatom-doped sites) bind Zn atoms more strongly than the surrounding matrix, i.e., they are "zincophilic" at the atomic/electronic level. Taken alone, this only establishes chemical affinity — it does not by itself prove that this affinity is what controls the deposited zinc's structure, texture (STR), or macroscopic shape.

The "established earlier result" in this space is usually the independently known correlation between zincophilic host sites and preferential, dendrite-suppressing Zn morphology — e.g., that hosts with such sites yield smooth, (002)-textured or platelet-like Zn deposits rather than dendritic/mossy growth, observed via SEM/XRD in prior work. That earlier result, alone, shows a morphological correlation but not the mechanistic cause.

Combined, the new site-level binding/nucleation measurement plus the earlier morphology/texture correlation together support a causal mechanism: zincophilic sites lower the nucleation barrier and pin initial Zn nuclei at defined locations, which in turn dictates the resulting crystallographic texture and dendrite-free shape of the deposit — something neither the atomic affinity data nor the morphology data alone could establish.

What remains open: whether this mechanism generalizes across different host chemistries/current densities, and whether the texture/shape control persists over long-term cycling or scales to full-cell conditions.
