Panel F11a (image reviewed) is a single cumulative-release curve: released drug rises from 0 to ~340 µg by 8 h, ~670 µg by 24 h (already ~84% of the eventual total), then decelerates to ~780 µg at 72 h and plateaus at ~795 µg by 168 h — a classic burst-then-sustained-release profile from a mesoporous carrier, with no Co-content label, no comparison across 0Co/2Co/5Co, and no error bars small enough to argue a dose effect.

What the combination settles that neither settles alone: the earlier EDS/N2 result established only that Co incorporation and reduced N2 uptake (mesopore volume) co-vary in rank order across the three samples, without showing the material still functions as a drug carrier. This release curve, on its own, only shows that some sample of this composition class loads and slowly releases drug over a week. Read together, they support that a mesoporous Co-containing bioactive glass — even one whose porosity is measurably reduced relative to the undoped material — still behaves as a functional sustained-release depot (burst fraction plus a slow diffusion-limited tail to a plateau near the total loaded dose), i.e., the reduced porosity from Co doping has not abolished the mesopore-mediated release mechanism implied by the N2 sorption data.

What is left open: the crop and caption do not identify which Co level (0Co/2Co/5Co) produced this specific curve, so no dose-response link between Co content (or pore volume) and release rate/extent can be drawn — that comparison isn't present in this panel. The mechanism connecting reduced N2 uptake to release kinetics (pore blocking vs. densification vs. diffusion pathway change) remains untested. The antibacterial effect referenced in the caption is not shown in this crop, so no efficacy or Co-dose relationship for antibacterial activity can be inferred either.
