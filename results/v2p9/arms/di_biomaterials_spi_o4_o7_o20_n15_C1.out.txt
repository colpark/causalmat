This measurement settles that, across the three Co-doped bioglass samples (MBG, 2Co, 5Co), increasing Co content is consistently associated with degradation of the mesoporous structure along two independent, converging lines of evidence: the small-angle XRD peak height (a proxy for long-range periodic ordering of the mesopore channels) falls monotonically and steeply (~180000 -> ~74000 -> ~23000, the 5Co sample barely above background), and the N2 uptake at P/P0~1 (a proxy for total pore volume/surface area) also falls monotonically over the same samples (~8.7 -> 5.6 -> 4.35 mmol/g). Because two structurally different measurement types -- one diffraction-based, one adsorption-based -- agree on the same sample ordering, this is reasonably strong support for the qualitative conclusion that higher Co doping progressively degrades the ordered mesoporous architecture of the material, both in terms of its crystallographic-scale periodicity and its overall porosity.

What it leaves open: (1) it does not establish proportionality or a quantitative dose-response curve, since only three Co levels were tested and the two quantities (arbitrary XRD intensity vs. mmol/g adsorption) are not commensurable, so the pair shows correlated decline, not matched magnitude of decline; (2) it does not establish the underlying atomic-scale mechanism -- e.g., that Co2+ substitutes for Ca2+ and disrupts channel self-assembly -- since neither diffraction intensity nor adsorption amount can directly show that substitution event; (3) it does not resolve causal direction or independence, i.e., whether loss of long-range order is what drives loss of pore volume, whether the reverse holds, or whether both are separate downstream consequences of Co incorporation. So the finding supports "Co doping harms mesoporous quality in a consistent, convergent way" but not "how much," "why atomically," or "which effect is upstream."
