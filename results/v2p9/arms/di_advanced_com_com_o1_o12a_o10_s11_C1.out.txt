This measurement pair settles that BST-SH surfaces genuinely carry a new, distinct chemical species — not merely an FTIR artifact — and that the mass loss TGA detects between 180-250 C is attributable to that species rather than to PAEN degradation. The combination is justified because each technique corroborates what the other cannot show alone: FTIR (new 2930, 2864, 1122 cm-1 bands only in BST-SH) identifies CH2-bearing aliphatic groups and a Si-O-type linkage chemically, while TGA (a ~1% weight-loss step present in composites but absent in pure PAEN) confirms this corresponds to a real, quantifiable, thermally decomposable mass fraction. Together they support the conclusion that surface modification of BST with an organosilane (consistent with MPTMS) has actually occurred and is present as bulk, detectable material, not a spurious spectral signal.

What this leaves open is substantial. It does not establish covalent Si-O-BST grafting as opposed to strong physisorption or adsorption of silane species — nothing in either technique distinguishes a covalent linkage from a non-covalent one. The ~1% TGA weight loss is not quantitatively calibrated against the FTIR band intensities, so the result shows co-occurrence of "new species present" and "new mass-loss step present," not a measured amount of grafted material. There is also no information on surface coverage or density — how much of the BST surface is actually modified remains unknown. Finally, pairing these two indirect/semiquantitative techniques, however mutually corroborating, does not substitute for a direct bonding-verification method (e.g., XPS, solid-state NMR, or similar) needed to confirm covalency and quantify grafting.
