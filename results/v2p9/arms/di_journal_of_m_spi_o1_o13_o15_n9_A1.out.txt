Both panels (F4a and F4c) are bright-field TEM micrographs at 0.5 µm scale showing dense, tangled dislocation networks, with an arrowed/labeled region of especially dark, densely packed dislocation contrast running along a linear boundary — captioned "dislocations pileup." In F4c the pileup band clearly follows a curved lamella/interface boundary, consistent with dislocations having been arrested and accumulated against that boundary. F4a shows a similar high-density tangled network but the "pileup" label sits within a more diffuse, less obviously boundary-aligned dark band; the authors' F4a caption frames it more generally as "dislocations configurations in the FCC (L12) phase," while F4c's caption is specifically "dislocation pileup."

What this supports: the images are consistent with a real, localized dislocation pileup at or near a lamella/phase boundary — that is, elevated dislocation density concentrated in a band adjacent to an interface, distinguishable from the surrounding lower-density tangle. This is a standard TEM diffraction-contrast signature (dark linear/curved contrast, higher density) and the authors' own arrow and label in F4c point to that same boundary-adjacent band.

What is left open: TEM contrast alone cannot uniquely confirm this is a true dislocation pileup (a stress-driven queue of discrete dislocations blocked by the boundary) rather than a grain/phase boundary itself imaged in strong diffraction contrast, a boundary precipitate/interface dislocation network, or simply a region of higher foil thickness/bend contour producing denser-looking contrast. No diffraction vector (g) or tilt series is shown, so Burgers vector, dislocation character, and true density/spacing cannot be verified from this crop alone. Whether the pileup is causally linked to any specific defect/strain (STR) mechanism claimed in the paper (e.g., load transfer, work hardening source) is not established by these two images; that would require additional quantitative or in-situ evidence beyond static TEM contrast.
