Panel F11b (antibacterial survival assay, n≈unspecified but with error bars/replicates) shows Scaffolds+Ampicillin knocking relative bacterial survival down over time — ~105% (day1, ns) → ~81% (day3, *) → ~52% (day7, *) — while blank control stays at 100% and drug-free Scaffolds alone stay elevated at 110–118% throughout. So the antibacterial effect requires the loaded drug (scaffold material itself is not bactericidal) and it deepens with time.

Read together with the settled release result (cumulative ampicillin: ~670 µg at 24h/day1, ~780 µg at 72h/day3, ~795 µg at 168h/day7, plateauing), the timepoints align almost exactly (day1=24h, day3=72h, day7=168h). The combination supports a stronger claim than either alone: the ampicillin being released from the ordered mesoporous 2Co-MBG scaffold is still bioactive and its sustained, non-burst delivery produces a progressively strengthening antibacterial effect rather than a one-time kill — i.e., the "gradual, plateauing" release profile previously characterized only as a mass-vs-time curve is now shown to correspond to a real, growing functional (bactericidal) consequence, and the drug-free scaffold control rules out the glass matrix itself as the active agent.

What this leaves open: (1) killing accelerates most between day3 and day7 while cumulative release is nearly flat over that same interval (780→795 µg) — so the correlation is with cumulative exposure duration, not instantaneous release rate, and no rate law is established; (2) only the single 2Co-MBG composition is tested here too, so still no comparison of whether the differently-ordered MBG/2Co/5Co structures differ in antibacterial performance; (3) mechanism (pore-mediated diffusion causing sustained potency) remains inferred, not demonstrated directly.
