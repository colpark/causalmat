This combined result supports the qualitative claim that the material's 808 nm optical absorption underlies its photothermal heating: the linear NIR absorbance-concentration relationship (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1, R2 = 0.9999) and the IR-camera trends—6-minute spot temperature rising with both concentration (200 > 100 > 50 > 25 ug/mL) and laser power density (0.5 to 1.5 W)—move together in the direction absorption-mediated photothermal conversion predicts, and the water-stays-cold control rules out the laser simply heating the solvent. So what is settled is directional and mechanistic: more absorbing material and more incident energy both produce more heat, consistent with (not proof of) absorption driving the photothermal effect.

What is left open is substantial. The two measurements are not commensurable: A/L is a static, no-irradiation, concentration-normalized optical coefficient, while the IR data are a single time-point (6 min) temperature under laser irradiation; pairing them shows qualitative co-variation, not a quantitative or fitted rate law linking absorbed light to heat output. The paper's ~19.4% photothermal conversion efficiency cannot be derived from these two panels alone—it requires a separate energy-balance/calorimetric calculation not present here. Both the concentration series (only four points) and the power series (only a 0.5-1.5 W range at one fixed concentration) are sparse trend comparisons, not calibrated dose-response curves. Finally, while the cold-water control excludes solvent heating as the sole cause, it does not rule out with certainty that scattering, convective effects, or other non-absorptive pathways contribute to the observed heating alongside absorption. In short: mechanistic direction is supported; quantitative conversion efficiency, rate law, and exclusivity of the absorption pathway are not.
