This measurement (TEM + XRD taken jointly) settles that reduction transforms the bare sheets into a two-phase material carrying both a metallic hexagonal-Bi component (confirmed by PDF#05-0519 reflections) and a Bi2O3 component (confirmed by the (321) peak near 33° indexed to PDF#45-1344), with TEM independently confirming that a new nanoparticle morphology emerges upon reduction that is absent on the unreduced sheets. What this justifies, at most, is a bulk/ensemble compositional claim (the sample as a whole contains both phases after reduction) combined with an independent local/morphological claim (particles of some kind appear after reduction). The conjunction is internally consistent with a composite or heterostructure interpretation — i.e., it is plausible and not contradicted by the data — but it does not itself prove that interpretation.

What it leaves open, plainly: there is no spatial linkage between the two observations. Nothing in the data ties the specific nanoparticles seen in TEM to either the Bi or the Bi2O3 signal specifically — no EDS, elemental mapping, or particle-resolved (e.g., SAED) diffraction was performed, so the imaged dots could equally be the metallic phase, the oxide phase, or a mixture within single particles. Because XRD is a bulk/ensemble average and TEM is a local image, their agreement is only consistency between two different evidentiary registers, not a demonstrated correlation. There is also no quantitative cross-check (e.g., TEM-derived particle volume fraction against XRD relative peak intensities) that would exclude alternative spatial arrangements, such as the two phases being segregated into separate regions or particle populations rather than co-located within the same nanoparticles. So the safe conclusion is "reduction yields a bulk two-phase (Bi/Bi2O3) material with new local particle morphology," while any stronger claim about particle-level phase identity or heterostructure architecture remains unestablished by this evidence alone.
