Panel F5a shows resistivity ρ (μΩ·m, log scale) vs T (300–625 K) for x = 0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04. The x=0 (undoped) curve starts near 650 μΩ·m at 300 K and falls monotonically to about 220 μΩ·m by 625 K (negative dρ/dT, semiconductor-like). All doped curves (x=0.005–0.04) sit far lower, clustered around 12–23 μΩ·m at 300 K, and rise gently with T to roughly 25–40 μΩ·m at 625 K (positive dρ/dT, metal-like).

What this settles: the doping level (x) is the dominant lever on the measured resistivity value — doping cuts room-temperature ρ by roughly 30–50x relative to x=0, and it flips the sign of the temperature dependence from decreasing (semiconducting) to increasing (metallic/degenerate) with heating. So a causal/relationship claim of the form "doping (x) lowers resistivity and changes its T-trend from semiconducting to metallic" is directly supported by this single panel, for this compound over 300–625 K.

What it leaves open: the panel gives no information on why (carrier concentration vs. mobility vs. band structure), no data on Seebeck coefficient or thermal conductivity, so nothing here bears on overall thermoelectric performance (power factor, zT) or on any other property that might be paired with resistivity in a PRP claim. It also can't distinguish among the doped compositions (x=0.005–0.04 are nearly degenerate on this plot) or say whether the trend extrapolates below 300 K or above 625 K.
