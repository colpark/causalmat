Panel F5d (viewed) shows: PBS, BNs-BSA-alone, and Lasers-alone groups all progress unchecked; BNs-BSA+660nm is similarly unchecked; BNs-BSA+808nm keeps all five tumors near zero until roughly day 14, after which four of five regrow; BNs-BSA+Lasers (660+808 combined) keeps all five tumors at zero through day ~20 with no regrowth.

Combined with the earlier-established result (that the photothermal conversion capacity documented in solution, Obs A, plausibly underlies the BNs-BSA-vs-PBS heating gap in vivo, Obs B), this new panel supports a more complete but still bounded conclusion: the photothermal heating capability is not merely a solution-phase or single-timepoint in vivo curiosity — it translates into a real, biologically consequential effect, since 808 nm illumination of BNs-BSA initially arrests tumor growth in all five animals, consistent with the heating advantage over PBS. However, the combination also shows that this photothermal capacity, on its own, is insufficient for durable tumor control: most 808 nm-only tumors relapse after two weeks, whereas full, sustained ablation is only achieved when the 660 nm laser is added. So together the two results support "photothermal heating from BNs-BSA is necessary and contributes to early tumor suppression under 808 nm" but not "photothermal heating alone is sufficient for cure" — durable control appears to require the 660 nm component too.

What remains open: whether the added 660 nm effect is complementary heating (higher/sustained peak temperature) or a distinct mechanism (e.g., photodynamic/ROS generation) is not measured in either panel — no temperature data exist for the 660 nm or combined-laser conditions, and no individual-animal linkage between measured temperature and which specific tumors regrew is available. The cause of the one non-regrowing 808 nm-only mouse (dosing, tumor size, uptake variability) is also unresolved.
