This step settles that the pre-existing two-phase lamellar architecture (sharp A/B interfaces, Observation A) is the structural substrate on which the earliest plastic response (the asymmetric contrast at the first strain step, Observation B) is organized. Together they justify the conclusion that strain onset is phase-selective and interface-localized rather than spatially uniform: one lamella type remains featureless while its neighbor develops mottled contrast and interfacial line contrast, meaning deformation begins preferentially in one lamella type and concentrates at the A/B boundary rather than distributing evenly across the microstructure. This is a real and useful mechanistic constraint — it rules out a picture in which both phases yield in lockstep or in which the interface plays no special role at the onset of plasticity.

What it leaves open is substantial. First, imaging contrast alone does not tell you which lamella is which phase, so you cannot yet say whether it is the B2 or the FCC phase that stays featureless versus the one that mottles — that assignment requires additional phase-identifying information, not present here. Second, the result cannot distinguish two competing mechanistic explanations for the asymmetry: an intrinsic difference in slip resistance between the two phases, versus a purely geometric/constraint effect arising from the interface itself. Third, with only two strain states compared (undeformed and first strain step), this establishes an ordering — before versus after — but not a trend; it cannot speak to rate of defect accumulation or whether the asymmetry is a transient early feature. Finally, it says nothing about whether this same phase-selective, interface-localized pattern persists, intensifies, or reverses at higher strains — that requires additional strain steps beyond this one to resolve.
