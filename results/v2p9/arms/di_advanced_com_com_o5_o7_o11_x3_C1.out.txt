This measurement supports a qualitative, identity-level claim: the surface modification visible at the particle scale in SEM (bare BST, ~70 nm, smooth, versus modified particles that are rough, coated, and larger) is the same physical event that underlies the macroscopic color trend from white to dark brown as Ag content increases. In other words, combining SEM and the photograph justifies saying that "coating/roughening" and "darkening" are two observational windows onto one and the same process, and that this process moves in the same direction as rising Ag loading — more Ag, more coating/roughening, more darkening, all together.

What it does not establish is any quantitative or mechanistic detail. Because the SEM comparison is only a two-point contrast (bare vs. "modified"), the data cannot show that darkening scales continuously or proportionally with the degree of surface coating or with particle size as seen in SEM — a graded series across multiple Ag contents would be needed for that. The photograph alone carries no structural information, so it cannot independently confirm what is physically happening at the particle surface; it only supplies the color gradient that the SEM pair is anchored to. Most importantly, because Ag content, particle size, and coating thickness all increase together by construction as Ag loading rises, this result cannot disentangle which of these three co-varying factors is causally responsible for the color change — it only supports that they change together, not which one drives the optical effect. Attributing the color shift specifically to Ag surface coating (rather than size effects or another correlated variable) remains open.
