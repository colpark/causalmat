This combined FTIR/TGA result settles that the two mass-loss events seen by TGA correspond to specific chemical species identified by FTIR: the ~25% gradual loss up to ~350 C is water (assigned via the ~3430 cm-1 O-H band), and the sharp ~60% step near ~360 C is decomposition/collapse of the cyanide-bridged framework (assigned via the ~2100 cm-1 bridging C≡N band and the sub-650 cm-1 M-CN bands), with the ~980 cm-1 band confirming vanadyl (V=O) is present in the structure. This justifies the conclusion that the material is a hydrated, cyanide-bridged (Prussian-blue-analogue-type) framework containing vanadyl units, and that its thermal instability proceeds in two distinct stages: dehydration first, framework breakdown second, at the temperatures given. Neither technique alone could support this joint claim — FTIR has no quantitative or thermal information, TGA has no chemical specificity — so the value of the combined measurement is precisely this cross-referencing of composition to mass-loss behavior.

What it leaves open: it does not establish an exact hydration number or complete stoichiometric formula (e.g., Na or Fe content), since that requires data beyond FTIR/TGA. It cannot distinguish sub-types of water (zeolitic vs. coordinated/ligand water), since both the single broad O-H band and the single gradual mass-loss region are undifferentiated. And critically, the pairing of each TGA step to its FTIR-identified species is inferential, based on overlapping temperature/composition ranges rather than a direct, same-event measurement linking the two techniques — so the assignment, while well-supported, is not proven by a single unified observation.
