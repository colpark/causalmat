Both panels (F5a AZ91/10 SiCp, F5b AZ91/20 SiCp) are SEM fractographs at identical magnification and scale (×150, 100 μm bar). Visually they show the same qualitative fracture morphology: an irregular, rough surface covered with fine bright tear ridges (elongated, fibrous-looking light streaks) that outline broader, comparatively flatter facet-like cells or patches. Neither panel shows classic large equiaxed dimples typical of fully ductile rupture, nor clean cleavage facets typical of fully brittle fracture — the appearance is a mixed/intermediate texture (tear-ridge-decorated quasi-facets) in both the 10% and 20% SiCp composites.

What this supports: the caption/description ("rough fracture surfaces with bright tear ridges around flat facet-like regions") is an accurate qualitative label for both micrographs, and the two SiC loadings (10% vs 20%) produce a similar overall fracture-surface class at this magnification — i.e., no gross change in fracture mode (e.g., ductile-to-brittle) is evident simply from increasing SiC content from 10% to 20%. This is consistent with a "mixed/tear-ridge" behavior_class assignment rather than pure ductile-dimple or pure brittle-cleavage classes for both PRP conditions.

What it leaves open: this is a single-magnification, low-resolution (×150) morphological read with no scale-resolved dimple-size or ridge-density quantification, no identification of SiC particle location, decohesion, or cracking within the ridges/facets, and no fatigue-vs-monotonic or strain-rate context. It cannot establish causal attribution of the fracture mode to SiC content specifically (versus other processing variables), nor quantify any difference in ductility/toughness between the two composites — only that their fracture-surface class appears qualitatively similar.
