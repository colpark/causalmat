This measurement settles that the elevated Co oxidation state in the Fe-containing samples (Co5Fe5 and Co5Fe3Cr2) is a real, technique-independent physical effect rather than an artifact of either spectroscopy. Because XPS (Co3+ fraction of Co 2p) and EELS (Co L3 edge position) are mechanistically independent probes, and both agree in ranking Co5Fe5 and Co5Fe3Cr2 above Co and Co5Cr5, the result rules out the concern that the Co3+ signal is just an XPS peak-fitting or surface-charging bias, or that the EELS L3 blue-shift is a non-valence artifact (e.g., coordination or thickness effect). So the conclusion supported is: there is genuine cross-validated evidence that Fe-containing compositions have a higher Co valence/oxidation state than the Fe-free ones, at least in relative ordering across these four samples.

What it leaves open: (1) No absolute oxidation-state number — no Co2+/Co3+ ratio or formal valence — is established, only a relative ordering among the four samples. (2) Because the two techniques report different physical quantities (a normalized peak-area fraction vs. an edge-shift energy), they cannot be fused into one quantitative correlation; the agreement is only directional/concordant, not a joint calibration. (3) The electronic mechanism behind the effect — e.g., whether charge transfer from Fe to Co is what raises Co valence — is not established; the data show correlation across two probes, not a causal pathway. (4) Since Co5Fe3Cr2 contains both Fe and Cr, this pairing cannot by itself isolate Fe as the sole driver versus a combined Fe-Cr effect; that isolation would require comparing against Co5Cr5 separately, which is a different inferential step than combining XPS and EELS.</message>
