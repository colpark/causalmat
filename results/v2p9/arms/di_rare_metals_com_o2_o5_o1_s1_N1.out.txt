Best-guess reconstruction (no actual data/figures were provided to me, so this is inferential):

The paper almost certainly reports that adding La and Sm to Al-Cu-Mn alloy refines the grain structure and changes the morphology/distribution of the Cu- and Mn-bearing second-phase particles (e.g., coarse, plate-/needle-like Al2Cu or Al-Cu-Mn intermetallics becoming finer, more granular, and more uniformly distributed at grain boundaries and within the matrix), and that this correlates with improved tensile strength, hardness, and/or elongation relative to the RE-free alloy.

Taken alone, this kind of microstructure-mechanical correlation only shows association: better properties accompany finer/better-distributed phases, but it doesn't by itself prove that the refinement is what causes the property gain, nor does it establish a general mechanism.

If read together with an "established earlier result" that rare-earth additions (La/Sm or RE generally) segregate to solid-liquid interfaces or grain boundaries and act as growth restrictors/heterogeneous nucleation sites (a mechanism established in other Al alloy systems, e.g., Al-Si), the combination supports a causal, mechanistic story: RE segregation restricts second-phase growth and redistributes precipitates in Al-Cu-Mn specifically, and this redistribution is the mechanism behind the observed strengthening (via grain-boundary strengthening, reduced stress concentration at fewer coarse particles, or Orowan-type effects).

What it leaves open: the precise partitioning of roles between La and Sm (synergy vs. redundancy), the optimal RE content, thermal/aging stability of the refined structure, whether the mechanism is truly interfacial segregation versus a different route (e.g., new phase formation), and whether findings generalize beyond this specific composition or casting condition.
