This measurement settles that the photothermal conversion capacity demonstrated in solution (Obs A: 6-min spot temperature rising with BNs-BSA concentration from 25→200 µg/mL and with power density 0.5→1.5 W at fixed 200 µg/mL, ~19.4% conversion efficiency) is consistent with, and can be offered as the mechanistic basis for, the therapeutic heating observed in vivo (Obs B: BNs-BSA drives tumour temperature to ~49°C by 5 min versus ~41°C for PBS under 808 nm light). Together, the two panels support the claim that BNs-BSA's service capability as a photothermal agent — a material property that scales with dose and laser power in a controlled cuvette setting — plausibly underlies its ability to generate a substantial, PBS-exceeding temperature rise in tumour tissue. In that sense, the pairing licenses treating the in vivo heating effect as an application of the solution-characterized photothermal property, not as an independent or coincidental finding.

What it leaves open: the in vivo panel is a single present-vs-absent comparison against a vehicle control, not a dose- or power-response series, so the concentration/power dependence shown in solution is not directly verified to hold inside tumour tissue. The two conditions also differ in what they compare (graded doses/power of one material vs. a fixed formulation against PBS), so causal attribution of the in vivo gap purely to photothermal conversion efficiency is not established — perfusion/heat dissipation, tissue optical absorption, and tumour biodistribution/accumulation of the nanomaterial are all uncontrolled and could contribute to the 49°C vs 41°C gap. Finally, the two measurements are read at slightly mismatched timepoints (6 min vs 5 min), so this is a supportive/consistency link across settings rather than a quantitative confirmation that the same conversion efficiency operates in vivo.</message>
