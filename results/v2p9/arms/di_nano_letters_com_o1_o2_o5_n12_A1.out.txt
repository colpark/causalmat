Panels F4f–h (siRNA, DOX, BSA encapsulation, %) each show two clusters of 3 points (green squares = hydrothermal, colored open squares = microfluidics) with orange mean bars and a bracket-and-asterisk comparison. In every panel the microfluidics cluster sits visibly above the hydrothermal cluster with little to no overlap between the two small point clouds, and each comparison is marked with a single asterisk, consistent with the reported means (siRNA ~53 vs ~37%, DOX ~47 vs ~34%, BSA ~59 vs ~43%).

What this justifies: the measurement supports a robust, cargo-general finding that the microfluidic ZIF-8 synthesis route encapsulates all three tested cargos (a small RNA, a small-molecule drug, and a protein) more efficiently than the conventional hydrothermal route, and that this advantage is large in relative terms (roughly 40-70% higher encapsulation) and statistically distinguishable at the reported significance level, replicated across three chemically distinct cargo types — a reasonably strong internal-consistency argument for a real process effect rather than a cargo-specific artifact.

What it leaves open: n=3 per group in each panel is a thin sample, so exact p-values, whatever statistical test was used, and its assumptions aren't visible from the crop alone (only that some threshold, presumably p<0.05, was crossed). The panels show only "encapsulation %," not particle size/yield/polydispersity trade-offs, cargo integrity or bioactivity after loading, reproducibility across independent batches, or whether the mechanism (faster mixing, different ZIF-8 crystal habit, etc.) is understood. Nothing here addresses downstream release kinetics or therapeutic performance — only that more cargo mass ends up encapsulated with the microfluidic method.
