Panel F3a shows, at the first strain increment, two adjacent lamellae with markedly different contrast: one is featureless (no defect contrast, i.e., still essentially undeformed), while its neighbor shows mottled internal contrast plus line contrast concentrated along the shared interface (caption: "dislocation pileup"), indicating dislocations have been generated/multiplied in that lamella and are backing up against the interface.

Read together with the earlier finding, this settles that the two-phase lamellar architecture is not merely a static morphological/compositional pattern (alternating A/B lamellae, confirmed pervasive from TEM to EBSD scale) but also a mechanically functional one: the two lamella types respond asymmetrically to load from the very onset of straining. One lamella accepts and accumulates extended defects (dislocations) first, while the paired neighboring lamella remains defect-free, and the interface between them acts as a barrier that produces pileup contrast. Individually, neither result shows this — the earlier TEM/EBSD comparison is purely morphological/phase-distribution (no strain applied, no defect information), and this single strained image alone, without knowing the lamellae are a confirmed, scale-consistent two-phase system, could not distinguish "asymmetric phase response" from "local, incidental variation."

What remains open: which lamella (A or B, FCC(L12) or B2) is the soft/defect-accepting one is still not identified — this image doesn't resolve phase identity any more than the earlier data did. Also unresolved: the specific defect mechanism (true pileup vs. other dislocation configuration), quantitative strain partitioning between phases, and whether this asymmetric onset behavior is representative beyond this one imaged site/strain step.
