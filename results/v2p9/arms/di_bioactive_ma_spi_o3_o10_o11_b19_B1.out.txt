Fig 10c/d add a second, independent functional readout (antibacterial ratio, light vs dark) on the same Ce0.1/0.2/0.3-at-fixed-Er0.5 series used in Observation B, plus a TiO2 and blank control. Under light the ranking is Er0.5Ce0.2 (~91–93%) ≈ Er0.5Ce0.3 (~87%) > Er0.5Ce0.1 (~68–73%) > TiO2 (~45%) > control (~4–7%); under dark, all samples collapse toward much lower values with the same ordering but far smaller gaps.

Combined with the earlier result, this justifies a somewhat stronger claim: the Er0.5-based co-doped catalyst's advantage over plain TiO2 is not a one-off artifact of the dye-degradation assay — the same composition (Er0.5Ce0.2, closely trailed by Ce0.3) also tops a second, mechanistically distinct property (bacterial killing), and the fact that the advantage is large under light but nearly absent in the dark corroborates that a light-activated process — consistent with the upconversion-driven visible-light harvesting proposed in b7->b17 — is doing the work, rather than some assay-specific chemistry of dye degradation.

What it leaves open is exactly what the earlier qualifications already flagged, unresolved by this new panel: it is still a Ce-varying, Er-fixed series (no Er0.75 antibacterial or degradation data), so Er0.5's superiority over Er0.75 (shown only in PL, Observation A) is still never tested against any functional outcome. And because Ce content, not Er content, is again the varied dimension, the antibacterial peak at Ce0.2 could still reflect Ce co-doping chemistry rather than the Er0.5-enabled upconversion pathway — the Er-vs-Ce confound from Observation B is reproduced, not resolved, by this measurement.
