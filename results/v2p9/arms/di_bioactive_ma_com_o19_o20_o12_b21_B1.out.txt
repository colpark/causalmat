Panels viewed: F10c and F10d (Bioactive_Materials j.bioactmat.2020.02.005) are paired dark/light antibacterial-ratio bar charts (presumably E. coli and S. aureus) across Control, TiO2, Er0.5Ce0.1Ti-O, Er0.5Ce0.2Ti-O, Er0.5Ce0.3Ti-O, both confirming the stated dark-condition trend (Ce0.1 ~28-33% < Ce0.2 ~43% < Ce0.3 ~45-47%, TiO2 ~6%, control ~0), with significance brackets, and an even larger, similarly ordered separation under light.

What the combination settles: The earlier XPS+EDS result establishes that at least one dopant (Er) is present as a specific oxidation-state species, Er3+, bound within a Ti-O oxide framework, not merely inferred from an EDS peak. The new panels show that antibacterial performance rises monotonically with nominal Ce-doping level and exceeds bare TiO2 in both bacterial strains and both dark/light conditions. Combined, these support that the enhanced, dose-dependent antibacterial function is a property of materials in which at least one rare-earth dopant (Er) is chemically confirmed to be incorporated as an oxide-associated Er3+ species, not of chemically uncharacterized "particles with an Er/Ce-energy EDS peak." That is, the functional benefit tracks a composition series whose Er chemistry is independently verified, strengthening the case that doping (not just morphology or bare TiO2 baseline) drives activity.

What remains open: Neither measurement, alone or together, establishes which dopant or oxidation state is mechanistically responsible. Ce's oxidation state is still uncharacterized (no Ce XPS), so the monotonic Ce-content dependence in antibacterial ratio cannot be attributed to a confirmed Ce3+/Ce4+ species — it is only a correlation with nominal Ce loading. Lattice-incorporation versus surface adsorption is also still unresolved, so whether the active species sits at reactive surface sites (plausible for antibacterial/photocatalytic action) or is buried in the lattice remains unknown, as does any role of the unexplained carbon signal.
