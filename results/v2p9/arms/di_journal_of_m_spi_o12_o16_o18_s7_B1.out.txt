Panel F6b (the bar chart) shows this work's RT ZT (~0.82, for the x=0.005-type composition Mg3.2Bi1.4Sb0.595Se0.005) beating four literature references (0.75, 0.70, 0.62, 0.32 for Refs 24, 22, 39, 40), i.e., it establishes an external, cross-study ranking of ZT values at room temperature.

Combined with the earlier internal result — that within this work's own composition series the highest-ZT sample (x=0.005, ZT~0.84) is NOT the highest-PF sample (x=0.01 has the largest PF but only the second-highest ZT, ~0.78) — the two together support a stronger, more specific conclusion: this work's optimum ZT composition is not simply "the one with the best power factor," and that same non-PF-optimal composition is still the one that outperforms all four cited external references in ZT. In other words, the paper's headline achievement (beating Refs 22/24/39/40) rests on the sample that sacrifices some power factor for whatever gives it a ZT edge — reinforcing, from the outside, that ZT and PF really are decoupled in this material system, since the composition being showcased against the literature is precisely the one where PF was not maximized.

What this still leaves open: neither panel reports thermal conductivity, so the physical reason ZT and PF diverge (presumably a lower thermal conductivity at x=0.005) remains unconfirmed. The bar chart also gives no compositional or measurement details for Refs 22/24/39/40 beyond a single ZT number, so it cannot say whether those references show the same PF/ZT decoupling or whether their comparison is at the same temperature, and it cannot establish how this work's ZT-vs-reference ranking or PF-vs-ZT ranking behaves at higher temperatures.
