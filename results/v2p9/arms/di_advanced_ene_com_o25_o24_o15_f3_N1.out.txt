Best-guess reading (no actual figures/data were supplied to me, so this is an inference from the title and known PBA/MOF battery literature):

The paper almost certainly reports that a vanadium hexacyanoferrate (VHCF) Prussian Blue Analogue, structured as a metal-organic framework cathode, delivers high specific capacity together with good rate capability and long cycling stability in an aqueous electrolyte. Taken alone, this new measurement only shows that the material *can* perform well under laboratory cycling conditions — it doesn't by itself distinguish whether that performance is intrinsic durability or merely favorable initial kinetics.

The "established earlier result" for this materials class is the well-known limitation of conventional Prussian Blue Analogue (e.g., iron hexacyanoferrate) cathodes: they suffer from Jahn-Teller distortion, lattice water occupation, and metal dissolution that degrade capacity retention over extended cycling, even though their rate capability (fast Na/K/Zn-ion insertion) is already good.

Read together, the two results settle that the VHCF-MOF architecture resolves the durability weakness that limited the earlier PBA family — i.e., it demonstrates genuine service capability (sustained, reliable performance over many cycles at usable rates), not just a one-off high-rate/high-capacity snapshot. Neither result alone establishes this: the rate/capacity number alone doesn't prove longevity, and the prior degradation finding alone doesn't tell us this new structural variant fixes it.

What remains open: full-cell (not half-cell) performance, behavior at practical mass loadings/current densities, cost and scalability of the MOF synthesis route, and true long-term service life (thousands of cycles, real device conditions) rather than lab-scale cycling windows.
