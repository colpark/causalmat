Panel F5a (resistivity vs T, x = 0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04) shows exactly the pattern described: at 300 K, x=0 sits near ~650 micro-ohm·m while all four doped compositions cluster tightly between roughly 12 and 23 micro-ohm·m — over an order-of-magnitude drop upon doping. Across 300-625 K, the doped curves all rise gently and monotonically with T (metallic-like, positive drho/dT, converging to ~25-40 micro-ohm·m by 625 K), while the undoped x=0 curve falls steadily with T (negative slope, from ~650 down to ~200 micro-ohm·m).

What this settles: doping at even the smallest level tested (x=0.005) collapses resistivity by roughly 30-50x relative to undoped material, and it flips the sign of the temperature coefficient of resistivity — from decreasing-with-T (non-metallic/semiconducting-like behavior) at x=0 to increasing-with-T (metallic behavior) once doped. This is solid, direct support for a doping-induced transition toward metallic conduction and a large reduction in resistivity value at all measured temperatures.

What it leaves open: the four doped curves (0.005-0.04) are nearly indistinguishable in this panel, so no reliable monotonic trend of resistivity with dopant fraction x can be read off — the plot cannot tell you whether more doping helps, hurts, or saturates within that range. The panel gives no Hall/carrier-concentration data, so the mechanism (carrier density vs mobility vs defect/grain-boundary scattering) is not established. There is also no low-temperature (<300 K) data, no error bars, and no microstructural information (density, grain size, secondary phases) that could confound the resistivity values independent of true doping chemistry.
