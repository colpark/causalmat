Panel F3c shows a Beer-Lambert calibration: A/L at 808 nm rises linearly with suspension concentration (R2=0.9999, alpha=6.4 L g-1 cm-1) across ~10-200 microg/mL. Taken alone, this only shows that the post-reduction material has a reproducible, concentration-proportional NIR absorbance at a photothermally relevant wavelength — it says nothing about what phase or structure is doing the absorbing, since optical density curves carry no chemical or morphological identity information.

The earlier TEM+Raman result, taken alone, established that reduction creates a new, discrete, Bi-like nanoparticle phase (not oxide) decorating the sheets, but said nothing about whether that phase (or the resulting material overall) has any useful optical/photothermal property.

Combined, the two settle that the same reduced material shown to contain metallic-Bi-like nanoparticles also exhibits a well-behaved, linear, quantifiable 808 nm absorption cross-section — i.e., the newly formed Bi-nanoparticle-bearing sample is optically characterized and dosable for NIR/photothermal use, supporting its plausibility as a photothermal reagent (PRP) with a defined mass extinction coefficient. Neither measurement alone could link "new Bi-like phase exists" to "this material absorbs 808 nm light linearly/reliably."

What remains open: the absorbance measurement doesn't isolate whether the Bi nanoparticles specifically (versus the sheet substrate or any residual undetected oxide, per the earlier caveat) are the source of the 808 nm absorption; no photothermal conversion efficiency (actual heat/temperature output) is measured, only absorbance; and "concentration" here refers to the whole sample/suspension, not a purified Bi-nanoparticle fraction, so a Bi-specific extinction coefficient cannot be isolated from this alone.
