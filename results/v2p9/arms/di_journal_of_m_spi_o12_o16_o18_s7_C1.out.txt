This result settles that power factor (PF) and ZT do not rank the doped compositions the same way at 300 K: x=0.01 gives the highest PF (~29 uW cm-1 K-2 at 300 K, ~22 at 623 K) while x=0 gives the lowest (at most ~5), but the highest room-temperature ZT (~0.84) belongs to x=0.005, which has the lowest PF among the doped samples, with x=0.01 second at ZT~0.78 and x=0.02-0.04 lower (0.63-0.69). So the composition that maximizes electrical power factor is not the composition that maximizes ZT at 300 K.

What this supports: PF alone is not a sufficient proxy for thermoelectric figure of merit ranking in this system; optimizing for maximum power factor via doping level (x=0.01) would not yield the best ZT sample (x=0.005 does better on ZT despite weaker PF).

What it leaves open: it cannot explain why the ranking diverges, since ZT is a composite quantity (PF and thermal conductivity), and thermal conductivity data is not given in either observation — so whether x=0.005's ZT advantage comes from lower thermal conductivity (or some other factor) is undetermined. It also cannot say anything about how the PF-ZT relationship behaves at 623 K or other higher temperatures, since ZT values are only reported at 300 K while PF is compared across both temperatures. The n-Bi2Te3 reference ZT (~0.88) cannot be tied to a PF value, so no comparison to that reference is possible. Finally, with only a handful of doping levels and purely ordinal (highest/lowest) comparisons, no quantitative PF-to-ZT relationship or trend can be derived — only that a rank mismatch exists at one temperature.
