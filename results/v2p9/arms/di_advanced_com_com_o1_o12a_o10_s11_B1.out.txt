F4c (BST@Ag1%/PAEN SEM, ~1 µm scale) shows particles fully enveloped by matrix material with blurred, indistinct particle-matrix boundaries and no visible voids, cracks, or debonding gaps at the interface — the classic morphological signature of good wetting/adhesion between filler and polymer.

What the combination settles: FTIR+TGA already established that a real, chemically identifiable, thermally quantifiable new species (CH2/Si-O-bearing) sits at the BST surface, distinct from bare PAEN. The SEM now shows that in the actual composite, that surface-modified particle is intimately and continuously wrapped by matrix, without the sharp, gap-lined boundaries typical of poor interfacial adhesion. Together they support that the surface treatment identified chemically is functionally associated with improved interfacial compatibility — the modified BST particles integrate into the PAEN matrix well enough to eliminate the gross morphological hallmark (visible interfacial voids) of weak filler-matrix bonding. This links a chemical-composition claim to a physical/morphological consequence, something neither alone provides: FTIR/TGA never showed what the interface looks like, and SEM alone (without knowing a new species exists) couldn't attribute the good wetting to that species rather than to Ag or particle-size effects.

What it leaves open: SEM cannot distinguish covalent Si-O-BST bonding from strong physisorption — "no gaps" is equally consistent with either, so the earlier covalent-vs-physisorption ambiguity is not resolved. The link between chemistry and morphology remains correlational (same sample, not a controlled comparison isolating the silane species as the cause of the improved wetting versus, e.g., Ag or processing effects). SEM is a local, qualitative image, not a quantified adhesion or coverage metric, so degree/uniformity of interfacial bonding across the whole sample is still unknown, and no mechanical/fracture data confirm the visual impression corresponds to enhanced interfacial strength.
