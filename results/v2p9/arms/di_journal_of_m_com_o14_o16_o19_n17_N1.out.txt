Working from the title alone (no figures or data were supplied), my best-reconstruction is this:

The paper reports that AZ61 processed by ECAP through an unusually open 160° channel (a low-strain-per-pass route) combined with electropulsing treatment (EPT) yields an "extraordinary" simultaneous gain in strength and ductility — i.e., the two mechanical end-points (MEC) move together rather than trading off.

Read alone, this measurement only shows a correlation: this particular processing recipe produces good mechanical numbers. It cannot by itself tell you which physical pathway is responsible — grain refinement from the shear strain, texture weakening/randomization, or electroplastic defect-annealing/precipitate dissolution from the EPT pulses could each independently explain the result, and the paper's single combined-route data point cannot separate them.

Set against the established earlier result that conventional ECAP alone (steeper channel angles, no EPT) on Mg alloys refines grain size but leaves a strong basal texture that caps ductility, the new measurement settles something the old result alone could not: that the extraordinary property gain is not simply "more ECAP passes/strain," since here the route uses a gentler 160° angle. This licenses the inference that EPT (or the milder strain path together with EPT-assisted recovery) is doing real mechanistic work — likely texture modification and/or defect/precipitate-state changes — beyond what strain-based grain refinement provides on its own.

What it leaves open: whether EPT's contribution is dominantly thermal (electroplastic heating/recovery) versus a genuine electron-wind/athermal effect, the relative quantitative weighting of grain size vs. texture vs. precipitate-state contributions, and whether the effect generalizes beyond AZ61/160° to other alloys or angles — none of that is settled by this single combined data point plus the prior ECAP-alone baseline.
