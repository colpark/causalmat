The strain-hardening curve shows a very steep drop from ~85 to ~14 GPa within the first ~0.009 true strain (region I), then a much slower decline to ~5 GPa by 0.058 (region II), before turning negative near 0.06 (region III, marking necking/instability onset). Read alongside the TEM result — that at the first strain step one lamella develops mottled contrast plus interface line contrast (indicative of dislocation activity) while its neighbor stays featureless — the combination supports the inference that the initial burst-and-collapse of hardening rate (region I) corresponds to a phase-selective onset of plasticity: one of the two ordered phases (L12 or B2) begins accommodating strain and rapidly exhausts easy dislocation storage/multiplication, while the co-existing phase remains essentially undeformed at this stage. This gives a plausible microstructural cause for why the macroscopic hardening rate falls so fast early on, rather than declining smoothly as in a homogeneous single-phase alloy — i.e., it ties the unusual two-stage (fast-then-slow) hardening kinetics to heterogeneous, partitioned plasticity between the two ordered phases.

What it leaves open: neither measurement identifies which specific phase (L12 or B2) is the one developing contrast first, so the mechanistic assignment of "which phase hardens/exhausts first" remains unconfirmed. The correlation is also only qualitative and single-instance — one strain step, one lamella pair, no dislocation density quantification — so it cannot establish that this asymmetry is the dominant or sole cause of the GPa-scale rate drop, nor explain the subsequent slow-decay plateau (region II) or the negative-rate instability (region III), which could involve additional or different mechanisms (e.g., second-phase engagement, interface sliding, void nucleation).
