Best-guess reconstruction (no actual figures/data were available to me):

The likely "established earlier result" is a computational/thermodynamic one — DFT-calculated binding (adsorption) energies showing that certain heteroatoms, functional groups, or defect sites on a host material bind Zn/Zn2+ more strongly than the bare substrate, i.e., they are "zincophilic" in principle. On its own, that result only shows a correlation between site chemistry and calculated affinity; it cannot show that this affinity actually controls where and how zinc nucleates and plates in a real cell, nor rule out that observed dendrite suppression or improved Coulombic efficiency comes from unrelated factors (surface roughness, conductivity, electrolyte effects).

This new paper's measurement is presumably a direct experimental/mechanistic probe — e.g., operando or ex-situ microscopy (SEM/AFM/TEM) mapping nucleation events, spatially resolved spectroscopy (XPS/EDS), or overpotential/nucleation-energy measurements — showing that Zn deposits preferentially and with lower overpotential specifically at the sites predicted to be zincophilic.

Read together, the combination moves the claim from "these sites have higher binding energy" (a static, computed property) to "these sites causally direct where nucleation occurs and lower the kinetic barrier for it" — i.e., it identifies the mechanism (MEC) linking site chemistry to deposition behavior, something neither the DFT calculation nor the deposition measurement alone establishes.

What remains open: the precise electronic-structure origin of the affinity, generalizability across different host materials, electrolytes, and current densities, and long-term/cycling stability of the effect — the identification is mechanistic but not yet shown to be universal or durable.
