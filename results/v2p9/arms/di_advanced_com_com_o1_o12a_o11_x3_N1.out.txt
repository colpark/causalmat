The new measurement is presumably the dielectric constant/loss, breakdown strength, and mechanical strength (flexural/tensile) reported for the BST@Ag core-shell filler in poly(arylene ether nitrile) (PAEN/PEN), compared at various filler loadings, alongside thermal stability data. The "established earlier result" is the classical percolation-theory prediction: near the percolation threshold, conductive (Ag) fillers in a polymer matrix produce a sharp, large rise in effective permittivity, but this is normally accompanied by rising dielectric loss, declining breakdown strength, and degraded mechanical integrity because conductive particles touch and form leakage/conduction paths.

Neither result alone settles whether high permittivity and mechanical/thermal robustness can coexist: the percolation theory alone only predicts the permittivity jump and its usual loss/strength penalty; the new composite measurement alone only shows this particular material's numbers without confirming the mechanism. Read together, they support the conclusion that insulating the Ag nanoparticles inside a BST shell decouples the dielectric enhancement from the percolation-driven conductive network — the BST shell suppresses direct Ag-Ag contact, so the composite gets an elevated dielectric constant with comparatively low loss, higher breakdown strength, and retained (or improved) mechanical strength and thermal resistance versus a bare Ag-filled system at the same loading.

What this leaves open: whether the effect holds at higher filler fractions approaching true percolation, long-term/cyclic reliability under thermal and electrical stress, generalizability to other core-shell filler chemistries, and whether energy-storage/capacitor-grade performance (not just lab-scale dielectric measurement) is achieved.
