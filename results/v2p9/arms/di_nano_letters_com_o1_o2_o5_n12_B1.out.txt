Panels F4f–h (siRNA, DOX, BSA) each show microfluidic particles with significantly higher encapsulation than hydrothermal, matching the reported numbers (~53 vs ~37%, ~47 vs ~34%, ~59 vs ~43%), with a star over each pairwise comparison and the same green (hydrothermal) vs magenta/blue (microfluidics) color coding used in the size panels.

Read together with the settled DLS/SEM result, this justifies a process–structure–performance conclusion: the microfluidic route, which reproducibly yields smaller, more size-uniform particles (~300-400 nm, narrow spread) compared to hydrothermal's larger, broader particles (~1000 nm), also reproducibly yields higher cargo loading — and this performance advantage holds across three chemically distinct cargo classes (a small-molecule drug, a protein, and a nucleic acid). Because the encapsulation advantage is consistent across cargo type, it looks like a general property of the synthesis route/particle architecture rather than something specific to one cargo's chemistry, and because it co-occurs with the independently corroborated size/distribution difference, the two synthesis routes can be said to differ in both structure and function in a consistent, non-coincidental direction.

What the combination does not settle: it does not establish that smaller size or narrower distribution is the cause of higher encapsulation — the two routes could also differ in porosity, crystallinity, defect density, or surface chemistry, any of which could independently drive loading differences, and no panel here isolates size as the mechanistic variable (no within-route size series or dose-response is shown). It also does not quantify the encapsulation-vs-size relationship, only that both differ concordantly by route. So this is a structure/performance correlation across routes and cargos, not a demonstrated causal link.
