Best-guess answer (no specific figure/measurement was supplied, so this is inferred from the paper's known subject matter — Co-doped mesoporous bioactive glass scaffolds as hypoxia mimetics for bone tissue engineering):

The established earlier result is the classic mechanism of Co2+ (or CoCl2) as a hypoxia mimetic: cobalt ions inhibit prolyl hydroxylase (PHD) enzymes, preventing hydroxylation and subsequent VHL-mediated ubiquitin-proteasomal degradation of HIF-1α, so HIF-1α protein accumulates and drives transcription of angiogenic/osteogenic targets (VEGF, etc.) — a mechanism previously shown mainly in simple cell-culture systems with soluble CoCl2, without any material-based, dose-controlled delivery vehicle.

This paper's own measurement is that a mesoporous bioactive glass scaffold can be engineered to release Co2+ in a controllable, sustained, dose-dependent manner, and that cells (osteoblasts/BMSCs/endothelial cells) cultured with scaffold extracts show corresponding dose-dependent increases in HIF-1α stabilization and downstream angiogenic/osteogenic markers.

Read together, the combination supports that the canonical PHD-inhibition/HIF-1α stabilization pathway is not merely a soluble-ion cell-culture artifact but can be reliably triggered by a solid, clinically relevant biomaterial platform, and that the magnitude of pathway activation can be tuned by controlling scaffold ion-release kinetics — something neither the original CoCl2 mechanism studies nor the scaffold-fabrication work alone could establish.

What remains open: causal necessity (no direct PHD/HIF-1α knockdown or chelation control is implied to isolate cobalt's role from other released ions like Si or Ca), in vivo relevance and long-term safety/toxicity of sustained cobalt exposure, and whether the scaffold's angiogenic/osteogenic benefit fully depends on HIF-1α or involves parallel, independent pathways.</message>
