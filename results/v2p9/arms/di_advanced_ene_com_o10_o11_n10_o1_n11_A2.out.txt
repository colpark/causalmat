Panel 1a is a DFT-computed free-energy diagram plotting ΔG(Co2+→Co3+) on x against ΔG(Co3+→Co4+) on y for four Co (oxy)hydroxide compositions, with CoO2/CoOOH/Co(OH)2 shown as a reference dehydrogenation series above. Reading the points confirms the stated measurement: CoCrOOH sits lowest on x (~1.05 eV, easiest Co2+→Co3+ step); CoFeOOH sits lowest on y (~6.2 eV, easiest Co3+→Co4+ step); CoFeCrOOH is intermediate on both axes (~1.28, ~6.4); Co(OH)2 is highest on y (~6.68 eV) and tied with CoFeOOH as highest on x.

What this settles: it supports a mechanistic (MEC) picture in which Cr and Fe dopants each preferentially stabilize/facilitate a different oxidation step of the Co redox cascade — Cr lowering the barrier for the first (Co2+→Co3+) electron-proton transfer, Fe lowering the barrier for the second (Co3+→Co4+) step — and that combining both dopants (CoFeCrOOH) yields a compromise rather than the best of both. This is consistent with a proposed synergistic-doping pathway for the OER Co redox sequence.

What it leaves open: this is a computed thermodynamic (ΔG) comparison of two isolated redox steps, not a full reaction-coordinate/rate analysis — it doesn't identify the true rate-limiting step, doesn't include the O-O bond-forming step needed for a complete OER mechanism, and has no experimental (e.g., operando spectroscopic or kinetic) validation that these computed active-site structures/energetics correspond to what forms during real catalysis. It also doesn't explain why an intermediate ternary composition would be optimal overall if that is claimed elsewhere in the paper.
