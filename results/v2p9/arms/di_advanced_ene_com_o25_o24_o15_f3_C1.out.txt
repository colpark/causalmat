This measurement supports associating vanadium (V-site) dissolution with the buildup of interfacial charge-transfer resistance (Rct) captured by PRF/service_capability, because the two independently measured series—Rct growth by EIS and dissolved-V growth by ICP-OES—trace the same temporal shape: both rise during early cycling and both saturate by roughly cycle 150 (Rct plateauing near 24-28 ohm, V near 3.7 mg/L). It further supports ruling out Fe dissolution and film/electrolyte resistance (Rfilm/Re) as the dominant driver, since Fe dissolution stays low (~1.1 mg/L) and Rfilm/Re remain flat while Rct climbs, making V a more plausible leading contributor among the species measured.

What it leaves open is substantial. First, this is a temporal correlation between two curves, not a mechanistic demonstration that dissolved V is the cause of rising Rct rather than some other cycling-linked degradation process that happens to saturate on a similar schedule. Second, the match is qualitative (same rise-then-plateau shape) rather than quantitative — there is no dose-response relationship established between mg/L of V dissolved and ohms of Rct gained, since these are different kinds of quantities (a cumulative electrolyte concentration versus an interfacial impedance state). Third, the precision of the "both saturate near cycle 150" claim is weak because Rct at the plateau carries large error bars. Finally, ruling out Fe as a major contributor is only an argument by elimination among the species tested; it does not by itself prove V is causally sufficient to produce the observed Rct rise, since other unmeasured processes could still be involved. In short: the data justify treating V dissolution as the leading candidate mechanism worth pursuing, not a confirmed cause.
