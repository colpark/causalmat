I opened panel F5c (power factor vs. temperature, x=0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04). The plot confirms the stated numbers: red circles (x=0.01) sit highest across the whole range, ~29 µW cm⁻¹ K⁻² near 300 K declining to ~22 at 623 K; black squares (x=0.005) are the lowest of the doped set, ~23 falling to ~13; green triangles (x=0) are far below everything, rising slowly from ~2 to ~5 with increasing T; x=0.02 and x=0.04 (blue triangles, magenta inverted triangles) track close together between the x=0.01 and x=0.005 curves, roughly 25–27 near room temperature falling to ~19–20 at 623 K.

What this settles: the doping level has a clear, non-monotonic effect on power factor — x=0.01 is the best-performing composition of the five tested across the entire 300–623 K window, and doping of any amount (even x=0.005) is vastly better than the undoped x=0 sample, which stays an order of magnitude lower throughout. It also establishes that for all doped compositions PF peaks near room temperature and falls with rising T, whereas the undoped sample shows the opposite trend (slowly rising), implying a different transport regime for the undoped material.

What it leaves open: this single PF curve cannot tell you why x=0.01 is optimal (no carrier concentration, mobility, or Seebeck/resistivity breakdown shown here), whether finer doping steps between 0.005 and 0.02 could beat x=0.01, or how this translates into thermoelectric figure of merit ZT (thermal conductivity not shown). No error bars or repeat-sample data are visible, so reproducibility/uncertainty of the ranking, especially the close overlap of x=0.02 and x=0.04, cannot be assessed from this figure alone.
