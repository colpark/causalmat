This measurement supports the PRF/service_capability claim that the material's 808 nm optical absorption underlies its photothermal heating function: the concentration-dependent NIR extinction (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1, R2 = 0.9999) and the IR-camera evidence that spot temperature at 6 min rises with both concentration (200 > 100 > 50 > 25 ug/mL) and laser power (0.5 to 1.5 W), while water alone stays cold, together justify the qualitative service claim that the material converts absorbed 808 nm light into heat in a concentration- and power-dependent manner, and that this heating is attributable to the material's absorption rather than to the laser simply warming the solvent.

What it leaves open: the two data types are not quantitatively commensurable — A/L is a static, no-irradiation, concentration-normalized optical slope, whereas the IR data are single time-point temperatures under irradiation — so the result establishes directional/qualitative consistency, not a fitted absorption-to-heat conversion law. It does not support or derive the paper's ~19.4% photothermal conversion efficiency figure, which needs a separate energy-balance/calorimetric calculation absent from these panels. The concentration and power series are each sparse (4 points; 0.5-1.5 W at one concentration), so only ordering/trend claims are justified, not rate constants or dose-response curves. Finally, while the water control rules out solvent-only heating, it does not rule out non-absorptive contributions (scattering, convection) to the observed temperature rise, so 'absorption underlies heating' should be read as the best-supported qualitative interpretation rather than a quantitatively isolated mechanism.
