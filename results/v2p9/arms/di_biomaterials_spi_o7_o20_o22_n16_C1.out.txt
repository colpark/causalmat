This measurement supports a qualitative, mechanistically plausible link for PRF/service_capability: it is consistent with the idea that Co-driven loss of mesoporosity (monotonically falling N2 uptake at P/P0~1 across 0Co→2Co→5Co: ~8.7→5.6→4.35 mmol/g) could be the structural driver behind a diffusion-limited release profile — burst uptake followed by a plateau — seen for the 2Co material (~340 ug at 8h rising to ~795 ug by 168h, leveling after ~72h). So the service capability claim it justifies is narrow: "reduced pore volume is compatible with, and could explain, slowed/plateaued diffusive release" — a consistency/plausibility result, not a demonstrated causal or dose-response relationship.

What it leaves open is substantial. First, because release kinetics were only measured for 2Co, there is no evidence that 0Co or 5Co would show faster or slower release, or different plateau timing, as mesoporosity changes — so the core comparative claim of the capability (that pore structure differences translate into different release kinetics) is untested. Second, the two observations are mismatched in kind: a single-point equilibrium adsorption comparison across compositions versus a time-resolved cumulative release curve for one composition, so no matched dose-response or correlation can be computed. Third, the plateau in the release curve is equally explainable by simple exhaustion of the finite loaded drug payload rather than by pore-network diffusion limits, and the data cannot distinguish these two mechanisms. In short: plausible, not established; the capability's predictive/comparative claim across compositions remains unverified pending matched multi-composition release data.
