The "established earlier result" this would be read against is presumably prior work showing that true hypoxia (or genetic/pharmacologic HIF stabilization) drives pro-angiogenic and pro-osteogenic signaling in bone repair, but without a deliverable, implantable, dose-tunable material platform.

Read together, the combination would support the conclusion that a solid-state, controllable-release biomaterial can reproduce the biologically established hypoxia-mimicking signaling effect (HIF stabilization → angiogenesis-osteogenesis coupling) in a form usable for bone tissue engineering — i.e., it converts a known pathway-level finding into a practical scaffold platform with tunable ion dose. Neither result alone settles this: the earlier hypoxia work alone doesn't show a material can deliver the effect locally and controllably; the cobalt-release/marker data alone don't establish that the pathway is causally hypoxia-mimetic rather than a generic ion effect.

What is left open: in vivo efficacy and safety (cobalt cytotoxicity/systemic exposure at the doses used), long-term release kinetics and degradation, whether effects generalize beyond the specific cell/animal model, and how this compares in efficacy or "value" to other angiogenic strategies (e.g., growth-factor or PRP-based delivery) — no head-to-head comparison is implied by this reconstruction.
