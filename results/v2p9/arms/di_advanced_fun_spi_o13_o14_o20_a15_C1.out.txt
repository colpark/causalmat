This measurement settles that the material's absorption at 808 nm is qualitatively consistent with, and plausibly underlies, its photothermal heating behavior. The linear NIR absorbance-concentration relationship (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1, R2 = 0.9999) combined with the IR-camera data — where the 6-minute spot temperature rises with both increasing concentration (200 > 100 > 50 > 25 ug/mL) and increasing laser power (0.5 to 1.5 W), while water alone stays cold — supports the conclusion that more absorbing material and more incident light energy jointly produce more heat, and that the laser is not simply heating the solvent. This is consistent with an absorption-mediated photothermal conversion mechanism (MEC/pathway).

What it does not establish: the two measurements are not directly commensurable — A/L is a static, no-laser optical property, while the IR data are a single time-point (6 min) temperature under irradiation — so the pairing only shows the two track concentration and power in the same qualitative direction, not a quantitative or fitted rate law linking absorbed light to heat. It cannot derive or validate the paper's reported ~19.4% photothermal conversion efficiency, which would require a separate energy-balance/calorimetric analysis not present in this data. The concentration series (4 sparse points) and power series (a separate two-point comparison at one fixed concentration) each show an ordering/trend but not a mechanistic dose-response curve. Finally, while the cold-water control rules out simple solvent heating by the laser, it does not rule out with certainty that some of the observed heating arises from scattering or convective effects rather than true absorptive photothermal conversion. In short: qualitative support for an absorption-driven photothermal pathway, but no quantitative or mechanistically exclusive proof.
