This cross-validation justifies the conclusion that the Fe-containing samples (Co5Fe5 and Co5Fe3Cr2) genuinely exhibit a higher Co oxidation state than Co and Co5Cr5, and that this is a real chemical/electronic feature of the material rather than an artifact of either spectroscopic method. Because two physically independent probes -- XPS's Co3+ peak-area fraction and EELS's Co L3 edge-position shift -- were measured on the same four samples and agree in direction (both elevated for the Fe-containing pair, both showing Co and Co5Cr5 as the lower baseline), the result rules out the concern that the apparent oxidation-state trend is a method-specific bias, such as XPS charging/peak-fitting error or an EELS edge shift driven by coordination geometry or sample-thickness effects rather than valence. So what it settles is: the relative ordering of Co oxidation state across these four samples is robust to measurement technique.

What it leaves open: (1) no absolute oxidation-state value -- no Co2+/Co3+ ratio or formal valence number, only a relative ranking among the four samples; (2) no quantitative correlation between the two measurements, since a peak-area fraction and an eV edge shift are incommensurate units and can only be compared as concordant or discordant, not fused into one number; (3) no mechanistic explanation -- the data do not establish that Fe incorporation raises Co valence via charge transfer or any other specific electronic pathway, only that two techniques agree something is elevated; (4) no isolation of Fe as the sole driver versus a combined Fe-Cr effect, since Co5Fe3Cr2 contains both elements -- any such discrimination would have to come from the Co5Cr5 comparison, not from the XPS/EELS agreement itself.</message>
