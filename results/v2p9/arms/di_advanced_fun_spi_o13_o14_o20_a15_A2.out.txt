Panel F5d shows six mini-panels of individual tumor-volume curves (5 mice per group), confirming the stated measurement:

- BNs-BSA+808 nm panel: individual curves are flat near 0 for most of the 20+ day window, but 4 of the 5 lines clearly kink upward starting around day ~14–15 and rise to ~250–700 mm3 by day 20+ — i.e., regrowth in most animals.
- BNs-BSA+Lasers (dual 660/808 nm) panel: all 5 curves stay flat at ~0 mm3 for the entire observation window, no regrowth in any animal.
- By contrast, PBS, BNs-BSA alone, Lasers alone, and BNs-BSA+660 nm all show uncontrolled, steadily rising growth curves across all 5 mice, confirming those are ineffective controls.

What this settles: the data directly support that single-wavelength 808 nm photothermal treatment with BNs-BSA produces only transient tumor suppression — durable, curative ablation requires the dual 660/808 nm combination, since only that group shows complete, sustained regression in every animal with zero relapse. This is solid support for a mechanistic claim that both wavelengths (i.e., both a photodynamic/660 nm-driven process and an 808 nm photothermal process) are needed together for durable tumor eradication — single-modality 808 nm heating alone is insufficient to prevent recurrence.

What it leaves open: the curves alone do not reveal *why* 808 nm-only treatment fails while the dual treatment succeeds — no mechanistic readout (temperature profile, singlet oxygen/ROS levels, apoptosis markers, immune infiltration) is shown in this panel, so whether the added benefit of 660 nm is via a genuine photodynamic (ROS/pathway) mechanism versus simply an additive/synergistic photothermal boost cannot be determined from growth curves alone. Sample size is small (n=5/group), and no statistical test or IC50/dose-response data is shown here to quantify the difference. So while the phenotypic outcome (efficacy) is well supported, the specific molecular pathway/MEC responsible for the difference remains unaddressed by this figure and would need complementary panels (e.g., ROS assays, temperature curves, or histology) to settle.
