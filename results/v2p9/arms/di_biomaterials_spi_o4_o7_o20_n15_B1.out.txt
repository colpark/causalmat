The panel (F11a) shows a single cumulative drug-release curve, not a comparison across Co levels: a burst phase to ~340 µg at 8 h and ~670 µg (about 84% of total) by 24 h, followed by slow release to a plateau of ~795 µg by 168 h — a classic biphasic burst-then-sustained profile from a mesoporous carrier.

Read together with the earlier result — that increasing Co content progressively degrades both long-range pore ordering (XRD) and total porosity (N2 uptake) across MBG, 2Co, 5Co — the combination supports one thing the release curve alone couldn't: it shows that whichever Co-containing composition was used here still retains functional, sustained (multi-day) drug-delivery capacity despite carrying the Co substitution known to reduce mesoporous order/volume. Given the caption links this panel to "its anti-bacteria effect," the pairing is consistent with a dual-function material — Co doping for antibacterial ion release, combined with a drug carrier that still loads and releases cargo over a week — i.e., the structural degradation from Co doping is not so severe as to abolish drug-delivery function in this sample.

What it leaves open: this panel does not identify which specific sample (MBG, 2Co, or 5Co) produced this curve, nor does it show parallel release curves at multiple Co levels. So the combination cannot establish a dose-response between Co-induced porosity/order loss and loading capacity or release kinetics, cannot quantify any tradeoff between antibacterial Co content and drug-delivery performance, and cannot causally link the burst-vs-plateau release phases to the specific pore-structure changes documented earlier.
