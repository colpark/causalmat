The paper reports that mesoporous bioactive glass (MBG) scaffolds doped with cobalt show a controllable, sustained (dose/composition-dependent) release of Co2+ ions in vitro, and that this release correlates with stabilization of HIF-1α signaling and upregulation of angiogenic/osteogenic markers (e.g., VEGF, ALP, osteogenic genes) in cultured cells, translating into enhanced vascularized bone regeneration in vivo.

Read alone, a release-kinetics/dose measurement only shows that the scaffold can deliver cobalt in a tunable way — it doesn't by itself prove biological relevance. The "established earlier result" that CoCl2 (soluble cobalt) chemically mimics hypoxia by inhibiting prolyl hydroxylases and stabilizing HIF-1α (driving VEGF/angiogenic-osteogenic coupling) also doesn't, alone, prove that a solid-state, engineered scaffold can reproduce that effect at safe, sustained, controllable doses.

Combined, the two settle that this specific biomaterial platform can functionally substitute for pharmacological hypoxia-mimetics: the scaffold's measured release profile is shown to fall in the range known to trigger the HIF-1α pathway, giving a plausible causal bridge from material design to biological outcome (angiogenesis-coupled osteogenesis) without systemic drug delivery.

What remains open: whether the in vitro dose-response generalizes to long-term in vivo cobalt accumulation and systemic toxicity, whether the vascularization/bone outcome is durable rather than transient, the exact mechanistic contribution of HIF-1α versus other cobalt effects, and translatability to human bone defects, none of which a release/expression measurement plus the CoCl2 precedent alone can establish.
