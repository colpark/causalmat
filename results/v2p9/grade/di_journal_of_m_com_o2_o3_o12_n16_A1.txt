THE ANSWER
Figure 7a plots minimum creep rate vs. applied stress (log-log) at 175°C for AZ91 and AZ91+SiC composites at 5/10/15/20/25 wt%. The data support a clear, monotonic trend at higher SiC loadings: the 15, 20, and 25 wt% curves sit below the unreinforced AZ91 line across the stress range shown (~80–120 MPa), meaning these composites creep more slowly than the base alloy at a given stress — i.e., particle reinforcement at these levels genuinely improves creep resistance, it is not just a stiffness or elastic-modulus artifact, since minimum creep rate is a steady-state plastic deformation measure. The stress exponents n also rise with SiC content (7.2 for AZ91 up to 8.9 for 25 wt%), consistent with a strengthening mechanism (e.g., threshold/back-stress from particles) rather than pure dilution.

What is left open: the 5 and 10 wt% curves overlap with or run slightly above AZ91, so at low reinforcement levels there is no reliable creep benefit and possibly a small penalty — this measurement does not explain why the benefit is nonmonotonic (particle clustering, porosity, or a real threshold effect could all produce this, and the panel/caption alone cannot distinguish these mechanisms). The panel also only covers 175°C and one stress window (~80–125 MPa); it says nothing about behavior at other temperatures, at higher/lower stresses, or about long-term life, ductility, or fracture. So the data justify "high SiC content (≥15 wt%) lowers minimum creep rate relative to AZ91 at 175°C" but do not justify a general "PRP/value" claim that SiC reinforcement uniformly improves creep resistance — the low-loading result and the missing mechanistic/other-temperature data leave that broader claim unsettled.
