This measurement supports a causal link between recombination behavior (PL 390-420 nm intensity) and photocatalytic service capability (degradation performance) at the two points where both were actually measured. TiO2, the sample with the highest PL intensity (interpreted as the most electron-hole recombination), shows the lowest degradation (~20% at 60 min) — consistent with recombination suppressing service capability. Er0.5Ce0.2, the sample with the lowest PL intensity (least recombination), shows the highest degradation (~46% at 60 min), with the same rank ordering holding at 20 and 40 min as well. So for these two samples, less recombination tracks with more photocatalytic activity, and this direction matches the paper's causal claim that suppressing recombination improves service capability. This justifies saying the claim is not falsified and is directionally corroborated at both ends of the tested range, with the relationship holding consistently across three time points (20, 40, 60 min), not just as a single snapshot.

What it leaves open: the two panels only share TiO2 and Er0.5Ce0.2 as common samples — Er0.5Ti-O (PL only) and Ce0.1/Ce0.3 (degradation only) cannot be cross-checked, so the full co-doping series' monotonic dose-response cannot be confirmed, only the two endpoints. Also, PL intensity is a proxy/arbitrary-unit spectroscopic signal while degradation is a cumulative percentage removed, so this establishes a consistent qualitative endpoint correlation, not a quantitative or mechanistic dose-response link between recombination magnitude and degradation rate. Intermediate compositions' behavior, and whether the relationship is linear, threshold-based, or saturating, remain unsettled.
