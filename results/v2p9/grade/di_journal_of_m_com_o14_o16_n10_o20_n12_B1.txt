The kinetic data show that adding 10 wt% C@Co (C10) cuts the diffusion-controlled second absorption stage at 360°C from 66 min (C0) to 11 min — roughly a sixfold acceleration of the rate-limiting hydrogenation step. Read together with the earlier EDS/HRTEM result (C and Co concentrated at particle surfaces/edges, with HRTEM at one such edge confirming crystalline CeMg12/Y5Mg24 alloy fringes abutting an amorphous band and curved carbon layers), the combination supports a coherent structure-to-kinetics story: a carbon(+Co)-bearing surface layer sits directly at the alloy particle edge, i.e. exactly where H atoms must cross to enter/leave the bulk during the diffusion-limited stage, and this coating's presence correlates with the large speed-up of that stage. This justifies treating the surface carbon/Co phase as a plausible physical site for whatever process shortens diffusion-controlled kinetics (e.g., acting as a catalytic or easier-diffusion pathway at the surface).

What it does not settle: the kinetic measurement is a bulk/ensemble average over many particles with no spatial resolution, so it cannot itself show which particles, edges, or phases are responsible; the microstructural result covers only one HRTEM field of view not shown to be representative, so we don't know the coated-edge structure is common enough to explain a bulk sixfold rate change. Neither result identifies the actual mechanism — Co catalytic dissociation, carbon as a fast diffusion conduit, versus a confounding effect of milling (finer particle size, more defects) that accompanies the C10 additive but isn't itself imaged or measured kinetically. Also, since no Co lattice fringe was resolved, atomic-scale Co dispersion within the carbon, and hence a specific catalytic pathway, remains unconfirmed — the combination gives correlation between surface coating and faster kinetics, not a demonstrated causal mechanism.
