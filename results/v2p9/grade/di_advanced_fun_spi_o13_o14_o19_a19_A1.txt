THE ANSWER
Figure 5c plots tumor surface temperature vs. irradiation time (0–5 min) for two groups: PBS control and BNs-BSA, under 808 nm laser at 7 W/cm2. The PBS curve rises modestly, from ~36-37 C at t=0 to ~41 C by 5 min. The BNs-BSA curve rises much faster and higher, from ~36-37 C at t=0 to a plateau/slow climb reaching ~48-49 C by 5 min, with most of the increase occurring in the first 1-2 minutes.

What this supports: BNs-BSA nanoparticles confer strong photothermal conversion in vivo at the tumor site under 808 nm irradiation — a real, substantial (~8-9 C) temperature increase relative to the PBS control under matched laser conditions. This is direct, quantitative support for photothermal heating capability (a "service_capability" of photothermal conversion/PTT), and the magnitude (reaching ~49 C) is consistent with a temperature range often cited as sufficient for photothermal ablation of tumor tissue.

What it leaves open: the panel only shows temperature vs. time for two treatment groups with no error bars, replicate count, or statistical comparison, so reproducibility/significance cannot be assessed from the image alone. It does not show dose-response (different BNs-BSA concentrations or laser powers), nor does it establish that this temperature rise translates into tumor ablation, therapeutic efficacy, or safety (those would require separate panels, e.g., tumor volume/survival data). It also does not localize the heating precisely (whole-tumor surface vs. core) or address off-target/systemic heating. So this measurement settles that BNs-BSA is photothermally active and heats tumors substantially more than PBS under these irradiation conditions, but it does not by itself establish therapeutic outcome, dose-response, or statistical robustness.
