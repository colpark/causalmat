THE ANSWER
This measurement settles that the surface modification of BST is real, not an artifact of either technique alone. FTIR's new bands (2930, 2864, 1122 cm⁻¹, seen only in BST-SH) establish that a chemically distinct species bearing CH2-containing aliphatic groups and a Si-O-type linkage is present on/at the surface. TGA's ~1% weight-loss step at 180-250 °C, present only in the composites and absent in pure PAEN, independently confirms that this is not merely a spectroscopic signature but corresponds to an actual, quantifiable mass of material that decomposes thermally. Because each technique corroborates what the other cannot show on its own — FTIR alone can't prove the species is a bulk, weighable component, and TGA alone can't say what chemical species is being lost — the pairing justifies the conclusion that a distinct silane-derived organic/Si-containing surface species has been successfully attached to BST in the composite, at a small but detectable mass fraction (~1%).

What it leaves open: it does not establish that the attachment is covalent (Si-O-BST grafting) rather than strong physisorption/adsorption of MPTMS or silane residues — nothing in either signal distinguishes bond type. It does not give a quantitative link between the FTIR band intensities and the TGA mass-loss value; the two are correlated only as co-occurring qualitative observations, not calibrated against each other. It gives no information on surface coverage or density of modification. And overall, this remains an indirect, semiquantitative case for surface presence, not a substitute for a direct bonding-verification method (e.g., XPS, solid-state NMR, or similar).
