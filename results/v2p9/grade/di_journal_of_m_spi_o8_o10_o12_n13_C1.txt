THE ANSWER
This measurement supports the conclusion that low-level Se doping produces a favorable transport combination rather than a trade-off: across the doping range surveyed (x = 0.005-0.02, and still at x = 0.04), samples show simultaneously enhanced 300 K Hall mobility (145-170 cm2/Vs, falling to ~125 cm2/Vs at x=0.04, all above the literature envelope) and sharply reduced 300 K resistivity (from ~650 down to 12-23 micro-ohm m relative to undoped/literature reference). Because these two signatures co-occur in the same samples over the same doping range, the result justifies the qualitative claim that Se doping does not lower resistivity merely by adding carriers at the cost of mobility (as conventional doping often does) but instead yields both higher mobility and lower resistivity together, at least at the doping levels tested. The x=0.04 point strengthens this because it is explicitly compared at similar carrier density n_H, isolating mobility as a contributor there.

What it leaves open: for the lower-x points (0.005-0.02), resistivity depends jointly on n_H and mu_H, and since carrier density was not held or compared at similar n_H for those points, the drop in resistivity cannot be attributed to the mobility increase alone versus a concurrent rise in carrier density from doping. The data are only available at a few discrete x values, so this supports an ordering across doping levels, not a continuous or quantitative dose-response curve. The opposite temperature trends between doped and undoped material indicate different scattering/degeneracy regimes, but this contrast is not used to disentangle mobility from carrier-density effects. Finally, everything is confined to 300 K comparisons against literature/undoped baselines, so no mechanism is established — only a co-occurring pattern of enhanced mobility and reduced resistivity.
