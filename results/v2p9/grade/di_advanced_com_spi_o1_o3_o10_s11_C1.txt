THE ANSWER
This result settles that the surface (interface) of the particle has been chemically altered in a way that co-occurs with a functional change in that same interface's behavior toward the two liquid phases. Concretely: the appearance of CH2 and Si-O bands specific to BST-SH, together with the observation that BST-SH (and only BST-SH) disperses in the upper (presumably nonpolar/organic) layer while BST-OH settles in the lower (aqueous/polar) layer, jointly support the conclusion that grafting MPTMS onto the particle surface converts that surface from hydrophilic to lipophilic character. Because the chemical signature and the partitioning behavior appear together specifically in the modified sample, the measurement justifies treating the surface-chemistry change and the interfacial-behavior change as linked to the same modification event, not as two independent, unrelated observations.

What it leaves open is substantial, however. First, this is a single binary contrast (BST-OH vs BST-SH), so it cannot establish a dose-response or graded relationship between degree of grafting and degree of hydrophobicity shift — it shows a switch, not a tunable structure-property relationship. Second, no grafting density or quantity is measured, so the strength or extent of the interfacial change cannot be tied quantitatively to any structural parameter. Third, and most important for "structure" specifically, the CH2 (organic tail) and Si-O (siloxane linkage) bands appear together in the same spectrum, so this measurement cannot separate which structural feature — the organic thiol tail versus the covalent siloxane anchoring — is actually responsible for driving the interfacial/wettability switch. So the measurement supports a real link between surface chemical modification and interfacial phase behavior, but does not resolve the mechanism (which structural element governs it) or the quantitative structure-interface relationship.
