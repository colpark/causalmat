THE ANSWER
Both TEM micrographs (F4a, F4c) show dense, dark, tangled dislocation contrast, and the authors have hand-labeled regions "Dislocations pileup" in both. F4c is the more diagnostic panel: a curved lamella/phase boundary runs across the upper portion of the image, and an arrow explicitly points to a dark, continuous band of dislocation contrast running parallel to and against that boundary — this is the classic morphology of a dislocation pile-up (glide dislocations arrested at a barrier, stacking into a linear high-density band). F4a shows a similarly dense, tangled dislocation network inside the FCC(L12) phase, but its printed caption is the more generic "dislocations configurations in the FCC(L12) phase," and no boundary or arrow is highlighted — the "pileup" label there appears to mark local density rather than a boundary-adjacent band.

What this justifies: the images support that dislocations do concentrate into a dense, boundary-parallel band at a lamella/phase interface, consistent qualitatively with pile-up-type accumulation, and that dense dislocation tangles are also present within the phase interior away from that specific boundary.

What it leaves open: TEM bright-field contrast alone (no diffraction vector/g·b analysis shown) cannot establish Burgers vector, slip system, or that the boundary is truly blocking rather than just being a preferential imaging/thickness artifact; it cannot distinguish a genuine pinned pile-up from a cell wall, sub-boundary, or overlapping slip bands in projection; there is no dislocation density/spacing quantification, and no stress or Hall-Petch-type mechanistic inference can be drawn from a single static image pair.
