The computed free-energy diagram (F1a) plots ΔG(Co2+→Co3+) against ΔG(Co3+→Co4+) for four Co-based (oxy)hydroxide compositions, with the labeled reference chain CoO2 ← CoOOH ← Co(OH)2 shown above via H+ (de)protonation steps. What it shows: CoCrOOH has the lowest ΔG for the first oxidation step (Co2+→Co3+, ~1.05 eV) but a mid/higher barrier for Co3+→Co4+ (~6.47 eV region relative to the others); CoFeOOH has the lowest ΔG for the second step (Co3+→Co4+, ~6.2 eV) but the highest ΔG for the first step (~1.4 eV); CoFeCrOOH sits between these two on both axes; and Co(OH)2 (black point, no transition metal doping) has the highest combined values on both steps (~1.4 eV / ~6.68 eV), i.e., the least favorable oxidation thermodynamics of the set.

What this supports: doping with Cr versus Fe trades off which oxidation step is thermodynamically eased — Cr-doping preferentially lowers the barrier to the first oxidation (Co2+→Co3+), Fe-doping preferentially lowers the barrier to the second (Co3+→Co4+), and combining both (CoFeCrOOH) gives an intermediate, more balanced profile across both steps compared to either single dopant. Undoped Co(OH)2 is confirmed as the thermodynamically worst performer for both oxidation steps among the four, consistent with dopants improving accessibility of higher Co oxidation states relevant to OER/MEC catalytic activity.

What it leaves open: this is a purely computational (DFT-type) free-energy comparison of static oxidation-state transitions — it does not establish the actual reaction pathway, kinetics, rate-limiting step identity, or which oxidation state is catalytically active under operating conditions. It also does not show experimental validation (e.g., in situ spectroscopy) that these computed transitions occur in that order or magnitude during real electrolysis, nor does it establish causality between lower ΔG and improved macroscopic MEC/OER performance — only that the computed thermodynamic landscape differs by composition.
