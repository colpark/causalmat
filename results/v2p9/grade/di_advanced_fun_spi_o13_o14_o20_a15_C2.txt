THE ANSWER
This measurement supports a qualitative mechanistic link, not a quantitative one, between the material's optical absorption at 808 nm and its photothermal heating pathway. The static NIR absorbance data (linear A/L rise with concentration, alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1, R2 = 0.9999) establish that the material itself is a strong, concentration-proportional absorber at the irradiation wavelength. The independent IR-camera data show that under actual laser irradiation, spot temperature at 6 minutes rises with both concentration and laser power, while water alone stays cold. Together these justify the conclusion that absorption of 808 nm light by the material -- rather than direct solvent heating -- is the operative mechanism driving the photothermal effect: more absorbing species and more delivered optical energy both yield more heat, and the water control rules out simple laser-solvent heating as the pathway.

What this leaves open is substantial. Because A/L is a no-irradiation optical property and the temperature data are a single time-point under irradiation, the two datasets are only qualitatively consistent (same direction of trend with concentration), not quantitatively linked -- there is no fitted rate law converting absorbed photons into degrees of heating. The paper's ~19.4% photothermal conversion efficiency figure cannot be derived from these two panels alone; it requires a separate energy-balance/calorimetric analysis not shown here. Both series (four concentration points, a short power range at one concentration) are sparse trend checks, not dose-response curves. Finally, while solvent-only heating is excluded, the data cannot rule out secondary contributions such as scattering or convective heat transfer alongside true absorptive conversion.
