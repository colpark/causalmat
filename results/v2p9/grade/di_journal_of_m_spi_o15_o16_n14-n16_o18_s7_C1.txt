This result supports a general, qualitative association: within the Se-doped series, samples exhibiting suppressed lattice-plus-bipolar thermal conductivity (kappa_L+kappa_b) are found among the compositions that also achieve respectable room-temperature ZT values approaching the undoped n-Bi2Te3 reference (~0.88). Concretely, x=0.01 shows a low kappa_L+kappa_b (~0.69 W/mK at 300 K, falling further to ~0.60 W/mK at 400-450 K) and a doped-series ZT of 0.63-0.84 across x=0.005-0.04, consistent with the physical expectation that reducing lattice and bipolar heat conduction can help raise the figure of merit. That is the extent of what this settles: a plausible, directionally consistent link between suppressed kappa_L+kappa_b and competitive ZT in this doping series, not a quantitative or causal one.

What it leaves open is substantial. First, the composition with the reported low kappa_L+kappa_b (x=0.01) is not the composition with the peak ZT (x=0.005, ZT~0.84); the kappa_L+kappa_b value at x=0.005 is not given, so the data cannot actually pair the best-ZT sample with a specific low-kappa_L value. Second, ZT is a ratio involving total thermal conductivity (lattice+bipolar plus electronic) and the power factor (Seebeck coefficient times electrical conductivity squared), none of which are reported here, so kappa_L+kappa_b alone cannot explain the ZT trend or its magnitude. Third, because composition (doping level) varies across the same series, kappa_L+kappa_b and ZT co-vary together by construction, so the apparent relationship could instead be driven by other doping-induced changes (e.g., carrier concentration, power factor) rather than by kappa_L+kappa_b itself - the data do not isolate a causal or even a clean correlational link. Finally, the temperature ranges are mismatched (300-475 K vs. only 300 K), so whether this association persists across temperature is untested; only the single 300 K point can be compared.
