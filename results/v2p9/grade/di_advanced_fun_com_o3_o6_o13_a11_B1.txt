The calibration curve (F3c) shows A/L at 808 nm is strictly linear in concentration (R2 = 0.9999, alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1) over ~12.5-200 ug/mL. On its own this only establishes that the reduced material obeys Beer-Lambert behavior and can serve as a quantitative photothermal-conversion/optical-absorption standard for this suspension as a bulk, ensemble-averaged sample -- it says nothing about phase composition or particle-level structure.

The TEM+XRD result establishes (with caveats) that the reduced sheets are a two-phase composite carrying both metallic Bi and Bi2O3, but leaves open whether that heterogeneity is spatially uniform or segregated, and whether a single bulk optical/physical measurement on the material would even be well defined (i.e., whether the material behaves as one consistent bulk medium).

Combined: the clean single-slope Beer-Lambert linearity in F3c is only possible if the ensemble of particles in suspension presents a reproducible, concentration-proportional optical response -- i.e., the bulk material (however phase-heterogeneous at the particle/lattice level) behaves as a single well-mixed optical medium at the macroscopic/bulk scale used for extinction measurements. So the combination supports that the two-phase composite identified by TEM/XRD is compositionally consistent/homogeneous enough across the measured concentration range to yield one linear alpha, rather than each phase or each subpopulation of particles contributing wildly different, concentration-dependent absorption behavior that would break linearity or require a multi-component fit.

What this still leaves open: the linear optical calibration does not identify which phase (Bi metal, Bi2O3, or the composite together) drives the absorption at 808 nm, does not confirm the two phases are co-located within individual particles versus present as separate particle populations, and does not resolve the earlier-noted gaps (no elemental mapping, no particle-resolved diffraction, no quantitative phase-fraction/intensity match). The optical measurement is a bulk average, so it cannot rule out spatial phase segregation at the particle scale, only that whatever the internal phase distribution is, it is reproducible enough across dilutions to give one linear response.
