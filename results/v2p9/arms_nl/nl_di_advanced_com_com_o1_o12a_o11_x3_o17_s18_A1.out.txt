These two panels (F6c dielectric constant vs. temperature, F6d dielectric loss vs. temperature) support the stated measurement fairly directly. All four curves (pure PAEN and the three BST@Ag-loaded composites) are essentially flat from room temperature up to roughly 130-135 C, then bend upward into a steep rise through 180 C. Pure PAEN's knee sits somewhat higher (~150-160 C) and its eventual rise is steepest in absolute terms for the dielectric constant, consistent with the reported PAEN knee near 155 C, while the BST@Ag composites show an earlier onset (~130-135 C) but generally higher absolute permittivity/loss values below and through the knee. The loss panel additionally shows the composite curves peaking and then declining slightly toward 180 C (a loss maximum near 170 C), whereas pure PAEN's loss is still rising at the plot's upper limit.

What this justifies: the qualitative claim that permittivity and dielectric loss are thermally stable (flat) below a knee temperature and increase sharply above it, and that the composite (filled) samples exhibit this knee at a lower temperature than the neat polymer, i.e., filler loading shifts the onset of dielectric degradation downward relative to pure PAEN's ~155 C knee.

What it leaves open: the panels do not establish a mechanism (e.g., glass transition, interfacial polarization, moisture/ionic conduction) for the knee, do not give quantitative uncertainty/error bars, do not compare the three Ag loadings' knee positions to each other with statistical confidence (they appear nearly coincident), and do not connect this behavior to actual service performance, reliability, or failure under real operating conditions—only that use above ~130-135 C in this composite risks a rapid rise in dielectric constant and loss.
