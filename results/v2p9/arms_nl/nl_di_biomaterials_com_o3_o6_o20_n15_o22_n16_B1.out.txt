The panel (F11b) shows relative bacterial survival for Scaffolds+Ampicillin declining from ~105% (day 1, not significant) to ~81% (day 3, *) to ~52% (day 7, *) versus blank (100%) and drug-free Scaffolds (110-118%, flat, no antibacterial effect on their own). Drug-free scaffolds never differ meaningfully from blank at any timepoint, so the antibacterial effect is attributable specifically to the loaded ampicillin, not the scaffold matrix.

Read together with the established release-curve result, this settles that the released ampicillin remains bioactive and produces a real, escalating antibacterial (bactericidal/bacteriostatic) effect that tracks the same timeline over which release was shown to occur and then plateau: little effect at day 1 (early release), a significant effect by day 3 (near the ~780 ug plateau onset), and the strongest effect by day 7 (after release has essentially stopped, ~795 ug). So the combination supports that the reservoir-type release delivers a functionally effective, sustained dose of active drug — release is not just mass leaving the material, it corresponds to genuine antibacterial service capability that persists (indeed strengthens) even after the release reservoir is exhausted.

What remains open: (1) whether this antibacterial assay was run on the same specific composition (2Co-MBG) as the release curve, or on a generically labeled "Scaffolds" preparation — the caption does not specify Co content, so exact material identity across the two datasets is not confirmed; (2) no dose-response or mechanistic link tying the specific ug amounts released to the specific survival percentages; (3) still no evidence that release/killing proceeds via the mesopore network specifically, versus surface or matrix-mediated release, since the porosity result remains structural/positional only.
