Best-guess reconstruction (no actual data/figures available to me, so this is inferential):

The paper compares Al2O3-YSZ composite thermal barrier coatings against plain YSZ coatings, tracking microstructural evolution (porosity/pore morphology, microcrack density, sintering-driven densification, phase/grain changes) through thermal cycling, and correlating this with measured cycle life to failure/spallation.

Read together with the established earlier result that sintering-induced densification raises a coating's effective stiffness and lowers its strain tolerance (STR) — the property that lets a coating accommodate thermal-expansion mismatch strain without cracking — this paper's microstructure data lets you close the causal loop that neither result closes alone. The earlier result only established the mechanism in the abstract (densification → lower STR → likely earlier cracking) without tying it to a specific composition or measured lifetime. This paper only shows a correlation between composition and cycle life without an independent account of why. Combined, they support the conclusion that Al2O3 addition retards the sintering/densification process, preserves porosity and microcrack network, keeps STR higher for longer, and this—rather than some unrelated compositional effect (e.g., simple hardness or thermal-conductivity change)—is the operative mechanism extending thermal cycle life in Al2O3-YSZ over plain YSZ.

What remains open: whether STR itself was directly measured in this coatings study (as opposed to inferred from porosity/crack proxies), whether the sintering-suppression effect is durable at cycle counts or temperatures beyond those tested, whether interfacial/TGO-related damage rather than STR is the dominant failure driver, and whether the mechanism generalizes across deposition methods or Al2O3 content ranges beyond what was tested.
