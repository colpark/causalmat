This combined result establishes that, in this alternating two-phase lamellar microstructure, the onset of strain-induced defect contrast at the first strain step is lamella-selective rather than uniform: one of the two lamella types develops mottled internal contrast and interface line contrast while the neighboring lamella type stays featureless. Because the pervasive, sharp-interfaced two-phase lamellar architecture was independently confirmed across scales (TEM to EBSD), this asymmetry can be read as a genuine phase-linked mechanical response — i.e., deformation is being partitioned preferentially into one phase of the lamellar pair — rather than a chance, isolated local artifact in a single field of view.

What it leaves open is substantial. It does not tell you which phase (FCC/L12 or B2) is the one accumulating contrast, since the morphology result never pinned lamella identity to phase name and the contrast observation only distinguishes lamellae by appearance, not composition. It is also strictly local and single-timepoint: valid only at the first strain step and only at the imaged site (panel F3a), so whether this selective response persists, intensifies, reverses, or generalizes across the sample or at higher strains is unknown. The result is purely qualitative — presence versus absence of mottled/line contrast — with no quantification of defect density, no characterization of the defect's physical nature (dislocations, stacking faults, etc.), and no measurement of volume fraction, lamellar spacing, or interface width. So the justified conclusion is: deformation partitioning between phases begins asymmetrically and phase-specifically, but which phase, how much, by what mechanism, and whether it holds more broadly remain undetermined.
