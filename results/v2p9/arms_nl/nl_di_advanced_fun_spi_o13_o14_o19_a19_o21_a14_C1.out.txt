This measurement settles that the photothermal conversion demonstrated in vitro is not merely a solution-phase artifact: the BNs-BSA material, when irradiated at 808 nm in a live tumour, drives tissue temperature to roughly 49 C by 5 minutes, versus roughly 41 C for a PBS control under identical illumination. Combined with the in vitro finding that 808 nm absorption produces concentration- and power-dependent heating in solution while water alone stays cold, the two results together support a single functional claim: the absorption-mediated photothermal effect shown in the dish is also exercised as a real, measurable heating capability at the tumour site in a living animal, exceeding the no-nanomaterial baseline. That is the service capability this pairing justifies -- a qualitative "it works in vivo too" claim, not a quantitative one.

What it leaves open is substantial. There is no in vivo concentration or power series, so no dose- or power-response curve can be drawn for the animal setting, only presence versus absence of heating. The in vitro qualifications persist unresolved: the absorbance-to-heat link is qualitative, the ~19.4% conversion efficiency is not derived from this data, and scattering or convective contributions have not been excluded. The in vivo comparison also cannot separate two possible explanations for the larger temperature rise -- intrinsic photothermal conversion by the material itself, versus simply more absorbing material having accumulated at the tumour (biodistribution/uptake), or some combination. Finally, the two temperature readings are not commensurate: tissue perfusion and conduction differ from solution/dish conditions, so the ~49 C/~41 C tumour figures cannot be directly compared in magnitude to the 6-minute solution spot temperatures or the absorbance/length slope reported in vitro.</message>
