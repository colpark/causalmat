This measurement (the three reversible, cycle-stable CV redox couples, read together with the earlier finding that the particle is a single homogeneous Na-V-Fe-C-N-O compound) settles that the compound possesses genuine, intrinsic multi-electron electrochemical activity — a necessary structural prerequisite for any battery-relevant "service capability." Because the redox behavior is confirmed to arise from one phase rather than from an impurity or a physical mixture, it supports the claim that this material itself, as a single compound, is redox-active and that this activity is reproducible and does not degrade over at least 20 electrochemical cycles.

What it does not settle: it does not tell you which element(s) — Fe, V, or both — carry each of the three couples, since CV peak potentials alone give co-location in voltage, not elemental or site assignment. It does not establish the electron-transfer mechanism, i.e., whether each couple is a one- or multi-electron process, or which crystallographic/framework site is involved. It also cannot be merged with the TGA thermal-stability data (gradual mass loss to ~350C, sharp step at ~360C) into a single "stability" claim — electrochemical cycling stability and thermal decomposition threshold are distinct regimes measured by different means. Finally, neither this result nor the earlier homogeneity finding supplies the exact chemical formula, Na:Fe:V stoichiometric ratio, or hydration state, so no quantitative capacity, rate capability, or site-resolved redox assignment can yet be derived. In short: intrinsic, stable, single-phase multi-electron redox activity is established; elemental/mechanistic assignment, thermal-limit correlation, and full stoichiometry remain open.</message>
