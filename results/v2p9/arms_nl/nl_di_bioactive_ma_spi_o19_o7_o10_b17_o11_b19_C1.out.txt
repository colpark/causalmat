This settles a limited, endpoint-level association, not a general or causal claim about the PRF/service_capability indicator.

What it supports: at the two samples common to both measurements, TiO2 (highest PL intensity) shows the lowest photocatalytic degradation (~20%), and Er0.5Ce0.2 (lowest PL intensity) shows the highest degradation (~46% at 60 min, same relative ranking at 20 and 40 min). Because the two orderings point the same direction at these shared endpoints, the result supports treating lower PL intensity as co-occurring with, and thus as a plausible qualitative indicator of, higher degradation performance — consistent with the usual interpretation that reduced PL signals less electron-hole recombination and better photocatalytic activity.

What it leaves open:
1. Coverage — the PL series has only three samples while degradation has four (Ce0.1, Ce0.3 also measured); the aligned direction is confirmed only at the two shared endpoints, not across the fuller degradation ranking, so intermediate samples' consistency is untested.
2. Nature of the comparison — PL is a relative three-point qualitative ordering, degradation is a cumulative percentage over time; matching directions between two differently-scaled quantities shows correlation at the endpoints only, not that reduced recombination causes the higher degradation.
3. Mechanism — no charge transfer resistance or photocurrent data is available, so no mechanistic pathway from the PL indicator to the degradation outcome is demonstrated.
4. Attribution — EDS cannot distinguish Er3+ from a Ce3+/Ce4+ mixture, so even where PL and degradation align, which dopant ion drives the effect is unresolved.

Bottom line: the pairing justifies citing the PL trend as directionally consistent with, and suggestive of, the degradation/service_capability trend at the TiO2/Er0.5Ce0.2 endpoints — a supportive but narrow, correlational, non-mechanistic result — while leaving full-ranking consistency, causality, mechanism, and dopant attribution all open.
