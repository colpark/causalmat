Panel F6a plots flexural strength (σ, MPa) vs. sintering temperature (1400–1700 °C) for three samples, S1 (red squares), S2 (black circles), S3 (cyan triangles), with error bars at each point.

What the figure actually shows: S1 and S3 overlap closely at 1400 °C (~165–170 MPa) and 1500 °C (~185–195 MPa), i.e., no meaningful difference between them at those two temperatures. At 1600 °C, S1 jumps sharply to ~465 MPa (large error bar) while S3 stays flat at ~195 MPa — a real divergence, but it appears only at the single highest temperature tested for S3/S1. S2 is measured over a different, higher temperature window (1550–1700 °C) and never exceeds ~170 MPa, peaking at 1650 °C then declining slightly to ~145 MPa at 1700 °C; S2 has no data point in common with S1/S3 except roughly the 1600 °C region, where it (~98 MPa) is far below S1 (~465 MPa) and also below S3 (~195 MPa).

This supports the conclusion that whatever processing/route distinguishes S1 confers a large flexural-strength advantage specifically at 1600 °C, and that S2's route yields consistently lower strength across its tested range than either S1 or S3. It does not support a general claim that S1 is stronger at all temperatures — at 1400–1500 °C it is statistically indistinguishable from S3.

Left open: the mechanism behind S1's jump at 1600 °C (only one point, wide error bar, no replication trend); whether S1 and S2 were tested at fully overlapping temperatures (they were not, so a fair S1-vs-S2 comparison is only possible near 1600 °C); and whether S1's high value is reproducible or an outlier.
