This combination settles that 2Co-MBG — the same batch confirmed by TEM/XRD to have a genuine periodic mesoporous structure (a single low-angle reflection corresponding to a ~5-6 nm center-to-center repeat, consistent with the pattern seen across MBG/2Co/5Co) — is also the material whose ampicillin release was measured, and that release is graded and self-limiting: roughly 340 ug at 8 h, 670 ug at 24 h, 780 ug at 72 h, plateauing near 795 ug by 168 h. Together these justify the general conclusion that this mesostructured glass acts as a sustained-release drug carrier, delivering the bulk of its ampicillin load within the first 24-72 h and then leveling off, rather than releasing all at once or continuing to release indefinitely.

What this does not establish is any mechanistic link between the structure and the release behavior. Because only a repeat spacing (not an actual pore diameter) was measured, no specific pore size can be credited with producing these release amounts or with setting when the plateau occurs. Because TEM ordering was confirmed only locally (~200 nm), the release curve cannot be attributed to a uniform bulk mesostructure across the whole sample - it may reflect only partially ordered material. Because mesophase symmetry/connectivity was never determined (single reflection only), no claim can be made about how channel geometry shapes the release profile. Because Co content was not tied quantitatively to the diffraction shifts, it's unknown whether or how cobalt loading modulates release via structural change. Finally, since release is a cumulative-amount curve and the structural data is static periodicity (not a transport measurement), no diffusion-rate-to-structure relationship can be inferred - the association is one of co-occurrence in the same material, not of demonstrated causation from structure to release kinetics.
