Likely content: The paper reports that Ce/Er co-doped TiO2 kills bacteria (e.g., E. coli/S. aureus) rapidly under visible-light illumination, presumably via a log-reduction or kill-percentage value measured over a short exposure time, attributed to narrowed band gap (Ce) plus upconversion of longer-wavelength light to UV/visible-active energies (Er), boosting reactive-oxygen-species generation.

The established earlier result is almost certainly that plain/undoped TiO2 is essentially inert as an antibacterial photocatalyst under visible light and only works under UV, because its band gap (~3.2 eV) doesn't absorb visible photons.

Combined, the two results settle a causal/mechanistic point that neither settles alone: the new visible-light kill-rate value on its own could just mean "this doped material happens to kill bacteria," without showing why; the old UV-only result on its own only shows pure TiO2's limitation, not that doping fixes it. Together they support the conclusion that Ce/Er co-doping is what confers visible-light photocatalytic antibacterial activity — i.e., doping causally extends the active spectral range from UV into visible, converting an otherwise visible-light-inactive material into a rapidly bactericidal one.

What remains open: whether Ce, Er, or their combination (synergy vs. either dopant alone) drives the effect; the underlying mechanism (band-gap narrowing vs. upconversion vs. defect states); dose-response, exposure-time, and concentration dependence; durability/reusability of the photocatalyst; efficacy against other bacterial species or biofilms; and any in vivo or cytotoxicity data.
