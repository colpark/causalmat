Panel F11b (viewed) shows three bars per day (1, 3, 7): Blank control ~100% at each day; drug-free Scaffolds ~110-118% (not marked significant, i.e. indistinguishable from blank); Scaffolds+Ampicillin ~105% (day1, ns), ~81% (day3, *), ~52% (day7, *), each starred as significantly lower than both blank and drug-free scaffolds.

Read together with the established mesostructure/release result, this settles that the same mesostructured 2Co-MBG carrier that shows a graded, cumulative ampicillin release (rising through 72h, plateauing by 168h) also confers a genuine, ampicillin-dependent antibacterial capability that strengthens over time in culture: the carrier matrix itself is inert (drug-free scaffolds track blank), so the killing effect is attributable to the loaded drug, and it grows from non-significant at day 1 to a ~50% relative-survival reduction by day 7 — i.e., the carrier does deliver functionally active, increasingly effective antibiotic over the same multi-day window in which release was shown to accumulate.

What the pairing does not settle, and in fact newly exposes: cumulative release is essentially flat between 72h (day 3, ~780 µg) and 168h (day 7, ~795 µg) — i.e., released dose has plateaued — yet antibacterial killing nearly doubles in that same interval (81%→52% survival). Because release is cumulative amount, not rate, and survival is a single endpoint per day, the combination cannot explain this: whether continued killing past the release plateau reflects prolonged bacterial exposure time, a lag in bactericidal action, or something structural remains unresolved. It also still cannot tie any specific pore size, bulk-scale uniformity, or channel geometry to this antibacterial performance, per the prior qualifications.
