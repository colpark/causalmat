The IR panels (F3e, F3g) show only qualitative thermal-image color maps over a 6-min irradiation window, not extracted numeric temperature-time traces.

What this measurement supports: a clear, monotonic dose-response photothermal effect. In F3e, the hot spot (yellow/red core) grows larger and more intense with increasing concentration in the fixed order 200 > 100 > 50 > 25 µg/mL, while the water control stays blue/cold throughout all 6 timepoints. In F3g, at fixed (200 µg/mL) concentration, the hot spot similarly intensifies with increasing 808 nm laser power density from 0.5 to 1.5 W. Together these two panels justify the conclusion that the material is a genuine NIR (808 nm) photothermal agent — it converts absorbed light to heat, the water blank rules out laser-only heating of the medium, and the heating response scales positively and reproducibly with both material dose and irradiance. This is solid qualitative evidence of photothermal responsiveness (PRP) and dose/power dependence.

What it leaves open: these are color-coded IR snapshots, not calibrated temperature-vs-time curves with numbers, error bars, or replicates, so no quantitative photothermal conversion efficiency (η) can be derived from these two panels alone. There's no photostability data (repeated laser on/off cycles), no wavelength-selectivity control, no concentration/power values below 25 µg/mL or above 1.5 W to bound the dose-response, and no link to actual therapeutic (PTT) efficacy, cytotoxicity, or in vivo outcome. So the panels establish that photothermal response exists and scales with dose/power, but not its magnitude, efficiency, durability, or downstream therapeutic value.
