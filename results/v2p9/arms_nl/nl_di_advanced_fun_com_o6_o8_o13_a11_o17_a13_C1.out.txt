This measurement (linear NIR absorbance at 808 nm, A/L vs. concentration, R2 = 0.9999, alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1) settles that the phase-identified sample -- established by XRD and Raman as a majority hexagonal-Bi material with only a minor, secondary Bi2O3 phase -- exhibits a strong, reproducible, concentration-proportional NIR extinction. Because the composition of the material was already fixed by the prior XRD/Raman result, this optical measurement can be attributed to that same Bi-dominant bismuthene sample as a whole. What it supports: the material possesses a robust, quantifiable NIR absorption property (a well-defined mass extinction coefficient with near-perfect linearity across concentrations), which is the kind of optical behavior needed to justify pursuing downstream applications such as photothermal conversion.

What it leaves open, plainly: (1) it does not tell you which phase is responsible for the absorption -- since the spatial arrangement of Bi and Bi2O3 (heterojunction versus separate, non-contacting domains) was never determined, the alpha value cannot be assigned specifically to the majority Bi phase rather than partly to the minor oxide; (2) it says nothing about morphology or size (no nanoflake/nanosheet dimensions were measured), so no size- or shape-dependent explanation of the extinction coefficient is supported; (3) it does not quantify how much Bi2O3 is actually present, so claiming the oxide's contribution is negligible remains unproven, only that it is a minor trace phase; and (4) critically, alpha is strictly a linear optical absorbance/extinction metric -- it demonstrates nothing about photothermal conversion efficiency, heat generation, or any actual energy-conversion performance. In short: the measurement justifies "this material absorbs NIR light strongly and predictably," but not "this material converts that light to heat efficiently" or "the Bi phase alone causes the absorption."
