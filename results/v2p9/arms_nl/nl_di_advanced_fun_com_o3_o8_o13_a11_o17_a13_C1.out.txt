This measurement (linear A/L vs. concentration at 808 nm, R2=0.9999, alpha=6.4 L g-1 cm-1), taken together with the earlier TEM+Raman result identifying a new, morphologically distinct, metallic-Bi-like nanoparticle phase produced by reduction, supports the conclusion that the reduced material as a whole is a strong, well-behaved NIR absorber at 808 nm — i.e., the reduction product has plausible value as an NIR-absorbing (e.g., photothermal-candidate) material. The bulk sample that was shown to bear the new Bi-like phase is the same bulk sample shown to have clean, concentration-linear 808 nm absorption, so the pairing is legitimate at the level of "this material, overall, has both a distinct new phase and useful optical behavior."

What it does not establish: it does not show that the Bi-like nanoparticles themselves, as opposed to the unreduced sheet substrate or residual oxide, are the actual source of the absorption — Raman is a bulk/ensemble technique not spatially co-registered with the TEM-imaged particles, and the extinction-coefficient measurement is an independent bulk optical measurement, not made on the same imaged particles. So the two findings are correlational at the whole-sample level, not mechanistically linked. It also does not quantify the conversion fraction of sheet to nanoparticles, nor rule out residual oxide contributing to or masking the signal. Finally, it says nothing about photothermal conversion efficiency, heating performance under irradiation, or any other functional/in-use metric — only that linear optical absorption at 808 nm is strong. Any claim of demonstrated photothermal utility, or that the Bi phase specifically drives the absorption, goes beyond what this pairing of results justifies.</message>
