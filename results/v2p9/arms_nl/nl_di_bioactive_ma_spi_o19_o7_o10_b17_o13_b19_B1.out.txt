Findings from the panels (F10a = S. aureus, F10b = E. coli; both show Control, TiO2, Er0.5Ce0.1/0.2/0.3Ti-O, each in Dark and Light rows):

Under light, colony counts drop from Control → TiO2 → Ce0.1 → Ce0.2 (fewest for both strains) → Ce0.3 (more than Ce0.2), so light-exposed killing peaks at Er0.5Ce0.2Ti-O for both S. aureus and E. coli. In the dark, Control and TiO2 plates are covered with confluent bacterial lawns, while all three Ce-doped plates already show discrete, sparser colonies even without light — a partial, light-independent reduction that is then further reduced by light exposure.

What this adds when combined with the earlier PL/degradation result: it extends the same TiO2 (worst) vs. Er0.5Ce0.2 (best) ordering to a third, independent functional readout — antibacterial killing — for two different bacterial strains. That convergence (lowest PL / highest degradation / fewest light-plate colonies all co-locating at Er0.5Ce0.2Ti-O, and the reverse at TiO2) strengthens the case that whatever the PL indicator tracks is associated with a genuine performance advantage of this composition across multiple assays, not an artifact of one measurement type. It also reveals an optimum-at-Ce0.2 pattern (Ce0.3 partially reverses), consistent with the non-monotonic degradation series already noted.

What remains open: the dark-plate reduction for doped samples shows a light-independent antibacterial component (e.g., ion release or intrinsic surface toxicity) that the PL/degradation story — which is specifically about light-driven charge separation — does not explain, so killing cannot be attributed purely to the photocatalytic/PL mechanism. Mechanistic links (charge-transfer resistance, photocurrent) remain untested, and Er3+ vs Ce3+/Ce4+ attribution is still unresolved by EDS. Colony counts are also only qualitative/visual, not quantified CFU values, so the strength (not just direction) of the antibacterial ordering can't be confirmed.
