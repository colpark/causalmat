What I see: F4a shows a dense tangled dislocation network filling an FCC (L12) grain interior, with dislocations bunching along what looks like a lamella/phase boundary at the image edge. F4c shows the same type of interior dislocation network, but here a sharp, dark, continuous band of dislocations is arrowed and explicitly labeled "Dislocations pileup" running along a curved lamella boundary, clearly distinct in density/contrast from the more diffuse tangle in the grain body.

What this settles: The two TEM micrographs are direct, localized evidence that dislocations generated during deformation accumulate against the lamella boundary rather than passing through it freely — i.e., the boundary is acting as an obstacle to dislocation glide. This supports a mechanism/pathway (MEC) in which lamella (phase or grain) boundaries impede slip and cause local stress concentration via classic pile-up, consistent with a boundary-strengthening (Hall-Petch-like) contribution to the material's flow behavior, and consistent with planar/tangled dislocation storage in the L12 FCC phase feeding that pile-up.

What it leaves open: This is a single static TEM snapshot, so it cannot establish the pile-up's role dynamically — whether it precedes slip transfer into the neighboring phase, triggers boundary-nucleated cracking, or simply reflects transient strain-hardening debris. It does not quantify pile-up length, dislocation count, or the resulting stress concentration, nor identify the active slip system/Burgers vector. It also does not show whether this feature is representative across the microstructure or an isolated site, so the causal link between this pile-up and macroscopic strength/ductility is asserted by caption label, not independently measured here.
