This measurement (really, the combination of the settled chemical-identity result with the PL series) justifies only a correlational conclusion: across the three-sample series TiO2 -> Er0.5Ti-O -> Er0.5Ce0.2, the step at which Er3+ is chemically confirmed (as Er3+ anchored to a Ti-O oxide framework, not just an ambiguous EDS peak) is the same step at which the 390-420 nm PL band—associated with recombination—drops relative to undoped TiO2, and PL falls further in the Ce-containing sample. So what is supported is a co-occurrence: confirmed Er3+ presence tracks with suppressed PL intensity in this series, consistent with Er3+ playing some role in reducing that recombination-linked emission.

What it leaves open is substantial. First, because XPS did not probe Ce binding energies, Ce's oxidation state and chemical role are undetermined, so nothing here shows Ce is responsible for the additional PL drop in Er0.5Ce0.2 versus Er0.5Ti-O—that further suppression could be due to Ce, to some Er-Ce interaction, or to another cause entirely. Second, the data cannot distinguish whether Er3+ is incorporated into the TiO2 lattice (altering band/electronic structure) or merely surface-adsorbed; both are consistent with the observed PL trend. Third, with only three samples, this is an ordinal ranking (strongest, half, lowest), not a quantitative dose-response curve relating Er3+ amount or confirmation strength to degree of PL suppression. Finally, and most fundamentally, this is a co-occurrence of a confirmed species with an optical trend, not a demonstrated causal mechanism—it does not establish that Er3+ specifically, as opposed to Er doping or some other correlated change in the samples, is what suppresses recombination.
