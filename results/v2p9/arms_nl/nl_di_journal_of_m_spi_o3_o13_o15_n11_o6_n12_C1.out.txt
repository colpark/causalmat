This measurement supports the conclusion that the interface between the two lamellae, not merely the bulk of either phase, is a locus of dislocation accumulation at the first strain step. Combining the phase-selective onset of plasticity (mottled/line contrast in one lamella versus a featureless neighbor) with the identification of a dense dislocation band as a pile-up along the lamella boundary gives a concrete microstructural reading: the boundary line contrast is consistent with dislocations piling up against the phase interface, rather than being an imaging artifact or a diffuse feature distributed through the lamella interior. So the pair of observations together justifies saying that the earlier-noted asymmetry between lamellae has a boundary-localized dislocation signature, and that the interface itself is implicated as a site of plastic activity.

What it leaves open is substantial. It does not identify which lamella (A or B) is FCC versus B2, so it cannot say which phase's boundary the pile-up sits against. It does not establish causal mechanics: whether lattice-mismatch internal stress drives the pile-up, or whether the pile-up is what raises yield strength, are not settled by these two qualitative panels. It cannot distinguish an interface/pile-up-driven mechanism from an intrinsic phase slip-resistance mechanism -- the pile-up could just as easily be a downstream consequence of one lamella being intrinsically harder, rather than an independent boundary effect. The comparison is still limited to one location and one strain step (undeformed baseline versus first strain step, F4a/F4c), so it says nothing about whether this is the general or dominant behavior across the microstructure, nor how it evolves with further straining. Finally, both observations are qualitative descriptions rather than measured dislocation densities or counts, so no quantitative link to strength or accumulated deformation follows from this result.
