This measurement (taken together with the earlier TEM+XRD result) justifies the conclusion that the strong, linear, concentration-proportional NIR absorbance at 808 nm (R2 = 0.9999, mass extinction coefficient alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1) is a bulk property of the reduced Bi sheets as actually prepared -- that is, of the same two-phase composite material (metallic hexagonal Bi plus a Bi2O3 component) whose composite/heterostructure nature was established structurally. In practical/value terms, this supports using this specific synthesized material as a photothermal or NIR-absorbing agent: the optical response is real, strong, well-behaved, and scales predictably with how much of the as-prepared material is present, which is what would matter for dosing or engineering purposes.

What it leaves open is the mechanistic attribution. The measurement does not establish which phase -- metallic Bi, the Bi2O3 component, or the interface between them -- is responsible for driving the NIR absorption, because the structural result never spatially or quantitatively linked individual TEM-imaged particles to the XRD-identified phases, and the optical measurement is a bulk solution reading against total mass concentration only, with no phase- or particle-resolved optical readout. Consequently it cannot be said that the specific nanoparticles seen in TEM are the NIR-absorbing entities, nor that any single phase's fraction correlates with absorbance -- the linear fit shows proportionality to total mass, not to a particular phase's contribution. It also inherits the prior limitation that bulk XRD and local TEM evidence remain mutually consistent but not directly correlated, so this optical result cannot be used to close that gap either. In short: the material's bulk NIR-absorbing value is supported; its structural/mechanistic origin within the composite is not resolved.
