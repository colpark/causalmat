The engineering curve (yield ~550-600 MPa, UTS ~1000 MPa, fracture at ~6.2-6.5% strain) shows pronounced work hardening — stress nearly doubles between yield and UTS — over a fairly short, limited ductility range, with continuous hardening and no yield-drop plateau. Read together with the earlier finding that early plasticity is phase-selective and localized as a dislocation pile-up against a lamella interface, the combination supports a coherent qualitative narrative: the strong macroscopic hardening from yield to UTS is consistent with a mechanism in which one lamella phase yields first while dislocations accumulate against the boundary with its (presumably harder) neighbor, generating back-stress/load transfer across the interface that could contribute to the sustained hardening seen macroscopically, and the comparatively low total elongation is consistent with strain being concentrated at boundaries rather than spread broadly, which could promote earlier fracture. This is what the pairing newly supports — a plausible qualitative link between an interface-localized dislocation mechanism at low strain and the shape of the flow curve.

What it does not settle: there is no quantitative connection between the described pile-up (a qualitative TEM/micrograph observation at one location, one strain step) and the measured hardening rate, UTS value, or elongation-to-failure — no dislocation density, stress, or strain-partitioning data ties the two. It remains unknown whether the pile-up mechanism persists, intensifies, or saturates as strain progresses toward the 6.2% failure point, or whether it is what actually initiates fracture. Which phase is harder/yields first, and whether interface pile-up versus intrinsic phase resistance dominates the hardening, are still undetermined. The causal attribution of yield strength or hardening to the pile-up remains unproven.
