Both panels are IR thermal-image grids (0-6 min under 808 nm irradiation), color-coded 25-60 °C.

F3e: rows are 200, 100, 50, 25 ug/mL and H2O, columns are time. The spot visibly goes from blue/green to yellow/orange/red fastest and most intensely at 200 ug/mL, progressively less at 100, 50, 25 ug/mL, and stays blue (cold, ~25 °C) for water across all 6 minutes.

F3g: rows are 0.5, 0.75, 1.00, 1.25, 1.50 W (at fixed 200 ug/mL), columns are time. Same pattern — higher power density gives faster, hotter (redder) spots; 0.5 W barely warms over 6 min while 1.50 W reaches deep red/orange by 3-4 min.

What this measurement supports: the material exhibits laser-induced (photothermal) heating whose magnitude and rate scale monotonically with both agent concentration and incident laser power density, and this heating is not simply an artifact of the solvent (water alone stays cold). This is solid qualitative/semi-quantitative evidence of a concentration- and power-dependent photothermal response consistent with a photothermal-conversion (PRP) mechanism.

What it leaves open: no numeric temperature values, conversion-efficiency (η) calculation, replicate/error bars, or statistical test are shown in these two crops — the images give color-mapped trends only. There's no evidence here about photothermal stability over repeated laser cycles, wavelength selectivity, the underlying absorption mechanism, or downstream biological/therapeutic efficacy (e.g., cell killing, tumor ablation) — this measurement establishes heat generation scales with dose and power, nothing about whether that heat is therapeutically sufficient or mechanistically explained.
