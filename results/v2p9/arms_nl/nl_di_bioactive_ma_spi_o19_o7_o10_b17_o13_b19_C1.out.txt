This measurement, taken together with the earlier established result, settles that at the two endpoint samples shared by both series — TiO2 and Er0.5Ce0.2 — the direction of PL intensity and the direction of photocatalytic degradation move together: TiO2 shows the highest PL (taken as an indicator of higher recombination... actually lower recombination, since higher PL commonly indicates more recombination, but here it's used qualitatively as an indicator) alongside the lowest degradation (~20%), while Er0.5Ce0.2 shows the lowest PL alongside the highest degradation (~46% at 60 min), with the same relative ordering holding at 20 and 40 min as well. This justifies treating the PL-based indicator and photocatalytic degradation as associated in a consistent, reproducible direction across the time course for this pair of samples, i.e., the two independent readouts (a qualitative luminescence ranking and a cumulative percentage degradation curve) are not contradictory and in fact reinforce one another at the extremes of the sample set.

What it leaves open is substantial. First, the agreement is only checked at two of the four degradation samples (TiO2 and Er0.5Ce0.2); Ce0.1 and Ce0.3 are not covered by the PL measurement, so the full ordering across all four degradation samples cannot be validated against PL — only the endpoints. Second, because PL is a coarse three-point qualitative ranking rather than a quantified recombination rate, and degradation is a cumulative percentage, the matching direction shows correlation, not a demonstrated causal mechanism: it does not establish that reduced recombination causes the higher degradation. Third, no charge-transfer resistance or photocurrent data are available, so no mechanistic pathway from the PL indicator to the degradation outcome is shown. Finally, EDS cannot distinguish Er3+ from a Ce3+/Ce4+ mixture, so even where the association holds, which dopant ion drives the effect remains unresolved.
