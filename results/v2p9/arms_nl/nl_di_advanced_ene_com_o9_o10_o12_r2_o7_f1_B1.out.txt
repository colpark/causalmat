Panel F2a (discharge capacity/coulombic efficiency vs. cycle number) shows the w/HCl co-precipitation cathode starting near ~91 mAh/g on first discharge, with coulombic efficiency already near 100% from early cycles, and capacity decaying gradually to ~55 mAh/g by cycle 250 while efficiency stays ~100% throughout.

Read together with the earlier CV/STEM-EDS result, this settles that the single, homogeneous phase's three reversible redox couples (0.55/0.52, 0.89/0.87, 1.08/1.07 V) are not merely spectroscopic artifacts but correspond to a real, quantifiable electrochemical service capability: a first-cycle capacity of ~91 mAh/g attributable to that one compound's multi-redox behavior, with near-unity coulombic efficiency confirming the couples are charge-balanced (reversible in the practical, not just voltammetric, sense). This is new — the CV pairing alone gave no capacity or electron-count information at all.

The combination also surfaces a tension the CV result alone could not: CV peak "persistence" was only verified out to 20 cycles, and the capacity trace now shows that despite this persistence, actual capacity fades substantially (~91 to ~55 mAh/g) over the full 250-cycle window. So reversibility/persistence of the redox couples at short cycle counts does not equate to long-term capacity retention — the earlier qualification that persistence "does not by itself establish long-term stability beyond that window" is now confirmed to matter, since real fade occurs beyond 20 cycles.

What remains open: which of the three couples (and which element/site — Na, V, Fe, C, N, or O) contributes how much of the 91 mAh/g; no electron-transfer count per couple; whether the observed fade reflects loss of one specific couple's contribution or uniform degradation across all three; and whether finer-scale or unimaged particle heterogeneity affects capacity differently than it would the CV signal. Coulombic efficiency near 100% argues against major side-reaction contributions, but does not itself explain the capacity fade mechanism.
