This pairing settles only a contextual, same-sample location: the 2Co material used for the ampicillin release curve is structurally the intermediate case in A's ordering/porosity comparison (XRD peak ~74000, N2 uptake ~5.6 mmol/g -- degraded relative to MBG but well above near-background 5Co). What it supports is the narrow claim that a sample with partial (not maximal) loss of mesoporous order and surface area is nonetheless capable of sustaining substantial, sustained ampicillin release -- rising from ~340 ug at 8 h through ~670 ug (24 h) and ~780 ug (72 h) to a ~795 ug plateau by 168 h. That is the full extent of the justified conclusion: co-location of a structural description and a release outcome on the same sample, not a demonstrated causal or even correlational link between degree of degradation and release behavior.

What it leaves open is substantial. A is a static, cross-sample structural comparison while B is a single-sample, time-resolved kinetic measurement -- different quantity types that cannot be matched magnitude-for-magnitude. Because release data exist only for 2Co (not MBG or 5Co), there is no comparative basis to say the Co-driven decline in order/porosity tracks, drives, or shapes release rate, plateau timing, or total amount released; no dose-response or even qualitative trend across Co content can be established. Consequently the 72-h plateau and ~795 ug total cannot be attributed to, or even correlated with, 2Co's reduced ordering/porosity -- the same profile could equally reflect drug-loading conditions, diffusion kinetics intrinsic to the MBG scaffold class, or other unrelated factors. In short, the measurement supports identity/context pairing only, and leaves the actual structure-release relationship (PRF / service_capability link) empirically untested.
