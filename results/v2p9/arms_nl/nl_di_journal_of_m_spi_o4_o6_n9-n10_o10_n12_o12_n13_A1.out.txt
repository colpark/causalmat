Panel F5c (power factor vs T for x=0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04) confirms the stated measurement: x=0.01 (red circles) is the highest curve throughout, running from ~29 µW cm⁻¹K⁻² at 300 K down to ~22 at 623 K; x=0.005 (black squares) is the lowest of the doped compositions (~23 at 300 K falling to ~13 at 623 K); undoped x=0 (green) sits far below all doped samples, rising only from ~2 to ~5 µW cm⁻¹K⁻² across the whole range. x=0.02 and x=0.04 fall in between, tracking each other closely and overlapping with x=0.005 at high T.

What this justifies: doping (any nonzero x) massively improves PF over the undoped baseline across the entire 300–623 K range, and among doped levels x=0.01 gives the best PF at every temperature measured, with x=0.005 giving the worst PF among doped samples. This is a clear, monotonic-in-appearance non-monotonicity in dopant content — PF is not simply increasing or decreasing with x; 0.01 is an interior optimum, since 0.005 (lower doping) is worse than 0.02 and 0.04 (higher doping), and 0.04 (highest doping shown here) doesn't exceed 0.01.

What is left open: the panel is power factor only — it does not by itself establish anything about thermal conductivity or ZT, so no conclusion about overall thermoelectric figure of merit can be drawn from this figure alone. It also doesn't reveal the mechanism (carrier concentration vs. mobility vs. Seebeck tradeoff) behind why x=0.01 is optimal, nor whether doping levels between 0.01 and 0.02, or above 0.04, might do better — the curve is only sampled at five discrete x values. Error bars/uncertainty are not shown, so the precision of the ranking (especially the near-overlap of x=0.02/0.04/0.005 at high T) can't be assessed from this image.
