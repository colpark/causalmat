The panel (F5c, caption "power factor") matches the stated measurement closely, so the data are well supported by the image.

What it shows: PF vs. T (300–623 K) for five doping levels. x=0.01 (red circles) is the highest curve across the whole range, ~29 µW cm⁻¹K⁻² near 300 K, declining monotonically to ~22 at 623 K. x=0.02 (blue triangles) and x=0.04 (magenta triangles) track together just below it, in the mid-20s falling to ~19–20. x=0.005 (black squares) is the lowest of the doped compositions, ~23 falling to ~13. The undoped x=0 (green) is far below all doped samples, only ~2 at 300 K, rising modestly to a plateau of ~5 by 550–623 K — the only curve that increases rather than decreases with temperature.

What this justifies: it supports the claim that doping (any amount tested, x=0.005–0.04) massively raises the power factor relative to undoped material, and that x=0.01 is the doping level giving the highest PF over the full temperature range measured, with a roughly 300 K value near 29 and a 623 K value near 22 — consistent with the numbers given. It also supports that x=0.005 is the lowest-performing doped sample, and that PF for doped samples decreases with rising temperature (opposite trend to the undoped sample).

What it leaves open: the panel alone doesn't identify the mechanism (whether the PF gain is driven by conductivity, Seebeck coefficient, or both), doesn't give error bars/uncertainty on the curves, and doesn't say whether x=0.01 is optimal beyond the tested range (0, 0.005, 0.01, 0.02, 0.04) — a value between 0.005 and 0.02, or above 0.04, was not measured. It also doesn't by itself establish overall thermoelectric figure of merit (zT), since that requires thermal conductivity data not shown in this crop.
