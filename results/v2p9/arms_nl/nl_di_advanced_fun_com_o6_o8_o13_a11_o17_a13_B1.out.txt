The photothermal-heating/cooling curve (F3h) shows a real temperature rise under 808 nm irradiation followed by cooling, with a linear -ln(theta) vs. time fit giving a cooling time constant tau_s = 133.4 s and a photothermal conversion efficiency eta = 19.4%. On its own this only shows that light-induced heating and a measurable heat-dissipation time constant/efficiency exist for whatever was irradiated; it says nothing about the material's composition or which component produces the effect.

The earlier result independently fixed that the irradiated sample is phase-identified: majority hexagonal Bi with a minor, trace Bi2O3 phase (XRD + Raman, mutually consistent), and that this same phase-identified material has a strong, linear, quantifiable NIR extinction (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1, R2 = 0.9999) at the same 808 nm wavelength used here.

Read together, the two results settle something neither settles alone: that this phase-identified bismuthene material does not merely absorb 808 nm light linearly, it actually converts that absorbed light into heat with a quantifiable, genuine photothermal effect (real temperature rise, defined cooling time constant, 19.4% conversion efficiency). This closes the earlier-noted gap that linear absorbance alone was "not a measure of energy conversion or in-use performance" — the combination now supports that the material has demonstrated, quantified photothermal-conversion performance, validating the earlier claim of suitability for downstream photothermal use.

What remains open: which phase drives the photothermal effect. The spatial-arrangement question (heterojunction vs. separate Bi/Bi2O3 domains) was never resolved, so the 19.4% efficiency cannot be attributed to the majority Bi phase alone versus a contribution from the minor oxide. Oxide abundance is still unquantified, so its role can't be ruled out. Neither result addresses morphology/size effects on absorption or photothermal performance, and the efficiency figure is a bulk, undecomposed measurement — not phase-resolved.
