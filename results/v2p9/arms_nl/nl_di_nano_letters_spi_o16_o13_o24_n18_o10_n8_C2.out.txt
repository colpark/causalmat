This measurement (the joint retained-structure/oxidation-ranking result) settles that Na capacity fading in Co3O4 is better explained by poor sodiation reactivity than by volume-change-driven structural failure. It supports this because the structural evidence (no lattice collapse, no metallic Co, intact pore network over the first cycle) rules out the volume-change-damage alternative, while the oxidation-degree computation (Na-Co3O4 stalling at ~36% conversion after 0.8 ps, well below Na-Co3S4's ~59% and Li-Co3O4's ~91% at 2 ps) shows that the reaction itself is intrinsically limited even absent structural failure. Together these are consistent with each other and jointly point away from mechanical/volumetric causes of fade and toward chemical/reactivity limits.

What it leaves open is substantial. It does not establish that the short-timescale (picosecond, AIMD-simulated) stalling in oxidation degree persists through the full experimental first-cycle electrochemical timescale — the two quantities differ in kind and duration, so persistence of limited conversion is inferred, not shown directly. It does not isolate whether the low oxidation degree is a sodium-specific effect or a host-lattice/anion effect, since the comparison ranking varies both cation and anion identity simultaneously across the three materials. It does not resolve whether the limited structural change or limited reaction originates at particle surfaces versus in the bulk. It demonstrates correspondence, not a mechanistic link, between why the reaction stalls and why the structure is preserved. And because the oxidation degree is a computed proxy rather than a direct measurement, this result does not quantitatively tie any specific oxidation-degree value to a specific amount of capacity loss — the causal attribution is qualitative, not quantified.
