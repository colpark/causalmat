What the combination settles: The earlier TEM+Raman result establishes that reduction produced a distinct, Bi-like nanoparticle phase in the bulk sample, and separately (from an even earlier step) that this same bulk material has strong, linear, high-R2 optical absorption at 808 nm. The new IR-imaging measurement adds that this absorption converts to measurable, dose-dependent macroscopic heat: temperature rise scales with both dye/dispersion concentration and incident 808 nm power density, in the same material that was chemically/morphologically identified as bearing the new Bi phase and that was already shown to absorb 808 nm light strongly and linearly. Combined, the three results support a coherent, quantitative photothermal narrative: a compositionally identified reduction product exhibits 808 nm absorption that is not just spectroscopically strong but functionally converts to controllable, concentration- and power-dependent photothermal heating -- i.e., the material has practical photothermal-conversion value, not merely favorable absorbance numbers on paper.

What it leaves open: (1) Spatial/mechanistic attribution is still unresolved -- IR thermal imaging is a bulk/macroscopic readout of the whole irradiated spot, not spatially resolved to the TEM-imaged Bi-like particles, so it cannot show the particles (rather than sheet substrate or residual oxide) are the actual heat source. (2) No photothermal conversion efficiency (eta) is computed from these images -- only qualitative/relative temperature rises and color-map trends are shown, so quantitative heating performance (e.g., W/g or % efficiency) remains undetermined. (3) The concentration series and the power series are not shown to be quantitatively cross-consistent (no single model linking alpha, power, and concentration to predicted temperature), so this is corroborating trend-level evidence, not a mechanistic or quantitative closure of the absorption-to-heat pathway.
