This measurement (the joint A+B result) supports attributing Na/Co3O4's capacity fading primarily to poor sodiation reactivity rather than to volume-change-driven structural degradation. It does so by combining two independent lines of evidence: the lattice and pore network of PCO remain structurally intact through the first cycle (ruling out mechanical/structural collapse as the driver of fade), while the computed oxidation-degree trajectory shows the sodiation reaction itself only reaches ~36% conversion and stalls early — well below the ~59% and ~91% seen for Na-Co3S4 and Li-Co3O4 respectively. Together these establish that the electrode does not fail because it falls apart, but because the redox/conversion reaction is intrinsically incomplete — an electrochemical/kinetic limitation rather than a mechanical one.

What it leaves open is substantial. First, the oxidation-degree evidence comes from a short (2 ps) AIMD simulation, so it does not establish that this limited-conversion behavior persists across the full real-world cycling timescale — only that it is short-lived in simulation. Second, because the three-material comparison varies both cation (Li vs Na) and host anion (oxide vs sulfide) simultaneously, it cannot cleanly isolate whether the low conversion in Na-Co3O4 is a sodium-specific chemistry effect or an artifact of the oxide host lattice. Third, neither piece of evidence distinguishes whether the limited reactivity or structural preservation originates at particle surfaces versus in the bulk. Fourth, the correspondence between intact structure and stalled reaction is consistent but not mechanistically demonstrated — it doesn't show *why* poor reactivity and structural integrity co-occur. Finally, the oxidation-degree metric is a computed proxy, not a direct measurement, so no quantitative link between a given oxidation degree and actual capacity loss (in mAh/g or %) is established.
