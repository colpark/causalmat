The IR imaging (F3e, F3g) shows that under 808 nm irradiation the same reduced Bi/Bi2O3 material heats up in a way that tracks both concentration (200 > 100 > 50 > 25 ug/mL, water staying cold) and, at fixed 200 ug/mL, laser power density (0.5 -> 1.5 W). Read against the earlier settled result -- that this same two-phase reduced material has a linear, bulk NIR extinction at 808 nm (R2 = 0.9999, alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1) -- the combination supports that the material's bulk optical absorption at 808 nm is causally converted into heat, and that this photothermal output scales with the same variable (total mass concentration) that governs the optical extinction, plus with incident laser power as expected for an absorption-driven heating process. So together they justify: the composite reduced Bi/Bi2O3 sheets are a genuine photothermal-conversion agent at 808 nm, not merely an absorbing medium, and the heating response is concentration- and power-dependent in a manner consistent with (though not independently proving) that the measured bulk absorbance is what drives the heating.

What remains open: neither measurement is phase-resolved, so it is still undetermined whether the metallic Bi component, the Bi2O3 component, or their interface is responsible for either the absorption or the heat generation. The IR data are also a bulk solution/film readout with no particle-level correlation to the TEM-imaged nanostructures, so the TEM-vs-XRD identity gap carries forward unresolved. No photothermal conversion efficiency (eta) is quantified here, and the temperature-concentration/power trend is only a qualitative, monotonic correlation, not a quantitative link back to alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1.
