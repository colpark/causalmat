This measurement settles that the photothermal capability shown in solution is not merely an in-vitro artifact: paired with the tumour reading, it supports the conclusion that BNs-BSA's absorption-mediated photothermal conversion is functionally exercised in vivo, producing a real, PBS-exceeding temperature rise at the tumour site (~49 C vs ~41 C at 5 min) when irradiated at 808 nm. That is the service capability the pairing justifies: the material behaves as an active photothermal agent in a living tumour, not just as a heater in a dish, giving qualitative in-vivo proof of concept for photothermal therapeutic use.

What it leaves open is substantial. It gives no in-vivo dose- or power-response curve (only one two-arm, one-dose, one-power, single-timepoint comparison), so no quantitative photothermal efficiency or heating-rate claim in vivo can be drawn, unlike the in vitro concentration/power series. It cannot separate how much of the observed tumour heating is due to the material's intrinsic absorption efficiency versus simply how much material accumulated/retained at the tumour versus local tissue optical/perfusion differences -- accumulation and intrinsic conversion are confounded. The underlying in vitro qualifications (qualitative absorbance-heat link, undemonstrated ~19.4% conversion efficiency, unexcluded scattering/convective contributions) remain unresolved. And because tissue dissipation differs fundamentally from solution, the ~49/41 C tumour figures are not numerically comparable to the solution spot temperatures or the A/L slope -- so this is proof that heating occurs in vivo, not a bridge that quantifies or fully explains why.
