Best-guess reconstruction (no actual figure/result was supplied to me, so this is inferential):

The paper reports that Ce/Er co-doped TiO2 achieves rapid, visible-light-driven bacterial killing (e.g., near-complete inactivation of E. coli/S. aureus within a short exposure window, tens of minutes), attributed to lanthanide-induced band-gap narrowing/upconversion that lets the catalyst absorb visible rather than only UV light and generate reactive oxygen species efficiently.

On its own, this new result establishes functional service_capability: the material actually performs the target task (fast antibacterial killing) under a practically relevant light source (visible, not UV). It does not by itself prove *why* — it could be improved surface area, changed crystallinity, or dopant electronic effects, and it doesn't confirm the material can absorb visible light per se.

The "established earlier result" — presumably prior spectroscopic/optical work showing Ce/Er doping shifts TiO2 absorption into the visible range and narrows the band gap — establishes the PRF (a physical readiness/capability precondition) on its own, but without a functional test it doesn't show the shifted absorption translates into actual antibacterial performance.

Read together, the two results settle that the visible-light absorption capability (PRF) is causally linked to and sufficient for the achieved service_capability (rapid bacteria-killing) — i.e., the doping strategy's optical mechanism is functionally realized, not just spectroscopically present. Neither alone shows this coupling.

What remains open: mechanistic detail (ROS species, band-edge positions), durability/reusability of the catalyst, performance across bacterial strains/biofilms, in vivo or wound-model efficacy, and dopant-concentration optimization.
