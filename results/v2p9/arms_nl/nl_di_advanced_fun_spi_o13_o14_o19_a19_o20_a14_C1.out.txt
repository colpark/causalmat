This measurement (taken together with the earlier settled in vitro/in vivo pairing) justifies a modest but real functional claim: the photothermal conversion behavior demonstrated for BNs-BSA in solution (808 nm absorption driving concentration- and power-dependent heating) is not confined to the dish. When the same material is delivered into a living tumour and irradiated with 808 nm light, tissue temperature rises well above the no-material (PBS) control -- about 49 C versus 41 C at 5 min. So what is settled is qualitative and directional: the material's absorption-based heating mechanism is "exercised" in vivo, producing a real, measurable, above-baseline thermal effect at the tumour site under laser exposure. That is enough to support the general claim "this material heats the tumour more than no material does, when irradiated," which is the basic premise needed before claiming a photothermal therapeutic effect.

What it does not settle, and leaves open, is everything quantitative or mechanistic. There is no in vivo dose series and no in vivo power series -- only one concentration and one power level were tested -- so no dose-response or power-response curve exists in vivo, and the in vitro slopes/efficiency numbers (including the ~19.4% conversion efficiency) cannot be validated or transferred to the animal setting. The in vivo comparison also cannot separate intrinsic photothermal conversion from simple differential accumulation: BNs-BSA getting hotter than PBS could reflect the material's absorption physics, more material retained at the tumour, or both, and this pairing cannot distinguish those explanations. Finally, because tissue dissipates heat differently (perfusion, conduction) than solution, the 49 C/41 C readings are not numerically comparable to the solution spot-temperatures or the absorbance/laser-power slope -- these remain two different kinds of measurement in two different media, not a single unified dose-response story.
