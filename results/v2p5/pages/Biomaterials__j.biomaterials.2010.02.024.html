key — the combining proposition
Observation A alone establishes that there is an ionic-strength boundary near ~0.75 M potassium phosphate separating two macroscopic outcomes: gelation below the boundary and particle formation above it, but it does not say what physical form the 'particle' regime takes. Observation B alone shows that at high ionic strength the material appears in a light-microscopy inset as separate spheres ~1 um in size, but on its own does not establish that this shape is specific to, or bounded by, the ~0.75 M threshold rather than being a general feature of the preparation. Put together, the two let you assign the spherical ~1 um morphology specifically to the above-threshold (particle-forming) side of the ~0.75 M boundary identified by A: what forms above ~0.75 M is not merely 'particles' in a generic yield sense but discrete spheres of about 1 um, as imaged in B.
limits
  • B is a single inset at high ionic strength, not a survey across the ionic-strength range, so the pair does not establish shape/microstructure in the gel regime (below ~0.75 M) or how shape changes with distance from the threshold
  • The ~1 um figure is a visual read from one microscopy image, not a quantified size distribution, so the pair does not establish particle-size statistics or sphericity uniformity
  • A defines a bulk/macroscopic phase boundary (gel vs particle yield) while B is a local microstructural snapshot; the combination does not establish that the transition itself is sharp or that spheres form immediately at 0.75 M rather than somewhere further above it

key — the combining proposition
Putting the light-microscopy (optical) and SEM observations together links the two imaging scales for the same samples: the irregular, clustered particle morphology seen optically at pH 4 corresponds, at SEM resolution, to small spheres embedded in a web-like film network (panel c), while the discrete spheres seen optically at pH 6 and pH 9 correspond, at SEM resolution, to discrete, larger aggregated spheres with no interconnecting film (panels d and e). Neither image alone shows this correspondence -- optical only shows macro-scale clustering vs. discreteness, SEM only shows nano/micro-scale presence or absence of a connecting film -- but together they support that the pH 4 particles are bound into non-dispersible clusters by a film-like network, distinct from the pH 6/pH 9 particles.
limits
  • Does not establish the chemical or physical mechanism by which pH 4 produces the film network (no compositional or chemical data cited)
  • Cannot rule out that the web-like film seen by SEM is a sample-preparation/drying artifact rather than a feature present in the dispersed state
  • Based on a small number of representative images (panels F1b-e), not a quantitative measurement -- gives no count, size distribution, or fraction of particles in clusters
  • Optical images alone cannot resolve whether a film network is present; SEM images alone cannot confirm that the film corresponds to the macro-scale non-dispersible clustering seen optically -- each modality is needed to close the other's gap

key — the combining proposition
Together, the two panels are consistent with a charge-driven loading mechanism for the cationic dye alcian blue: as loading rises, zeta potential shifts from negative toward positive, crossing zero somewhere between 6.6% and 8.1% loading and reaching +43 mV, while loading efficiency for the same dye stays near 100% only up to input ratio 20 before falling to 38%. The loading range where the surface charge crosses from negative to positive overlaps with the loading range where efficiency begins to decline, so the pair together supports the idea that as bound drug accumulates and pushes the particle toward net-positive charge, the electrostatic driving force for further uptake weakens and efficiency drops.
limits
  • Observation A reports the zero-crossing and final charge (+43 mV) as a function of % loading, while Observation B reports efficiency as a function of input mass ratio (ratio 20, ratio 60); these are not the same measured axis, so the numeric overlap between the zero-crossing bracket (6.6-8.1%) and the ratio-20 efficiency threshold is only approximate, not a matched one-to-one comparison
  • The zero-crossing is bracketed by only two loading points (6.6% and 8.1%), too few to establish a continuous dose-response curve linking charge state to efficiency
  • The pair gives ensemble-average charge and ensemble-average efficiency, not a per-particle link between an individual particle's charge state and whether it continues to bind drug
  • Crystal violet's own zeta-potential trajectory is not given here, so the alcian blue charge/efficiency correspondence cannot be cross-checked against the other cationic dye in this pair

key — the combining proposition
Taken together, the two observations are consistent with a diffusion-and-saturation picture: Observation A shows that a small-molecule drug (rhodamine B) distributes uniformly through the ~1-2 um particle interior rather than concentrating at the surface, i.e. it enters the bulk protein matrix; Observation B shows that loading efficiency for cationic drugs stays near 100% up to a certain drug:protein ratio (60 for crystal violet, 20 for alcian blue) and then declines. Combined, this pattern -- uniform interior filling plus a threshold beyond which efficiency drops -- is compatible with drug diffusing into the matrix until the matrix's capacity is used up, after which added drug is no longer taken up as efficiently.
limits
  • Observation A visualizes only rhodamine B, while Observation B's efficiency/ratio curve is measured for crystal violet and alcian blue -- different molecules, so the uniform-diffusion pattern seen with rhodamine B is not directly demonstrated for the drugs whose saturation behavior is quantified
  • Observation A appears to be a single-condition (single loading level) image, not a series across the ratio range (up to 60 or 20) where Observation B's efficiency decline occurs, so it is not shown whether the interior remains uniformly filled (versus developing a rim) at the higher ratios where saturation is seen
  • the decline in loading efficiency in B could in principle arise from a different limiting step (e.g. surface/electrostatic saturation) rather than the bulk matrix filling completely, and A alone cannot rule this out because it was not imaged at the saturating ratios
not identifiablewhether the efficiency drop in Observation B reflects the protein matrix's interior capacity being filled (as suggested by A's uniform diffusion pattern) versus some other loading-limiting mechanism (e.g. surface or charge saturation) cannot be separated, since A and B were not measured on the same drug or across the same ratio range
