Figure 2b (Al 2p high-resolution XPS spectrum) shows two fitted components: a large, dominant peak centered at about 78.3 eV labeled "Al" (metallic aluminum, intensity ~72 counts) and a smaller, broader shoulder peak at about 75.5 eV labeled "Al2O3" (aluminum oxide, intensity ~17 counts). The metallic Al signal is far more intense than the oxide signal, indicating that the surface oxide/hydroxide layer on the nanoparticle is thin and only partially covers the particle, since the underlying metallic aluminum core is still strongly detected by XPS (a surface-sensitive technique) rather than being fully attenuated by a thick, continuous oxide shell.

This is consistent with, and supports, the claim: it demonstrates that native Al nanoparticles carry only a thin, incomplete oxide layer leaving substantial exposed metallic Al, which explains why they are prone to self-corrosion and aggregation and motivates the need for additional protective conversion coatings (Ce oxide/hydroxide, APTES) described in the paper. The panel itself does not directly show the "very thin and non-uniform" character of the Ce-specific conversion film mentioned in the claim (this spectrum appears to characterize the native/untreated Al 2p oxidation state rather than a Ce-treated sample), so it corroborates the general vulnerability narrative but is not direct evidence about the Ce layer specifically.
