Candidate answer:
Panel b shows two stacked plots from a scanning tunneling microscope (STM) break-junction / molecular junction conductance measurement on a dodecanethiol self-assembled monolayer (structure HS-(CH2)10-CH3 shown in inset).

Top plot: Tunneling current (A, log scale, 10^-12 to 10^-6) vs. Voltage (0 to 3.0 V). It overlays ~100 repeated I-V sweeps (Run 1 through Run 100), color-coded from dark blue (Run 1) to orange (Run 100), with a clear systematic upward drift in current at a given voltage as run number increases — i.e., the current-voltage curves progressively shift to higher current over repeated cycling, with the spread between runs widening most at higher voltages (near 10^-6 A ceiling for later runs vs ~10^-8 A for Run 1). Two dashed reference curves are overlaid: a red dashed "Simulation (variable gap)" line and a gray dashed "Simulation (constant gap)" line, both of which track closely with the low-current (early-run) data. Small arrows on the Run 1 curve indicate forward/reverse voltage sweep direction (hysteresis loop), showing the up-sweep and down-sweep traces are nearly coincident.

Bottom plot: Relative Gap (G0-normalized junction gap, y-axis ~0.6–1.05) vs. Voltage (0–3.0 V), showing extracted junction gap distance staying essentially flat at ~1 G0 (red dashed reference line at 1 G0) across the full voltage range, with only a slight upward tick near 2.5–3.0 V and some scatter/outlier points below.

No scale bar is present (this is a graph, not a micrograph). The overall trend conveyed is that repeated voltage cycling causes a gradual, systematic increase in tunneling current (attributed to a changing junction structure/gap), while the extracted relative gap stays close to constant (~1 G0) until higher voltages, and simulations assuming a variable vs. constant gap are compared against the measured low-cycle data.
