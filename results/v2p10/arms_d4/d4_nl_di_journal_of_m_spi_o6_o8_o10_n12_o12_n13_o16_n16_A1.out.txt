Panel F6a (Mg3.2Bi1.4Sb0.6-xSex, ZT vs T, 300-625 K) confirms the cited 300 K values reasonably well: reading the curves at the left edge, x=0.005 (black squares) sits near ~0.85, x=0.01 (red circles) near ~0.78-0.8, x=0.02/0.04 (blue/magenta) cluster around 0.63-0.7, and the dashed n-Bi2Te3 reference curve [ref 8] sits near ~0.85-0.9 — all consistent with the stated ~0.84, ~0.78, 0.63-0.69, and ~0.88 figures.

What this supports: at room temperature, the lightly Se-doped composition (x=0.005) achieves a ZT essentially matching the benchmark n-Bi2Te3 reference curve, and the trend among doped samples at 300 K is monotonically decreasing with increasing Se content (x=0.005 > 0.01 > 0.02 ≈ 0.04). It also supports that undoped x=0 is far worse (ZT ~0.2 across the whole range), so Se-doping is clearly beneficial versus the undoped baseline. This is a legitimate, panel-supported claim about the material's near-room-temperature figure of merit relative to a standard commercial thermoelectric.

What it leaves open: the 300 K comparison is not representative of overall performance — the panel shows all doped compositions converge and rise sharply above 400 K, peaking around 450-525 K at ZT ~1.2-1.3, well above the n-Bi2Te3 reference (~1.0 peak), with x=0.01 (not x=0.005) becoming the best performer at those higher temperatures. So the 300 K figures alone do not establish which composition is optimal overall, nor do they capture the material's actual peak/average-ZT advantage, which is the more relevant metric for thermoelectric device performance across its full operating range.
