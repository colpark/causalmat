This measurement settles that the phase-identified bismuthene material (majority hexagonal Bi with a minor Bi2O3 trace) does not just absorb 808 nm NIR light proportionally to concentration (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1) but actually converts that absorbed light into heat at a defined, measurable rate: a photothermal conversion efficiency of eta = 19.4% with a characteristic time constant tau_s = 133.4 s. Combined, these two results support the conclusion that this material is a genuine photothermal agent -- it both captures 808 nm light in a linear, concentration-dependent way and transduces that captured energy into heat -- closing the earlier gap where alpha alone was only an absorbance/extinction property with no established link to energy conversion.

What it leaves open: (1) which phase is responsible -- the majority Bi or the minor Bi2O3 -- for either the absorption or the 19.4% efficiency, and whether Bi/Bi2O3 form a heterojunction or exist as separate phases, remains unresolved; (2) no morphology or size (nanoflake/nanosheet dimension) contribution to absorption or photothermal behavior is established; (3) the amount of Bi2O3 present is unquantified, so its possible contribution to the 19.4% efficiency is unbounded, not disproven; (4) the tau_s/eta fit is a single measurement at one concentration and laser power, so it does not show how efficiency or heating scale with concentration or power density; and (5) the two properties are shown to coexist in the same material, not to be quantitatively linked -- there is no evidence that alpha predicts or determines eta. In short: the material's photothermal functionality is now demonstrated, but its structural origin, composition dependence, and scaling behavior remain undetermined.
