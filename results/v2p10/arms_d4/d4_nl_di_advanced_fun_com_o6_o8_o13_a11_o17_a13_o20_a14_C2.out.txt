This measurement justifies the conclusion that the phase-identified bismuthene material (majority hexagonal Bi with a minor Bi2O3 trace) does not just absorb 808 nm NIR light in a linear, concentration-dependent way (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1) but actually functions as a working photothermal agent: it converts absorbed light into heat at a defined efficiency (eta = 19.4%) with a characteristic heating time constant (tau_s = 133.4 s). In service-capability terms, this settles that the material possesses genuine photothermal-conversion functionality, not merely optical absorption -- the specific gap flagged after the alpha measurement alone (that absorbance is not evidence of energy conversion) is now closed by pairing it with an independent conversion-mechanism measurement on the same sample.

What it leaves open is substantial. It does not identify whether the majority Bi phase, the minor Bi2O3 phase, or some heterojunction between them is responsible for either the absorption or the 19.4% efficiency -- the Bi/Bi2O3 spatial arrangement remains undetermined. It does not attribute any role to morphology or particle/flake size. It does not quantify the Bi2O3 fraction, so its possible contribution to the measured efficiency is unbounded rather than ruled out. Critically, this is a single-point measurement: it shows conversion occurs at one concentration and one laser power density, but says nothing about how eta or heating scales with concentration or irradiance -- necessary for any real service/dosing claim. Finally, coexistence of alpha and eta in the same material is shown, but not a quantitative link between them (alpha does not predict eta). So the capability is demonstrated qualitatively, not characterized quantitatively or mechanistically.
