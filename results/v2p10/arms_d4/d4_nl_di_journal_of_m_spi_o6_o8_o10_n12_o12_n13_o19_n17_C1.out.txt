This measurement, taken together with n12, supports the conclusion that the power-factor peak seen at x=0.01 (~29 at 300 K, ~22 at 623 K), which clearly exceeds both x=0.005 and the undoped x=0 sample (PF at most ~5), is consistent with and helps explain the favorable low-resistivity, carrier-concentration-driven regime that n12 established for Se-doped samples — i.e., the PF rise is not an artifact of resistivity moving the wrong way, and it is not contradicted by a mobility penalty from doping.

What this justifies: doping-induced changes in carrier concentration and resistivity, as characterized in n12, are compatible with and plausibly contribute to the strong PF enhancement observed near x=0.01 relative to the undoped material.

What it leaves open: (1) n12's mobility data span x=0.005–0.02 and x=0.04 but not x=0.01 itself, so the PF peak cannot be pinned to a directly measured mobility at that exact composition; (2) PF depends on the Seebeck coefficient S, which n12 does not address, so it cannot be determined whether the PF peak reflects the resistivity/mobility story, a favorable S at x=0.01, or both; (3) n12's mobility comparison uses a heterogeneous literature set, so any apparent mobility preservation could reflect differences in sample quality/synthesis rather than the Se-doping mechanism itself; (4) the microscopic mechanism (Se substitution on the Sb site) is not established; (5) the rho/mobility trend from n12 is not confirmed across the full 300–623 K range over which PF is reported, so temperature-dependence of the PF cannot be tied to it; and (6) only a few common doping points exist, supporting an ordering across x rather than a fitted quantitative relationship among n_H, mu_H, rho, and PF.</message>
