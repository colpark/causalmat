The earlier established result is that Mg3Bi2-based (Mg3Bi2-xSbx) n-type alloys already reach respectable ZT values, but mainly in the moderate-to-elevated temperature range (roughly 350–550 K), because at room temperature these materials typically suffer from low carrier mobility, bipolar conduction, and modest power factor, giving poor ZT near 300 K on its own. That earlier result, by itself, only tells you the material class is thermoelectrically promising at higher operating temperatures — it says nothing about room-temperature usability.

The new Se-doping paper's measurement, taken alone, shows that a specific Se-doped composition reaches a high ZT/figure of merit specifically at ~300 K — but a single new data point alone doesn't tell you whether this is a genuine extension of the material system's performance range or an isolated, composition-specific effect.

Read together, the two results settle that Mg3Bi2-based n-type thermoelectrics can deliver competitive figure-of-merit performance across a broadened temperature window — from room temperature (via Se doping, likely through optimized carrier concentration and suppressed bipolar effects) up through the previously established mid/high-temperature regime. This supports the case for using this material family in near-ambient thermoelectric cooling/power applications, not just high-temperature waste-heat recovery.

What remains open: the two measurements don't establish that a single sample/composition achieves high ZT across the entire range simultaneously (the room-temperature optimum and the higher-temperature optimum may require different doping levels), nor do they address long-term stability, reproducibility across labs, contact/device-level efficiency, or the precise microscopic mechanism (band convergence vs. point-defect scattering vs. carrier tuning) responsible for the room-temperature gain.
