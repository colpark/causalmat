Panel F2d (viewed) shows a severe, rapid capacity fade for the Na/PCO cell at 0.1C: ~870 mAh/g (cycle 1) drops to ~480 (cycle 2) and continues falling to ~70 mAh/g by cycle 40, while Coulombic efficiency rises from ~55% to ~95% over the same cycles — i.e., large first-cycle irreversible loss followed by continuous, near-total capacity decay despite improving efficiency.

Read together with the earlier settled result (retained crystalline PCO phase, Co not reduced to metallic state, invariant SAXS pore-scale scattering, and comparatively low/early-saturating simulated oxidation degree for Na-Co3O4 vs. Li-Co3O4 and Na-Co3S4), this combination supports the conclusion that the material's poor cycling/service capability is not explained by gross conversion-driven structural collapse or pore-scale pulverization — since bulk crystallinity and pore microstructure were shown to persist through sodiation, and the oxidation degree is intrinsically limited. In other words, the capacity fade cannot be attributed to the oxide breaking down structurally at the pore or crystal scale; something else must be capping and eroding the usable capacity across cycles.

What this leaves open, and neither result settles alone, is what actually drives the fade: the two observations are consistent with (but do not demonstrate) fade arising from limited intrinsic sodiation reactivity capping capacity from the start, compounded by other factors such as volume change, SEI growth, contact loss, or irreversible Na trapping — the same qualification already on record (poor sodiation activity vs. volume change as cause of fading remains undecided). The picosecond-scale MD oxidation metric also still cannot be equated with cycle-scale electrochemical degradation, so no causal, quantitative link between oxidation degree and the observed capacity trajectory is established.
