This combined result settles that the reduced material is not just a strong 808 nm absorber but functionally converts that absorbed light into heat under irradiation, with the heating tracking absorption-predicted dose-dependence: it rises with nanoparticle concentration (200>100>50>25 ug/mL) and with laser power density (0.5→1.5 W at fixed 200 ug/mL), while water alone stays cold, ruling out the medium itself as the heat source. So for PRF/service_capability, this justifies the claim "material exhibits NIR-triggered photothermal heating that is concentration- and power-dependent" -- i.e., it demonstrates a functional service capability (photothermal actuation) building on the earlier purely optical (absorption) characterization, and shows the capability is a real, controllable, dose-responsive effect rather than an artifact.

What it leaves open: (1) attribution -- because IR thermography is a bulk/ensemble reading, not spatially resolved to the TEM-imaged particles, it cannot confirm the Bi-like nanoparticles specifically (rather than unreduced substrate or residual oxide) are the actual heat source; (2) magnitude/efficiency -- no photothermal conversion efficiency or heat-to-light conversion parameter is derived, only that a temperature rise occurs and scales in the expected direction; (3) quantitative linkage -- the normalized alpha (extinction coefficient) from optical data is not verified or derived from the absolute 6-min endpoint temperatures, so the connection is a qualitative dose-response consistency check, not a quantitative confirmation; (4) conversion yield -- the fraction of sheet converted to nanoparticles, and whether residual oxide contributes to or masks heating, remains unquantified; (5) functional form -- only two power points and four concentration points establish ordering/direction, not a precise functional relationship. In short: capability demonstrated qualitatively, mechanism and magnitude unresolved.
