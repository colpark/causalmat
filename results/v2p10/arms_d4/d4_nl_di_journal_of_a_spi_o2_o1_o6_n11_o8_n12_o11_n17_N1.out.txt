Best-guess reading (no measurement/figure/earlier result was actually supplied, so this is inference from the title and typical TBC literature):

The paper almost certainly shows that Al2O3-YSZ composite coatings undergo less microstructural degradation during thermal cycling (slower sintering-driven densification, fewer coalesced microcracks, more stable splat/pore architecture) than plain YSZ, and that this correlates with a measurably longer thermal cycle life (more cycles to spallation) for the composite coatings.

Read alone, that only establishes a correlation for one comparison: microstructure stability differs between the two coating types, and life differs between the two coating types, in the same paper's dataset. It does not by itself prove which specific microstructural feature is causally responsible for the service-life gain, nor does it establish that the effect generalizes.

If this is combined with an "established earlier result" that already links microstructural stability (resistance to sintering/crack coalescence) mechanistically to service capability (durability) in TBCs generally, then the combination settles something the single paper cannot: it converts a bare correlation in one system into an instance confirming a known causal pathway — i.e., PRF (the process-result finding that microstructure evolution governs service capability) is now supported both generically and in this specific Al2O3-YSZ vs YSZ case, strengthening confidence that Al2O3 addition improves service life via the same stabilization mechanism, not by coincidence.

What remains open: whether the benefit holds at other temperatures, thicknesses, Al2O3 fractions, or deposition methods, and whether microstructure stability is the dominant cause versus a contributing factor alongside phase stability, thermal expansion mismatch, or bond-coat oxidation effects.
