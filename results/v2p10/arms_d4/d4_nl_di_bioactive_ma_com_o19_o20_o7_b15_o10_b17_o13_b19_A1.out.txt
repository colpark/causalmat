Both panels support the claim. In F10a (S. aureus) and F10b (E. coli), the light-irradiation row shows a clear composition-dependent gradient: Control and plain TiO2 plates carry many colonies, Er0.5Ce0.1Ti-O is intermediate, Er0.5Ce0.2Ti-O visibly has the fewest, most sparsely scattered colonies of any light plate for both bacterial strains, and Er0.5Ce0.3Ti-O rebounds to a somewhat higher (but still reduced versus control) colony count. In the dark row, all plates remain heavily covered with confluent bacterial streaks/colonies, including the doped samples, though the doped dark plates do look somewhat less densely smeared than the Control/TiO2 dark plates, consistent with "sparser but still covered."

What this settles: it supports that Er0.5Ce0.2Ti-O is the best-performing composition among those tested for light-activated antibacterial service capability, effective against both a gram-positive (S. aureus) and a gram-negative (E. coli) organism, and that this photocatalytic killing capability is light-dependent — the same material shows little advantage in the dark.

What it leaves open: the plates give only a qualitative/visual impression, not colony-forming-unit counts, replicate numbers, or statistics, so the magnitude and significance of the Ce0.2 advantage over Ce0.1/Ce0.3 can't be confirmed quantitatively from the image alone. It also doesn't establish the mechanism (ROS vs. ion release), performance at other doping levels, exposure times, or durability/biocompatibility needed to generalize "service capability" beyond this single 20-minute plate assay.
