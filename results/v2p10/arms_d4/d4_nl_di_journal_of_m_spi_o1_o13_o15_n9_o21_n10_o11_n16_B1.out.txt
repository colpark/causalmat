F2d (high-mag SEM of the tensile fracture surface) shows a dimpled, lamellar fracture morphology in which small holes (red arrows) and microcracks (red boxes) are concentrated specifically at lamella ends and edges — i.e., at the boundaries between the two lamella types — while the dimple interiors between boundaries appear comparatively featureless.

Read together with the earlier TEM pairing (pile-up dislocation tangle at a lamella boundary vs. orderly straight parallel dislocations along a slip trace elsewhere), this combination supports a link between the two prior observations and final damage: the location that shows anomalous, tangled/pile-up dislocation activity (the lamella boundary) is also the location where the fracture surface preferentially shows void/microcrack initiation, whereas lamella interiors, where slip appeared more homogeneous (straight parallel lines), do not show the same clustering of fracture-initiating features. Together this strengthens the case that lamella boundaries are a mechanistically distinct, damage-prone site in this microstructure — not just a site of different dislocation arrangement, but one plausibly tied to void/crack nucleation — beyond what either the TEM pairing or the fracture image shows alone.

What it still leaves open: F2d is a post-mortem fracture surface, not diffraction-indexed, so it cannot say which phase (L12 or B2) the cracking sites correspond to, nor can it confirm the imaged pile-up band is the same boundary type or same strain state as the boundaries cracking here. It also gives no quantitative count linking pile-up density to hole/microcrack density, and cannot rule out that the boundary geometry itself (not phase-specific slip behavior) drives both the pile-up and the cracking.
