This measurement (Observations A and B read together) settles a qualitative photothermal claim: the phase-identified bismuthene material's strong, linear, concentration-proportional NIR extinction at 808 nm (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1, established by A) is the property responsible for the concentration- and power-dependent heating seen at irradiated spots (established by B, with water as a non-heating control ruling out a trivial artifact). It supports the conclusion that this material converts absorbed 808 nm light into heat — i.e., a genuine, mechanistically-linked photothermal effect — because the same directional trend (more material or more incident power leads to more absorption and more heat) connects the two independent data sets, and the control rules out the heating being some artifact unrelated to the sample's optical absorption.

What it leaves open: (1) no quantitative photothermal conversion efficiency or numeric heat-conversion value is established — alpha is only an absorbance/concentration slope and the thermal data are single-timepoint (6 min) spot temperatures, not an energy-balance calculation; (2) it does not identify which phase drives the heating — since the spatial arrangement and relative contribution of the majority Bi phase versus the minor Bi2O3 phase to absorption were never resolved, the heating cannot be attributed to Bi alone versus partial Bi2O3 contribution; (3) no quantitative or curve-fit relationship between alpha and temperature rise is shown, since the concentration and power sweeps were separate one-variable experiments, not a full factorial — only ordinal trends are supported; and (4) morphology/size effects, photostability, and sustained or device-level, in-use performance remain untested, as B reflects only a single 6-minute laboratory snapshot.</message>
