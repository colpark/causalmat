Panel F5d (viewed) shows six treatment-group tumor-growth curves. In the "BNs-BSA+808 nm" panel, all five tumors are suppressed near zero through ~day 10-14, but four of the five curves then turn upward and regrow (one reaching ~700 mm3, others ~250-450 mm3 by day 20). In the "BNs-BSA+Lasers" (combined 660/808 nm) panel, all five curves stay flat at ~0 for the full ~20-day observation.

Read together with the established in vitro result (alpha=6.4 L g-1 cm-1 at 808 nm; photothermal fit tau_s=133.4 s, eta=19.4%), this justifies a new claim neither piece supports alone: the material's demonstrated photothermal conversion has a real, measurable in vivo consequence — 808 nm irradiation of BNs-BSA does suppress tumor growth for an initial window, showing the lab-measured heat conversion translates into actual biological "service capability" (tumor control), not just heat in a cuvette. That closes the gap between "converts light to heat" and "does something therapeutically."

But the combination leaves open exactly what the in vitro photothermal numbers cannot: why 4/5 tumors escape and regrow after day ~14 under 808 nm alone (insufficient heat dose at tumor depth, incomplete coverage, thermal resistance/recurrence of surviving cells, single-wavelength limitation) is not explained by eta or tau_s alone. It also does not establish whether the 660 nm component's full suppression reflects an independent (photodynamic or complementary) mechanism versus simply added photothermal dose — the in vitro measurement was 808 nm-specific and says nothing about 660 nm's mode of action. So durability/completeness of tumor ablation and the mechanistic role of the second wavelength remain unresolved.
