This measurement supports the conclusion that the Bi/Bi2O3 composite's concentration-proportional 808 nm NIR absorbance (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1) is functionally linked to its photothermal behavior: under irradiation, spot temperature rises track concentration in the same rank order (200 > 100 > 50 > 25 ug/mL) and also rise with laser power density (0.5 to 1.5 W) at fixed concentration, while the water control stays cold. Together these justify saying the material is a genuine photothermal agent whose heating output scales with how much of it is present and with how much light energy is delivered, and that the heat generation is a property of the composite itself rather than of the solvent.

What it leaves open, plainly: it does not identify which component -- metallic Bi, Bi2O3, or their interface -- is actually converting absorbed photons into heat, so the phase-attribution gap from the absorption measurement carries straight through to the photothermal stage unresolved. It does not yield a quantitative photothermal conversion efficiency; matching qualitative trends in temperature rise with concentration and power give no basis for computing a numeric efficiency value. It also does not mechanistically prove that absorption causes the heating, since the two measurements are different kinds of quantities -- a rate-like optical coefficient versus a cumulative 6-minute endpoint temperature -- and parallel trends alone are consistent with, but do not demonstrate, a direct causal link. Finally, as with the earlier result, everything here remains a bulk/ensemble correlation, not evidence tied spatially or quantitatively to any single phase or particle.
