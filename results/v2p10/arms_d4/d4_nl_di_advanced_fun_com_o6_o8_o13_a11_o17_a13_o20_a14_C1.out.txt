This measurement justifies the conclusion that the phase-identified bismuthene material (majority hexagonal Bi with a minor Bi2O3 trace) is not just a passive NIR absorber but an active photothermal converter: its linear, concentration-proportional absorbance at 808 nm (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1) coexists with a demonstrated light-to-heat conversion process, quantified by a photothermal conversion efficiency of 19.4% and a characteristic heating time constant (tau_s) of 133.4 s. Together these close the earlier gap that alpha alone was only an optical extinction property, not evidence of energy conversion — this material does convert absorbed 808 nm light into measurable heat at a defined efficiency and rate.

What it leaves open: it does not identify which phase — the majority hexagonal Bi or the minor Bi2O3 trace — is actually responsible for the absorption or for the 19.4% conversion efficiency, nor does it resolve whether Bi and Bi2O3 form a heterojunction or exist as separate, unconnected phases. It does not establish any role for nanoflake/nanosheet morphology or particle size in either absorption or photothermal behavior. It does not quantify how much Bi2O3 is present, so its possible contribution to the 19.4% efficiency remains unbounded rather than ruled out. The tau_s/eta fit is a single measurement at one condition, so it does not show how efficiency or heating scales with concentration or laser power density. Finally, the pairing only shows the two properties coexist in the same material — it does not demonstrate a quantitative link between them, i.e., it does not show that alpha predicts or determines eta.
