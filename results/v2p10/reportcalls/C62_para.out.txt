Step 1 combines two panels on the same set of samples: the UVVIS data show co-doped TiO2 absorbs visible light further into the spectrum (past 420 nm, Ce0.2 strongest) than plain TiO2 (~410 nm edge), and the catalysis assay shows co-doped samples degrade dye faster than TiO2 (Er0.5Ce0.2 ~46% > Ce0.3 ~41% > Ce0.1 ~31% > TiO2 ~20%), with that ordering holding at 20, 40, and 60 min. Together these support a link between broader visible-light absorption and better degradation in co-doped samples versus TiO2.

Step 2 needed that established ordering as a baseline to test against, because the new antibacterial measurement (Er0.5Ce0.2 ~91-93% > Ce0.3 ~87% > Ce0.1 ~68-73% > TiO2 ~45% > control ~4-7%) only becomes informative when compared to the prior composition ranking. Since the same ordering reappears - co-doped beating TiO2, Er0.5Ce0.2 on top - the step concludes the co-doping advantage generalizes to a second functional endpoint, not just dye degradation. It still flags: no measured mechanism ties absorption to either outcome; the strongest absorber (Ce0.2) isn't the top performer on either test; only 4-5 compositions were compared; and the two endpoints differ enough that a shared cause versus coincidence can't be distinguished.
