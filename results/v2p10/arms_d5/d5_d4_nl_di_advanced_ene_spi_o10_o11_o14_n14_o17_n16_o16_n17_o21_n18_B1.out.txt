The panel (F4g) is a single photograph: a transparent battery stack (captioned "Zn-MnO2 battery stack with three cells-in-series") wired to a multimeter, connected to and apparently powering a switched-on smartphone. On its own this photo only demonstrates that a multi-cell stack can deliver enough voltage/current to run a real consumer device — a qualitative, one-time proof-of-concept of practical service capability, with no chemistry label, no duration, and no failure point recorded.

Read together with the established symmetric-cycling result (CnC HS stable to ~7100 min vs. C HS developing spikes from ~2600 min and failing near 4400 min), the combination supports a modest bridging claim: the paper is using the same platform not just to show a lab-scale voltage-stability metric but to argue that stability of that kind underwrites a demonstrably usable device-level output (powering a phone). That is, it supports moving from "one sample outlasts the other in symmetric-cell cycling" toward "cells built on this chemistry are advertised as capable of real practical service," strengthening the plausibility that the stability advantage matters for actual use, not just an artifact of the symmetric-cell test geometry.

What it leaves open: the photo's caption does not identify whether the pictured stack uses CnC HS, C HS, or another formulation, so the two results cannot be directly linked to the same sample. It gives no duration, cycle count, or failure time for the phone-powering demonstration, so no quantitative or even ordinal comparison to the 4400/7100-minute figures is possible. A three-cell series stack also differs in voltage/current regime from the two-electrode symmetric cells, so the geometries aren't equivalent. This remains an illustrative capability demonstration, not evidence tying the specific stability-vs-failure divergence to real-device performance.
