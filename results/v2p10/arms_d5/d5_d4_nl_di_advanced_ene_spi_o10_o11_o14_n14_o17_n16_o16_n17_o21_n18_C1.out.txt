This measurement settles that the same two-sample comparison already distinguished by chemistry/morphology and rate-dependent overpotential (CnC HS vs. C HS) also separates cleanly on long-term cycling durability: CnC HS remains stable (within ~0.03 V) out to 7100 minutes, whereas C HS begins developing voltage spikes around 2600 minutes and fails near 4400 minutes. What this supports is a consistent ordering across three independent axes of evidence — nitrogen-linked chemical state, zinc morphology (flatter/sparser vs. dense-platelet), plating overpotential gap, and cycling lifetime — all pointing the same direction: CnC HS is the better-performing material by every metric measured, and C HS is worse on all of them. That convergence is meaningful as a coherent, non-contradictory picture of one material outperforming the other.

What it leaves open is causation and mechanism. Because all these properties co-occur in the same two samples by construction, this cannot establish that the nitrogen chemistry, the morphology, or the overpotential behavior is what causes the earlier spike onset and failure in C HS or the stability of CnC HS — some other unmeasured difference between the hosts could be responsible. It also cannot support any quantitative or dose-response relationship, since the two sides are different kinds of measurements (a rate-dependent voltage-gap difference-of-differences versus a cumulative time-to-failure trace). With only two samples, the result supports ordering/ranking only, not a generalizable trend across a material family. Finally, it inherits the earlier caveats that the spectral peak assignments (pyridinic/graphitic, Zn-N) are the paper's interpretive labels, and neither the spectral shift nor the morphology difference was quantified.
