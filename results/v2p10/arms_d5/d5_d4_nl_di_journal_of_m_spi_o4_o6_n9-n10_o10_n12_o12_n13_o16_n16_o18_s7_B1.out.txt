The bar chart (F6b) shows this work's RT ZT (0.82) for the composition Mg3.2Bi1.4Sb0.595Se0.005 — i.e., the x=0.005 sample, the same composition the earlier result identified as the internal ZT optimum (not the PF/resistivity optimum at x=0.01). This external comparison shows that composition outperforming four literature benchmarks (Refs 24, 22, 39, 40: 0.75, 0.70, 0.62, 0.32).

Read together, the two results support this conclusion: the composition where the ZT optimum was found within this study's own series (x=0.005, decoupled from the PF/resistivity optimum at x=0.01) is not merely a local best-of-a-small-set artifact — it is also competitive with, and superior to, the best reported values for related Mg3Sb2-based materials in the field. This corroborates that whatever unmeasured quantity (presumably thermal conductivity or a Seebeck effect) pulls the ZT optimum back to x=0.005 away from the PF/resistivity optimum is producing a genuine, externally benchmarked performance gain, not just an artifact of the four-to-five-point internal composition sweep.

What this combination leaves open: it still does not identify the mechanism — no kappa or Seebeck values are given here either, so the physical reason x=0.005 beats x=0.01 (and beats the literature) remains unresolved. It is a room-temperature-only comparison, so nothing is said about whether this composition's advantage over the literature persists at higher temperature, where the earlier PF data (623 K) already showed an unmatched temperature gap. It also does not establish whether the four reference materials were measured under comparable conditions/synthesis routes, so the comparison licenses a ranking claim but not a controlled isolation of the causal factor. Finally, it says nothing about how x=0.01 would rank against the same literature set, so it cannot quantify how much performance was "saved" by the ZT optimum sitting at x=0.005 rather than x=0.01.
