THE ANSWER
This combination supports a limited associative claim, not a mechanistic one. What it settles: at the two samples common to both the PL series (TiO2, Er0.5Ti-O, Er0.5Ce0.2) and the degradation series (Er0.5Ce0.2, Ce0.3, Ce0.1, TiO2) — namely TiO2 and Er0.5Ce0.2 — the direction of change is consistent. Er0.5Ce0.2 shows the most suppressed 390-420 nm PL (interpreted as reduced electron-hole recombination) and the highest degradation activity (~46% at 60 min, same ranking at 20/40 min); TiO2 shows the strongest, least-suppressed PL and the lowest degradation activity (~20%). Together this licenses saying that, at these two endpoints, reduced PL co-occurs with enhanced degradation — a conclusion neither series alone supports, since the PL work never measured degradation and the degradation work never measured PL.

What it leaves open is substantial. It is a two-point correspondence, not a dose-response trend: three of the five distinct samples across the two series lack a matching measurement in the other series, so the middle of the degradation ordering (Ce0.3 > Ce0.1) has no PL counterpart, and Er0.5Ti-O's PL value has no degradation counterpart. Ce's oxidation state remains undetermined, so the Ce0.3 > Ce0.1 > TiO2 ordering (no Er involved) cannot be tied to any confirmed Ce mechanism. Whether Er3+ is lattice-incorporated or merely surface-adsorbed is still unresolved, so even at the two matching points the combination cannot attribute the degradation gain to Er3+ altering band/electronic structure versus some other surface effect. Finally, this is only a co-occurring trend, not demonstrated causation between reduced recombination and photocatalytic performance.
