THE ANSWER
This measurement supports the conclusion that the Bi/Bi2O3 composite material itself — the same bulk material whose 808 nm absorbance was already shown to scale linearly and proportionally with concentration (alpha = 6.4 L g-1 cm-1) — is responsible for the observed photothermal heating, rather than the heating being an artifact of the solvent or measurement setup. This is justified because the IR imaging shows spot temperature rises after 6 minutes tracking the same concentration ordering (200 > 100 > 50 > 25 ug/mL) that governs absorbance, and additionally rising with laser power density (0.5 to 1.5 W) at fixed concentration, while a water-only control stays cold. Together these convergent trends make it reasonable to say the material's proportional NIR absorption is what drives its proportional photothermal output, and that the effect is concentration- and power-dependent as expected for a genuine photothermal agent.

What this leaves open: it does not identify which phase (metallic Bi, Bi2O3, or the interface between them) is actually converting absorbed light to heat -- the same phase-attribution gap present at the absorption stage recurs unresolved at the heating stage. It does not yield a quantitative photothermal conversion efficiency; only qualitative scaling with concentration and power is established. It also does not mechanistically prove absorption causes the heating -- alpha is a rate-like optical proportionality constant while the temperature rise is a cumulative endpoint after fixed time, so matching trends are consistent with, but not proof of, a causal link. Finally, all of this remains a bulk-level, correlational finding, not spatially resolved to any specific phase or particle.
