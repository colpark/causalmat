By utilizing photoactive naturally derived tetrakislawsone as a cathode, a light-assisted delithiation process and charging of the battery is accomplished. - Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Here, we used an organic molecule, tetrakislawsone (TKL), derived from a henna leaf as a single electrode material that serves both photoactive and Li-ion storing capabilities. The lithiated tetrakislawsone (LiTKL) is a tetramer of lawsone (2- hydroxy-1,4-naphthoquinone) and derived from the henna leaf, a commonly used natural dye for skin, hair, and textiles. We previously reported bislawsone, a dimer of lawsone, as a cathode material for Li-ion battery applications. Bislawsone displayed $125\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ of discharge capacity at $20\mathrm{mA}g^{-1}$ with a voltage plateau at $2.1{-}2.5\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ .15 Moreover, the tetramer of lawsone displayed 227 mAh $\mathbf{g}^{-1}$ at $25~\mathrm{mA~g}^{-1}$ with superior cycle stability compared to bilawsone.16 Lawsonebased materials are semiconductors with a band gap of $3.81~\mathrm{eV}$ which have been used as dyes for DSSC.17,18 Herein, we demonstrate lithiated tetrakislawsone both as a photoactive and Li-ion storage material to construct photorechargeable organic batteries (Figure 1). LiTKL electrodes, capable of absorbing light, produce pairs of electrons and holes. Using a single material with both storage and harvesting functionalities can eliminate the inefficient charge transfer occuring across the interface of light-harvesting materials and charge storage materials. Additionally, a photocharging effect is promoted by using a tetramer of lawsone with high crystalline nature. The long-range order of TKL (compared to lawsone) can promote delocalization of the charge carriers within the materials which leads to the increase in charging effect. The produced electrons, collected by current collectors, reduces anodes, while holes repel Li and promote the delithiation and charging process. This photoassisted charging effect was confirmed with an increase in charging current during the potentiostatic measurement and an increase in discharge capacity under light irradiation due to the competing charging process. Herein, we unveil the charge harvesting capabilities of the redox active organic materials, which in turn paves the way for development of sustainable and efficient photocharging batteries.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Optical Properties of TKL and Li-TKL. The primary process in photocharging is the effective absorption of the light and conversion of this energy to an electron−hole pair, followed by a movement of the photogenerated electrons into metal current collectors. To facilitate an easy path for this process, the absorption properties and band alignments of the  TKL cathode were studied using UV−vis spectroscopy and cyclic voltammetry (CV) as summarized in Figure 2. The nature and strength of lithium binding in Li-TKL and $\mathrm{LiCoO}_{2}$ is compared using DFT methods followed by Bader’s QTAIM analysis (Supporting Information, Figure S1).  Experimental UV−vis spectra show the absorption edges at 500 and $580~\mathrm{nm}$ for the TKL and Li-TKL respectively, indicating that the TKL in charged (unlithiated) and discharged (lithiated) states can effectively absorb the visible light. Absorbance maximas are located at $397~\mathrm{nm}$ (TKL) and $427~\mathrm{nm}$ (Li-TKL), which indicate the absorbance range spans from blue to violet. The location of absorption maximas and their corresponding red shifts are well in agreement with our TD-DFT results performed for both the molecules using the respective solvation model. From the UV−vis spectrum, the band gap for TKL and Li-TKL were calculated to be 2.67 and $2.41~\mathrm{eV}.$ , respectively (as shown in the Tauc plot, Figure 2b), indicating the semiconducting nature of the TKL molecule.  Next, cyclic voltammetry (CV) was used to estimate HOMO and LUMO energies of the TKL molecule. In general, HOMO and LUMO energies are energies required to extract (oxidation) or inject (reduction) an electron from/to a molecule respectively, which can be measured from reduction and oxidation potentials. Cyclic voltammetry (Figure 2c) shows the reduction onset potential of $-0.65\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/}$ $\mathrm{AgNO}_{3}$ reference electrode. By calibrating and converting this potential using a ferrocene redox couple, the LUMO energy of the TKL was estimated to be $-3.7~\mathrm{eV.}$ Furthermore, the HOMO energy of $-6.4~\mathrm{eV}$ was calculated from the LUMO energy and band gap energy, as shown in Figure 2d. The experimentally observed HOMO and LUMO energy levels of TKL corroborate well with our DFT calculated molecular level ionization potential (IP) and electron affinity (EA) for the hole $\left({\mathrm{TKL}}^{\bullet-}\right)$ and anion $\left(\mathrm{TKL}^{\bullet+}\right)$ of TKL, respectively, as shown in Figure 2e. As light irradiates the TKL electrodes, electrons could be excited to the LUMO of TKL and transferred to SS current collectors. Our DFT calculations predict the HOMO $(E_{\mathrm{HOMO}}=-5.9~\mathrm{eV})$ level of Li-TKL to be destabilized by ${\sim}0.6$ eV than its corresponding nonlithiated derivative, as shown in Figure 2e. This substantiates the fact that the oxidation of LiTKL by the holes $\left(\mathrm{TKL}^{\bullet+}\right)$ could occur as soon as the electrons are excited to the LUMO of TKL and transferred to SS current collectors (Figure 2d). Considering the excited state stability of TKL with $4\mathrm{~H~}$ -bonds per molecule, faster forward electron injection than recombination ability, and our overall experimental evidence on the charge−discharge process to occur successfully, we believe that both of the processes are happening within comparable time scales. The time scales of various events such as production of electron−hole pairs, their recombination time, and $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ diffusion time scales through SEI and electrolytes are appended in the Supporting Information, Section 3.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Modified-Coin Cell Fabrication. The photocharging properties were evaluated using modified coin cells as summarized in Figure 3. The architecture of the photoelectrodes is critical for efficient light absorption and charge transfer. Here, stainless steel (SS) mesh was used as a current collector because of its open structure that allows the flow of both incoming light and Li-ions to the TKL molecules. Furthermore, the surface of the SS mesh is homogeneously coated with carbon using chemical vapor deposition (Figure 3a) to ensure the charge transfer of photoexcited electrons from TKL molecules to the SS current collector as shown in Figure 3c. The carbon coating was thin enough and did not block the open spaces in the mesh structure as shown in the SEM images (Figure $^{3\mathrm{b},\mathrm{c}}$ and Figure S2). After the carbon coating process, the SS mesh presents the broadened nature of the D and G bands in Raman spectra (Figure 3d) indicating defective amorphous carbon. After the carbon coating process, TKL containing slurry was coated in the center of the carboncoated SS so that the TKL-coated region will be placed directly under the glass window in the positive case. As shown in the Figure $^{3\mathrm{b},\mathrm{c},}$ surface morphology of the electrode changed from soft, amorphous carbon-like-texture to nanosize crystal-like texture after the TKL molecule is coated. Finally, the modified coin cell was used to assemble the TKL-based photoassisted batteries. A small window was created on commercial CR2032 coin cells and covered with a piece of cover glass using epoxy. Our modified-coin cells ensure hermetic sealing and an oxygen/moisture-free environment which allow the optical and electrochemical characterizations in an open atmosphere. The hermetic sealing was confirmed by leaving the modified cell in an open atmosphere and inspecting Li-foils as shown in Figure 3e. Even after exposing the cell to an open atmosphere for 13 days, there was no change in the color of the lithium foil, indicating that the modification on the coin cell did not alter the hermetic sealing.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Photocharging Effect. The photocharging capability of the TKL molecule was measured by potentiostatic and galvanostatic charge−discharge measurements. Before the potentiostatic measurement, the modified TKL cell with an optical window was first discharged in the dark to $1.5~\mathrm{V}$ versus $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ to lithiate the TKL molecule. After the discharge process, a constant voltage of $3\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ was applied in the presence and absence of $405~\mathrm{nm}$ violet laser irradiation, and the charging current was recorded. As shown in Figure $^{4\mathrm{a},}$ the charging-current increases under light irradiations and decreases in dark conditions, and this on/off behavior was observed repeatedly for the duration of the experiments. The increase of the charging current in the presence of light indicates the acceleration of the charging process by light irradiation.  Furthermore, photocharging was confirmed by the galvanostatic charge−discharge test as shown in Figure 4b. The charged cell was discharged at a constant current $\left(50\mathrm{mA}/\mathbf{g}\right)$ under light and dark conditions, and the attainable discharge capacities were compared. In dark conditions, specific discharge capacities of 333, 313, 306, and $296\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ were obtained for the first to fourth discharge cycles. The slight decay of the specific capacity is often observed in quinonebased organic electrode materials due to an instability of organic molecules in the electrolytes.20−22 After the four charge−discharge cycles in darkness, the cell was charged in the dark and discharged under the light irradiation. Surprisingly, a larger discharge capacity $(332\mathrm{mAh/g})$ was obtained compared to the previous cycle’s discharge capacity $(296~\mathrm{mAh/g})$ as shown in the inset figure of Figure $_{4\mathtt{C}}$ .  Defining the Coulombic efficiency as a ratio of the discharge capacity (lithiation) to the charge capacity (delithiation), the Coulombic efficiency is less than $100\%$ in the dark but it exceeds $100\%$ only in the presence of light as shown in Figure 4c. Such extra discharge capacity arises from the competing photocharging reactions during the discharge process. This reverse process regenerates the lithiated TKL back to TKL during the discharging process as illustrated in Figure 4d.  These concurrent competing reactions of photocharging and discharging extend the discharge capacity and result in the Coulombic efficiency of above $100\%$ . The efficiency can be further improved by optimizing the electrode architecture to improve the light interaction with the TKL molecule. Discharge voltage remained unchanged regardless of the light irradiation. Redox potential of n-type organic molecule (TKLcoordinated with $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ ) is governed by the LUMO energy of the molecules.15 Because the light irradiation does not alter the LUMO energy, the discharge voltage of the TKL molecule does not change in the presence of light. Light will assist the charging process by supplying energy for electrons to move from HOMO to LUMO. Then the applied potential will complete the charging process by providing enough energy for electrons to reduce Li as depicted in Figure S4. This is the reason why the photocharging occurs only in a close-circuit condition where electrons produced from the TKL cathode can flow to the Li metal anode for the corresponding reduction reactions. In the open circuit condition, only $30~\mathrm{mV}$ of potential increase was observed in $15\mathrm{~h~}$ of the light irradiation (Figure S5). Therefore, light irradiation assists and accelerates the charging process and cannot be the solo energy source for the charging process due to the two-electrode design of the cells.
