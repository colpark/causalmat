- caption: Fig. 1. SFE variation (Fe-25Mn-xAl-ySi-1C) with respect to Si substitution under the constraint of ${\sf A l}+{\sf S i}=8.5$ wt. $\%$ according to several references [8,35,36]. SFE decreases about $20~\mathrm{mJ}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ at 3 wt. $\%$ of replacement of Al by Si.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To reduce SFE in austenitic lightweight steel, synergetic addition of Al and Si is demonstrated in this work. The atomic weights of Al and Si are 26.98 and $29.09~\mathrm{g/mol}$ , respectively, implying a potential to replace Al with Si without a significant increase in density. Actually, it is reported that the rates of density reduction of Al and Si in austenite are 0.106 and $0.058~\mathrm{g}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ per wt. $\%$ of addition, respectively [17,33]. On the other hand, the effects of Al and Si on SFE are opposites in austenitic Fe-based alloy. SFE increases by roughly $9\mathrm{m}//\mathrm{m}^{2}$ per wt. $\%$ of Al [34], while decreases by about $4\mathrm{m}//\mathrm{m}^{2}$ per wt. $\%$ of Si [16]. Therefore, synergetic addition of Al and Si leads to density reduction while SFE is manageable for enabling TWIP effect. The total amounts of Al and Si were set as 8.5 wt. $\%$ to attain approximately $10\%$ of density reduction under a carbon concentration of 1.0 wt. $\%$ in austenite. The effect of synergetic Al and Si addition on SFE variation (see Fig. 1) was calculated by thermodynamic description assessed by various literatures [8,35,36]. The effect of grain size on SFE was not considered in the calculation because it is insignificant when the average $\gamma$ grain size is greater than ${\sim}35\mu\mathrm{m}$ [37]. As shown in Fig. 1, SFE decreases by about $20~\mathrm{mJ}/\mathrm{m}^{2}$ at 3 wt. $\%$ of replacement of Al by Si. However, SFE of the designed alloy $(\mathrm{Fe}-26.7\mathrm{Mn}-5.6\mathrm{Al}-3.0\mathrm{Si}-1.0\mathrm{C}-$ 0.2Mo-0.1V, in wt. $\%$ ) was too high to trigger TWIP effect based on reported criteria [4]. Surprisingly, it is reported in the following investigation that mechanical twinning can occur in this alloy during plastic deformation.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Fig. 7 shows the deformation microstructure in the coarsegrained steel $1030^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $1\mathrm{~h~},$ ) plastically deformed at several engineering strains $(5\%,10\%,20\%,.$ $40\%$ , and $70\%$ ). At $5\%$ strain, the plasticity occurred by mainly dislocation slip, as shown in Fig. 7(a). Most dislocations with Burgers vectors of $1/2\langle0\textit{1}\overline{{1}}\rangle$ were aligned on the same {1 1 1} plane, showing a characteristic of planar slip which led to substructure of Taylor lattice. When the strain reached $10\%$ , dislocation substructure of perfect dislocations continuously developed. However, some stacking faults (SFs) were found in several austenite grains, as shown in Fig. 7(b-c). Under $\bar{1}\bar{1}1$ two-beam condition, SFs on (1 1 1) and $(1\bar{1}1)$ planes were observed. The SFs on (1 1 1) plane disappeared under $0\bar{2}2$ twobeam condition because of the $\mathbf{g}\cdot\mathsf{R}=0$ rule. Actually, the WHR was not noticeably enhanced at this stage because the population of SFs was not high. When the strain reached $20\%$ , multiple planar slips of dislocations occurred extensively, and very clear Taylor lattices were observed, as shown in Fig. 7(d). Such deformation behavior is common in TRIPLEX steels and CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy [17,22,60,61]. Substructure of planar slips, or Taylor lattices, is favored in FCC crystal with low SFE, high friction stress, or short-range ordering (SRO) [16,62]. Although the SFE of the studied steel was high, its SFE was still lower than those of conventional austenitic lightweight steels [17]. Moreover, the studied alloy possessed SRO (see Fig. 4) and high friction stress (see Section 3.4). All the above factors drove dislocation glide into the mode of planar slip. At this strain, several nanoscale twins with thicknesses of 2-4 nm were also observed as shown in Fig. 7(e), and the corresponding HRTEM image is presented in Fig. 7(f). Actually, both Taylor lattices and mechanical twinning are products of planar slip but they should be enabled by different characteristics of dislocations. In Fe-Mn-Al-C austenitic steels, planar slip of perfect dislocations accompanied by Taylor lattices contributes steady work hardening via slip band refinement induced plasticity (SRIP) and microband induced plasticity (MBIP). In MBIP, grain subdivision by microbands results in Hall-Petch hardening effect [22]. This is different from SRIP, in which locking by intersecting slip bands or pile-ups is not emphasized [18]. In TWIP steel, the planar slip occurs via dissociation of perfect dislocation into partial dislocations and stacking fault. The emission of deformation twins evolve from these partial dislocations when a critical stress is reached [63]. Here, the emission of stacking faults was triggered by locks of intersecting slip bands at $10\%{-}20\%$ strain. This is consistent with the increase in the WHR at $13.3\%$ strain and explains the high WHR of over 2 GPa at $20\%$ strain due to the formation of deformation twins. It is noteworthy that the co-existence of Taylor lattices (MBIP) and mechanical twinning was discovered here. Similar deformation behavior has previously been observed in Fe–30.5Mn–2.1Al–1.2C (in wt. $\%$ [23] and CoCrFeMnNi high-entropy alloy [64,65], but it has not been reported in other austenitic lightweight steels.  At $40\%$ engineering strain, substructures of Taylor lattices were well developed. Moreover, more deformation twins were discovered, as shown in Fig. 7(h)-(i). When the engineering strain reached $70\%$ , which was close to necking, extremely high-density dislocations in Taylor lattices formed in the steel, as shown in Fig. 7(j), and many mechanical twins actually resided along the boundaries of Taylor lattices, as shown in Fig. 7(k)-(l). These twins were much thicker and had a morphology called deformation bundle twins [66]. Hence, the deformation twins developed by gradual accumulation of stacking faults and nanoscale twins. This behavior also explains the increase in WHR in the strain range of $16.8\%$ to $50.5\%$ . In Fe-Mn-Al-C austenitic steel without $\kappa$ precipitation, the WHR was only around 1500-1800 MPa when the grain size was similar to this case [17,21]. The enhanced WHR was attributed to the TWIP effect superposing on the MBIP effects.

- caption: Fig. 9. Schematic diagram of the modified Cohen-Weertman mechanism [75]. (a) Dislocation pileup producing the Lomer lock at the intersection of slip planes. (b) Mechanical twins generated in the bundle of SFs.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Based on TEM observations, it is proposed that emission of stacking faults occurred after the planar slip of perfect dislocations or partial dislocations with extremely short dissociation distances. Fig. 9 shows the possible formation mechanism of deformation twin in the current steel. At the initial stage, Taylor lattices form via dislocation of multiple planar slips. Consider the dislocation pileup which produces the Lomer lock by intersection of 1/2[1 0 i] dislocations on (1 1 1) and 1/2[0 1 1] dislocations on (1 1 i), as shown in Fig. 9 (a). When the resolved shear stress reaches a critical level, $1/2[0\mathrm{~}1\mathrm{~}1]$ dislocation can emit stacking fault by dissociation into $1/6[\bar{2}\mathrm{~}1\mathrm{~}1]$ Shockley partial and 1/3[111] Frank partial dislocations. When this reaction occurs for most Taylor lattices, deformation twins was generated in the bundle of faults, as shown in Fig. 9 (b). This is actually the perfect-dislocation fashion of the Cohen-Weertman mechanism [75]. When SFE is low, as in TWIP steel, the mechanism of mechanical twinning follows the original Cohen-Weertman mechanism. When SFE is high, as in most FeMn-Al-C alloys, the stress for triggering emission of staking fault is hardly reached before fracture, so deformation twinning was rarely observed. This modified Cohen-Weertman mechanism should be applied to alloys or steels with moderate SFEs. A model to predict twinning stress is proposed in this work.
