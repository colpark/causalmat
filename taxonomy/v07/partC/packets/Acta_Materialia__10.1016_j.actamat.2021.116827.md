- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Compression testing of a cylindrical specimen produces different stresses (radial compressive, Hoop tensile, and frictional shear stresses) at different locations of the material [12]. Correspondingly, the type and amount of deformation varies over the entire compression specimen, which in turn causes microstructural variation at different locations. Pure radial compressive stresses only prevail at the mid-thickness region of the compression specimen as shown in Fig. 1b. To characterize the microstructure formed from pure compressive stresses, the specimen was thus sectioned along the diameter in the z-direction (parallel to the compression direction, Fig. 1a). The SEM observations were then made from the regions along the equatorial plane (Fig. 1b). For this, the observation surfaces were polished by standard metallographic technique using SiC emery papers up to 2000 grit size and followed by polishing using $1\mu\mathrm{m}$ and $0.25\mu\mathrm{m}$ diamond particles. The polished surfaces were etched by Kroll’s reagent $(2\%~\mathrm{HF}+6\%~\mathrm{HNO}_{3}+92\%$ distilled water) for 10 sec. Microstructural observations were made by scanning electron microscope (Gemini, Germany) in back scattered mode. More details for microstructural characterization can be obtained elsewhere [12,21].

The two-point statistics are useful functions for defining microstructure in a mathematical framework due to the large amount of feature information that can be captured. The probability distribution functions here allow for the inclusion of many more microstructural information in the model than just the volume fraction or average grain size. For example, in P11 and P22 the shape and size of the central peak surrounding the point $(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=(0,0)$ correspond to the average grain morphology and average grain size for the phase in question. Meanwhile, in P11 and P22 the probability associated with the point $\boldsymbol{(\mathrm{x},\mathrm{y})}=\boldsymbol{(0,0)}$ corresponds to the volume fraction of the phase in question. This is due to the fact that at $\left(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\right)=0$ , the magnitude of the vector is 0 and as such it is the probability of finding a point that lies in the desired phase which is the definition of volume fraction Fig. 2 shows a SEM (BSE) image of the Ti-6Al-4V alloy deformed at a temperature of $750^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and a strain rate of $10~s^{-1}$ and the corresponding two-point statistics.  The plots P11, P12, P21 and P22 shown in Fig. 2 illustrate the tWo-point statistics associated with the SEM (BSE) image in Fig. 2. As such, the colorbar on each plot corresponds to the probability of finding a vector with its head and tail in the corresponding phases. Furthermore, the x and $\mathsf{y}$ axes correspond to the horizontal and vertical axes of the SEM (BSE) image respectively and are in units of pixels.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The heat treated and water quenched Ti-6Al-4V alloy shows a continuous grain boundary (GB) $\alpha$ phase along prior $\beta$ GB in the microstructure (Fig. 4a). In addition, lamellar $\alpha$ phase nucleates beside the GB $\alpha$ -phase and grows inside the prior $\beta$ grain. The remnant $\beta$ phase remains as thin lamellae between adjacent $\alpha$ lamella and forms $(\alpha{+}\beta)$ -colony microstructure [12]. After isothermal compression, most of the lamellar $(\alpha{+}\beta)$ colonies straighten along the direction of Hoop tensile stresses (perpendicular to the compression axis). In addition, some of the $(\alpha{+}\beta)$ colonies plus the corresponding $\mathtt{G B}\alpha$ phase, which seems to be originally parallel to the compression axis show a kinked or bent morphology after compression. This difference in the morphology is related to their relative orientation with respect to the compression axis which in turn controls the Taylor factor for different $(\alpha{+}\beta)$ colonies considering basal $\{0001\}{<}11\overline{{2}}0{>}$ and prism $\{10\bar{1}0\}{<}11\bar{2}0{>}$ slip systems [12,13]. While the straight $(\alpha{+}\beta)$ colonies possess high Taylor factor and initiates multiple slip helping them to accommodate the imposed deformation, slip initiation is somewhat restricted for the kinked $(\alpha{+}\beta)$ colonies because of the low Taylor factor.

### F6
- path: /home/aid1/Documents/causalmat/matmech/Acta_Materialia/10.1016_j.actamat.2021.116827/images/64395b470876818213d68f62d6b4d57c256a112ae33a0c65bb4cf37608d6e21b.jpg
- caption: Fig. 6. Comparison of the simulated (left) and experimental (right) P11 statistics from the deformed microstructures for each of the specimens compressed at different strain rates, (a) $10^{-3}s^{-1}$ (b) $10^{-2}s^{-1}$ (c) $10^{-1}s^{-1}$ (d) $1~{\mathsf{s}}^{-1}$ (e) 10 $S^{-1}$
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): In Fig. 6, the simulated statistics show strong anisotropic deformation behavior as evident from the shape of the central peak as well as the locations of the arms that branch off from the peak. The locations of these arms seemingly match the location of those in the statistics from the specimens compressed at strain rates of $\dot{\varepsilon}=10^{-3}s^{-1}$ , $10^{-2}s^{-1}$ ,  and $1~{s}^{-1}$ .  However, at strain rates of $\dot{\varepsilon}=10^{-1}s^{-1}$ and $10s^{-1}$ ,  these arms are not seen and the simulated statistics seem to overestimate the deformation of the central peak (i.e. the simulated peak is smaller than observed in the experimental statistics). The ability for the simulations to illustrate the anisotropic behavior of the material stems from the inclusion of the two-point statistics. Since the two-point statistics contains location information the model is able to individually deform each direction and as such anisotropic behavior can be modeled. The largest source of error for the simulations in Fig. 6 can be identified through the intensities associated with the limiting cases for two-point statistics as described by Corson [27,28]. These limiting cases are located at positions where $r=0$ and as $r~\rightarrow~\infty$ .For the point $r=0$ , two-point correlation function breaks down and converts to a single point function, which for a two-phase material is the volume fraction of the phase of interest. Furthermore as $r~\rightarrow~\infty$ , Corson [27,28] describes the limiting case as being equal to the product of the volume fraction of the two phases of interest (i.e. for P 11 as $r\rightarrow\infty$ $f_{2}\rightarrow V_{1}^{2}\}$ . What is important is that these limiting cases are described not by grain size or morphology but by the volume fraction of phases which this model does not consider. Therefore, it is believed that the initial statistics are not representative of the entire microstructure since the largest source of error in Fig. 6 is associated with these limiting cases. Furthermore, the simulation results presented in Fig. 7 not only suggest the same overestimation as Fig. 6 but also show the same error associated with the volume fraction of the initial statistics. To counter this issue, the effect of initial statistics was identified by calculating the two-point statistics from large area SEM (BSE) montages instead of single SEM (BSE) images.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The second form of validation for the proposed model focuses on recognizing a representative volume element from the large area SEM (BSE) montages for each of the compression conditions. It is important to consider here that the two-point statistics are representative of the entire microstructure within the compression specimens because it validates the capability of the model to predict the microscale evolutions from an applied macroscale strainrate tensor. To this end, SEM (BSE) montages were created for each of the specimens compressed at different strain rates $(\dot{\varepsilon}=10^{-3}$ $s^{-1}$ , $10^{-\bar{2}}s^{-1}$ , $10^{-1}s^{-1}$ , $1s^{-1}$ and $10~\mathsf{s}^{-1}$ ) and are used to calculate the two-point statistics. One such SEM (BSE) montage is shown in Fig. 8 for the specimen compressed at $1s^{-1}$ strain rate. It is important to note that the montages are captured at a relatively higher magnification, while simultaneously maintaining high resolution and covering a large microstructural area to facilitate the calculation of the two-point statistics with adequate statistical reliability. Also, these SEM (BSE) montages were made at the same magnification and resolution as the single SEM (BSE) images (shown in Fig. 4) used for the earlier calculations. By keeping the magnification and resolution constant between the SEM (BSE) montages and the single SEM (BSE) images, it allows for the comparison of the two-point statistics obtained from these two different approaches.
