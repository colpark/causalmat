- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The GeV ion exposure was carried out at the high-energy heavy-ion microbeam facility of the Heavy Ion Research Facility in Lanzhou (HIRFL). Figure 1a,b shows the schematic drawing of the microbeam facility and the photo of the irradiation platform that can provide maskless microbeam irradiation/writing and single ion hit using ions up to several GeVs.35,36 The ion beam of $2.15\mathrm{GeV}^{86}\mathrm{Kr}^{26+}$ was focused by the high gradient quadrupole triplet $\left(\mathrm{Q}5{-}\mathrm{Q}7\right)$ , transmitted through the $\mathrm{Si}_{3}\mathrm{N}_{4}$ vacuum window of $200~\mathrm{nm}$ thickness, and then hit the sample in air. Because of the high LET of the Kr ions, each individual ion can induce a continuous trail of damage in resists within the ion range. Along the ion trajectory, the property of resists is drastically changed due to the dense energy deposition, so that high-resolution and high-aspect ratio structures can be obtained after development. In a typical process, negative-tone $\mathrm{HSQ}$ resist was spin-coated onto the oxidized silicon substrate. Subsequently, the samples were irradiated with microbeam spot and then developed,37 rinsed, and dried. The experimental setup and the processes are illustrated in Figure 1c. Note that the whole sample preparation and the lithography process were performed in the atmosphere.

To investigate the ultrahigh resolution of the obtained features, the diameter of the structures was further measured and analyzed by atomic force microscopy (AFM). It is known that AFM has extremely high measurement accuracy at the vertical dimension, though its lateral resolution is inaccurate due to the tip convolution effects.39 Fortunately, due to the ultrahigh aspect ratio, all $\mathrm{HSQ}$ nanofilaments fell over and conformally adhered onto the substrate during the drying process. From the cross-sectional curve shown in Figure 3b, the height of six nanofilaments is measured to be from 2.2 to $2.5\mathrm{nm}$ . Figure 3c provides an additional AFM image obtained at another sample position, showing almost the same height map as that in Figure 3a. By further doing a statistical measurement on the nanofilaments in Figure 3c, the average diameter of the structures is $2.5\mathrm{nm}$ with a standard deviation of $0.4~\mathrm{nm}$ (Figure 3d), confirming the ultrahigh resolution of HSQ nanofilaments.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To better understand the mechanism of the extremely high resolution discovered in this work, we first investigated the energy deposition process and the dose distribution in the GeV Kr ion lithography. As shown in Figure 4a, when high-energy heavy ion bombards the solid, its energy is transferred to the column around the ion path. The energy transfer is evaluated by electronic stopping power $\left(S_{\mathrm{e}}\right)$ and nuclear stopping power $\left(S_{\mathrm{n}}\right)$ , which is defined as the energy transfer per unit trajectory $\left({{\mathrm{{d}}E}/{\mathrm{{d}}x}}\right)$ via its coulomb interaction with the extranuclear electrons and the nuclei of the target. We then performed Monte Carlo simulation with the transport of ions in matter (TRIM) code to explore the structure and the distribution of ionizing interaction event.41 It can be seen in Figure $^{4\mathrm{b}}$ that the $\mathrm{Kr}$ ion trajectory is very straight due to its high energy and large mass number, and the ionizing interactions induced by the incident ion and secondary electrons take place densely in the nanometer range around the ion path (as illustrated in the zoomed-in figure at the right side), though some ionizing events also occur hundreds of nanometers away induced by high energy secondary electrons.  On the basis of the stopping and range of ions in matter (SRIM) code and the Waligorski−Zhang Model,42,43 we calculated the deposited dose around the single $\mathrm{Kr}$ ion trajectory. The stopping power of $^{86}\mathrm{Kr}^{26+}$ ions in the lithography substrate was calculated with SRIM code, which obtained a $S_{\mathrm{e}}$ of $2.54\mathrm{keV/nm}$ and a $S_{\mathrm{n}}$ of $1.24~\mathrm{eV/nm}$ in ${\mathrm{HSQ}}.$ These data demonstrate that the electronic stopping dominates the energy deposition of $2.15\mathrm{GeV}$ Kr ions, and the nuclear stopping can be neglected. According to the Waligorski− Zhang model, we introduced a correction factor $C_{s}$ to their derivative in eq 1, which is the ratio of SRIM’s semiempirical stopping power over the integrated stopping power of the Waligorski−Zhang model. In this equation, $r$ is the radial distance from the linear ion path, $D(r)$ represents the radial dose distribution around the ion path, and the other parameters describe the properties of the incident ion and the target components (details given in Supporting Information Figure S2).  where $Z^{*}$ is the effective charge moving with a relative velocity $\beta=\nu/c$ ( $c$ is the speed of light) through the detector medium containing $N$ electrons per $\bar{\mathrm{cm}^{3}}$ , $m$ is the mass of the electron, $\theta$ is the “range” of an electron of energy $w=0.01\mathrm{keV}_{;}$ , and $T$ is the maximum range of delta-rays for $\beta<0.03.$ , $\alpha=1.079\$ , and for $\beta>0.03.$ , $\alpha=1.667$ . The calculated energy deposition of $2.15\mathrm{GeV}^{86}\mathrm{Kr}^{26+}$ in HSQ is shown in Figure $^{4\mathrm{c},}$ and the radial dose shows a sharp decrease with the radial distance. The dose decreases from $9.{\dot{7}}3\times10^{5}$ Gy at the center to about $1.07\times10^{4}$ Gy at $2\mathrm{nm}$ radial distance, that is, most of the energy deposition of 2.15 GeV Kr ion takes place in the trajectory vicinity ( $95\%$ energy deposited within the radius of $1\mathrm{nm}$ ). Actually, the ultrastraight path of the $2.15\mathrm{GeV}^{86}\mathrm{Kr}^{26+}$ ion have recently been verified by our experiments via fluorescence nuclear track detector (FNTD), as shown in Figure 4d. This is the first direct experimental observation of a straight path of single heavy ions along a long path up to several tens of micrometers. Together with the extremely localized energy deposition, the ultrastraight path of heavy ions makes singleion lithography advantageous for achieving ultrahigh aspect ratio nanostructures, which explains the uniform feature size of HSQ resist in the experiment.  In traditional lithography, the micro/nano structures are formed by the energy deposition due to the exposure of multiple photons of several electronvolts and electrons/ions of $\mathtt{k e V}$ to MeV energy. Nanostructure lithography using single GeV ions in HSQ have not been reported up to now, and the physical character of the energy deposition of the GeV Kr ion plays a key role in the nanostructure formation in HSQ lithography. First, the energy deposition density of $2.15\mathrm{GeV}$ $\mathrm{Kr}$ ion is 49.3 times higher than that of $20\mathrm{keV}\mathrm{He}^{2+}$ (254.6 vs $5.2\mathrm{eV}/\mathring\mathrm{A}$ in HSQ), 84.3 times higher than that of $5\mathrm{keV}$ electrons $\left(3.0\mathrm{eV}/\mathring{\mathrm{A}}\right)$ . The comparison of the simulated energy deposition and trajectory of $2.{\dot{15}}\mathrm{GeV}^{86}\mathrm{Kr}^{26+}$ , $20\mathrm{keV},$ and 20 keV $\mathrm{Ga}^{3+}$ in $\mathrm{HSQ}$ also confirms the much higher energy deposition of $2.15\mathrm{GeV}^{86}\mathrm{Kr}^{26+},$ as shown in Figure S3. In addition, Monte Carlo simulation of the ionizing interactions induced by $\mathrm{1~MeV~}^{4}\mathrm{He}^{2+}$ beam in HSQ is shown in Figure S4. Different from high energy $\mathrm{Kr}$ ions trajectory, it has a large scattering section and short penetration depth. Second, due to the production of high energy secondary electrons, the observed $\mathrm{HSQ}$ nanowire of $2.5~\mathrm{nm}$ (radius of $1.25~\mathrm{nm},$ ) corresponds with a deposited dose of ${\sim}2.5\times10^{4}$ Gy according to eq 1, suggesting that the threshold dose of forming a nanowire structure is at the scale of $1\times10^{4}$ Gy. A more detailed comparison with electron beam and proton beam exposure parameters is given in Table S1 in Supporting Information.
