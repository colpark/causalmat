- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The SW-like Li metal anode was prepared through a facile and controllable self-regulation electro-deposition process, during which an ultrathin inducible thiophdiyne (TD) layer grown on a Cu foil was used as the working electrode and Li foil was used as the counter electrode. While operated with a fixed current density after a certain charge capacity, metallic Li with SWlike morphology could be directly deposited on the TD layer.  As high-polarity function group with large electronegativity and inductive effect, sulfur-containing compounds such as thiophene have been normally used to fabricate microporous electrode material with strong Li ions binding ability.[33] The acetylene bonds in the carbon skeleton show excellent lithiophilic behavior and fast charge diffusion, facilitating the uniform deposition of Li to obtain a dendrite-free Li anode.[34] It is considered that linking TD units with acetylene bonds can improve the extended conjugate structure, enhance the charge diffusion behavior and electronegativity, leading to a strong binding energy between Li atoms and the lithiophilic TD substrate. Owing to its enhanced electronegativity and inductive effect, the area surrounding the TD acts as a homogeneous nucleating area for regulating the nucleation of Li. Therefore, a TD layer composed of uniformly distributed TD and acetylene bonds, was used as the substrate for the homogeneous nucleation and growth of Li metal, as shown in Figure 1a. The TD layer was formed on a bare Cu foil through a facile in situ crosscoupling reaction.[35] The TD layer showed strong lithiophilicity because of its extended conjugate structure, enhanced charge diffusion behavior and electronegativity caused by linking thiophene units with the acetylene bonds (Figure  1a–i).[16,35] With such strong electronegativity and inductive effect, the area around thiophene units could act as a homogeneous nucleating area for regulating the nucleation of Li (Figure  1a–ii), achieving a stable Li electrode with specific seaweed-like morphology (Figure  1a-iii). The detailed preparation procedure for the TD layer is provided in Figures S1–S14, Supporting Information.[36–38]  As observed using scanning electron microscopy (SEM), the surface of the TD layer was very smooth (Figure  1b) and was composed of granules (Figure 1c). Thus, the TD layer was found to be suitable for the growth of Li metal. The thickness of the TD layer was estimated to be approximately $40~\mathrm{nm}$ (Figure 1d). It was expected that the ultrathin TD layer will not decrease the energy density of lithium metal batteries. The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image in Figure  1e shows the porous structure of the TD layer. The top surface and cross-section of the as-prepared TD-SW-Li, which was grown on the TD layer, could be directly examined after disassembling from the asymmetrical asymmetry $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{TD}$ cells. As shown in Figure 1f,g, at the fixed current density of $2~\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ (after the deposition of metallic Li at $5\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ ), the entire top surface of the obtained Li (labelled with cyan color) was flat with lumpy stripe patterns, without any dendrite interface. The lumpy stripe patterns were actually the top-view of the TD-SW-Li rods (Figure 1h,i). The TD-based SEI layer was formed by the deposition and interaction of the electrolyte and lithium salt (brown color). It was observed that the passivation TD-based SEI layer had a thickness of few microns, which could effectively induce the Li metal deposition without affecting the energy density of the Li metal anode. Meanwhile, the ultrathin passivation TD-based layer also improved the interface contact and ion transport performance impedance for homogeneous Li deposition.[23] The as-deposited TD–SW–Li rods with seaweed-like morphology grew closely on the TD based SEI layer, as shown in Figure 1g,i. The SEM images of the cross-sections of the TD– SW–Li observed from different angles are shown in Figure  1j. The TD-SW-Li rods were tight with a height of approximately  $60\upmu\mathrm{m}$ . The top of the Li metal rods was bigger than the bottom, and the rods grew tightly from the surface of the TD based SEI layer. While some of the Li metal rods were exfoliated during the sample preparation, the interior of the TD–SW–Li rods could be clearly observed. These rods were composed of vertical Li rods (Figure 1k,l). Each of the Li metal rods grew alone without agglomeration avoiding the formation of Li metal lumps. The average diameter of the vertical Li metal rods was approximately $3~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ . The original SEM images (without color) are shown in Figure S15, Supporting Information. The results indicated that the TD substrate could be used to induce the homogeneous growth of the TD–SW–Li rods. Meanwhile, the TD–SW–Li rods could be obtained at different current densities, such as 0.1, 0.5, and $1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ after the deposition of metallic Li at $5\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , as shown in Figures S16a–c and S17, Supporting Information. The average diameters of the Li rods were approximately 2.0, 2.2, and $2.8~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ when the current densities were 0.1, 0.5, and $1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , respectively. This suggests that the morphology of the TD–SW–Li rods and the thickness of the Li rods could be regulated by varying the current density. The Li metal layer deposited directly on the bare Cu foil (Cu–Li) were found to be dense and compact at the controlled current densities (Figure S16d, Supporting Information).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Subsequently, the electrochemical performance of the Li plating–stripping process was investigated with the asymmetric Cu–Li/Li and TD–SW–Li/Li coin cells using a Li anode as the counter electrode. Coulombic efficiency (CE) is considered as the most effective parameter for evaluating the Li plating–stripping behavior of a material, and is obtained by calculating the ratio of Li stripped from the substrate during the repeated plating–stripping cycles.[18] As can be observed from Figure 2a, when the current density was $0.1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , the CE of the TD–SW–Li-based cell was as high as $90\%$ even after 1000 cycles, while the Cu–Li based cell delivered a fluctuating, poor CE which finally dropped after 100 cycles. At a high current density of $2\mathrm{mAcm}^{-1}$ (Figure 2b), the CE of the TD–SW–Li-based cell remained stable for over 600 cycles. Whereas, the bare Cu–Li cell delivered a decreasing CE and failed after 100 cycles. These results indicate that TD–SW–Li could greatly improve the cell stability by regulating the Li plating–stripping performance to achieve long-term reversibility. A comparison of the plating−stripping performances of previously reported $\mathrm{Cu}/\mathrm{Li}$ cells (as summarized in Table S1, Supporting Information) and the TD–SW–Li-based cell prepared in this study revealed that the TD–SW–Li-based cell showed better plating–stripping cyclic stability during the long-term reversibility process than the previously reported cells. This can be attributed to the unique seaweed-like morphology of the TD–SW–Li-based cell. Furthermore, the cells were first discharged separately at fixed current densities (e.g., 0.1, 0.5, 1, and $2\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2},$ ) with a capacity of $0.5\mathrm{mAh~cm}^{-2}$ to evaluate the effect of Li morphology on the performance of the battery. Figure  2c,d respectively shows the initial voltage profiles of TD–SW–Li and bare Cu–Li-based cells during the first Li plating process. The Li nucleation overpotential was considered as an intuitional representative of the nucleation barriers on the Li deposition interface.[34] For the bare Cu–Li-based cell, a sharp voltage dip could be observed. The bare Cu–Li cell exhibited a nucleation overpotential of $-0.062\mathrm{V}$ at $0.1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and $-0.119\mathrm{~V~}$ at $2\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ . For the TD–SW–Libased cells, a relatively slow voltage dip was observed during the initial plating stage with a nucleation overpotential of $-0.045\mathrm{~V~}$ at $0.1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ and $-0.096\mathrm{~V~}$ at $2~\mathrm{mA}~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ . The improvement in the nucleation overpotential of the TD–SW–Li cell during the Li deposition process could be ascribed to the good lithiophilicity of TD–SW–Li.[38] As tendency was observed at the current densities of 0.5 and $1~\mathrm{mA~cm^{-1}}$ (Figures S18 and S19, Supporting Information). These results indicate that the TD–SW–Li-based cells exhibited better plating–stripping CE and slower voltage dip than the bare Cu–Li cell. Furthermore, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements of the asymmetric cells were carried out. It is evident from Figure  2e, that the TD–SW–Li and Cu-based cells exhibited similar interfacial resistances in the initial stage. However, the Nyquist plots of the TD–SW–Li-based cell showed a semicircle with an interfacial resistance of approximately $46\Omega$ after the first Li plating process, which is larger than that of the Cu–Li based cell (approximately $24~\Omega$ ). This can be attributed to the formation of a stable and ultrathin TD-based SEI layer in the TD–SW–Li-based cell, while Li metal was directly deposited on the Cu–Li-based cell.[39] The TD–SW–Li- and Cu–Li-based cells showed similar interfacial resistances (approximately $22\Omega_{\cdot}$ ) after the 1st Li stripping process. The cells exhibited exceedingly close interfacial resistances after the 5th plating process (Figure  2f). After the 10th and 50th plating cycles, the semi– circle for both the cells showed a small interfacial resistance for the residual Li metal on the stripping surface (Figure S20, Supporting Information). To investigate the electrochemical stability and rate capability of the TD–SW–Li cell, symmetrical TD–SW–Li/Li- and $\mathrm{Cu-Li/Li}$ cells were also assembled. The plating–stripping behavior of the TD–SW–Li/Li cell was tested at a current density of $0.1{-}2\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}$ with a fixed capacity. From Figure $2\mathrm{g}$ , it can be observed that the TD–SW–Li/Li anode-based symmetrical cell showed a relatively low and stable overpotential $(<10\mathrm{mV})$ even at a high Li deposition amount of $2.0\mathrm{mAh~cm^{-2}}$ at a current density of $2~\mathrm{mA}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ . In contrast, the Cu–Li/Li cell displays voltage fluctuations and an unstable overpotential with the increasing current density. As shown in Figure  2h, at a current density of $1~\mathrm{mA}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , the $\mathrm{Cu-Li/Li}$ cells worked only for $60\mathrm{~h~}$ before failure. The TD–SW–Li/Li cell continued working for $200\mathrm{~h~}$ with a relative stable overpotential plateau without obvious voltage hysteresis and ragged voltage profile than Cu–Li/Li cell. This indicates that the TD–SW–Li/Li anode is suitable for maintaining the plating–stripping stability of Li batteries during prolonged cycling.[31,40,41]

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The growth of the TD–SW–Li and its plating/stripping process were further studied through density function theory (DFT) calculation. The adsorption energies of a single Li atom on the selected TD unit were calculated by DFT to investigate the Li nucleation mechanism on the TD layer, as shown in Figure 4a. The corresponding adsorption energies were $-0.83$ (at the center of the thiophene rings, Figure 4a), $-1.36$ (on the top of the sulfur atoms, Figure  4a-ii) and $-1.09\mathrm{eV}$ (near the acetylene bond, Figure  4a-iii). Thus, both the thiophene units and acetylene bonds in TD exhibit lithiophilicity, which significantly enhanced the Li adsorption ability of TD. Figure 4a also shows the top and side views of the charge densities of TD with one Li atom. The introduction of S atoms greatly improved the electronic state of the S-surrounding region because of the similar electronegativities of the C (2.55) and S (2.58) atoms.[24] The extra electrons were mainly concentrated in the S atoms surrounding the region, which could prevent further acceptance of additional electrons for common S-doped carbon materials.[24] For the TD layer (Figure  4b-i), the extra electrons transferred rapidly and dispersed evenly around the thiophene rings linked to the acetylene sites, owing to the increasing conjugated structure and the inductive effect of the thiophene surrounding area. Therefore, it can be concluded that charge migrated from the Li atoms and accumulated in the intermediate region between the S atom containing the TD layer and Li atom during the charge migration process (Figure 4b-ii). Subsequently, more Li atoms could be absorbed and reduced uniformly around the thiophene containing carbon skeletons. This was helpful for promoting the uniform generation of nucleating sites for multipoint nucleation (Figure 4b-i). Thus,the TD layer was considered to act as a homogeneous nucleating substrate for regulating the growth of Li metal through a self-regulation deposition process, as shown in Figure 4c. Compared to bare Cu, the existence of a TD layer could promote the uniform distribution of Li on the deposition interface during the absorption stage because of its enhanced lithiophilicity (Figure 4c-i). In addition, the uniformly distributed thiophene units and acetylene bonds in the TD skeleton served as energetic and homogeneous sites for inducing the Li nucleation in the initial nucleation stage (Figure  4c-ii). Finally, a TD–SW–Li metal was obtained during the continuous deposition process (Figure  4c-iii). Therefore, the theoretical results suggested that the self-regulation of TD–SW–Li metal during the nucleation and deposition process was achieved by uniform distribution of active points on the deposition substrate.
