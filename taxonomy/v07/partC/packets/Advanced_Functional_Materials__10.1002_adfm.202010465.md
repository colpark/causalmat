- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Here, we report an effective strategy to synthesize conductive polydopamine-reduced-graphene oxide (PGO)-hybridized cellulose (PGC) bio-nanosheets, and a PGC bio-nanosheetassembled hydrogel (PGCNSH) with good mechanical flexibility, conductivity, and cell/tissue affinity (Figure  1). Most importantly, the PGCNSH demonstrated ultra-high biostability by withstanding long-term immersion in aqueous environments and implantation for over 30 days. Using the highly stable, conductive, biocompatible PGCNSH, we successfully fabricated epidermal and implantable bioelectronic devices to acquire reliable biosignals even when in contact with biofluids. Additionally, the PGCNSH served as “E-skin” to efficiently transmit electrical stimulation to accelerate diabetic wound healing. The PGC bio-nanosheets and the assembled PGCNSH are promising for use as novel electronic biomaterials to fabricate reliable bioelectronic devices to support human health.  A top–down approach was developed for fabricating 2D PGC bio-nanosheets, which principally involved PGO-assisted extraction of the cellulose nanofibrils (CNFs) from microcrystalline cellulose (MCC) and PGO-templated in situ regeneration of nanostructured cellulose (Figure 1a and Scheme S1, Supporting  Information). First, the PGO nanosheets intercalated into the chain packing of MCC to assist the extraction of CNFs in the presence of concentrated NaOH/urea solutions. During this process, NaOH/urea penetrated between the crystallites and destroyed the inter- and intra-molecular hydrogen bonds between the cellulose molecules in MCC to extract nanoscale CNFs. Meanwhile, the PGO nanosheets with abundant catechol groups (Figure S1, Supporting Information),[27] good water dispersibility, and alkali resistance (Figure S2, Supporting Information), provided favorable templates allowing the extracted CNFs to immobilize on their surfaces because of the physical interactions between the abundant oxygen-containing groups of PGO and hydroxyl groups of CNFs to form an intermediate, namely CNFs-PGO complex. This is essential for the formation of subsequent 2D nanosheets. Second, the CNFs-PGO complex was subjected to a freeze–thaw treatment, which allowed the dissolution of CNFs and in situ regeneration of cellulose on the PGO surfaces. During this process, the hydrogen bonding between CNFs was further disrupted and CNFs were dissolved into small cellulose molecules, while the hydrophilic PGO served as the planar template to guide the cellulose molecule arrangement in a preferential orientation and in situ regeneration into cellulose II sheets on PGO surfaces. Consequently, PGC bio-nanosheets with high homogeneity, excellent conductivity, and high mechanical strength were achieved.  A bottom–up approach was then used to engineer a biostable and conductive hydrogel by physical self-assembly and chemical crosslinking of PGC bio-nanosheets (Figure  1b and Scheme S1, Supporting Information). The self-assembly of the PGC bio-nanosheets was driven by the non-covalent interactions between individual nanosheets, and the chemical crosslinking was achieved by using epichlorohydrin (ECH) as a covalent agent that reacted with cellulose chains in the PGC bio-nanosheets. The physically–chemically dual-crosslinked PGC bio-nanosheets endowed the hydrogel with good flexibility and mechanical strength. In addition, the assembled PGC bio-nanosheets with intrinsic conductivity constructed a continuous and well-connected 3D network, which provided good electrical connection, thereby guaranteeing high conductivity of the hydrogel. Thus, the intrinsic physical properties of 2D bio-nanosheets (mechanical strength and electrical conductivity) were reflected in the resultant PGCNSH. In addition, the strong interactions between cellulose and PGO combined with the physicochemical crosslinking of the PGC bio-nanosheets effectively restricted the relaxation of the cellulose chains, and endowed the hydrogel with ultra-high biostability. Conversely, direct mixing of conductive PGO with pre-dissolved cellulose did not result in hydrogels with such attractive properties (Schemes S2, S3, Supporting Information), as will be discussed in a later section.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The PGO-templated extraction of CNFs from MCC and in situ regeneration of cellulose to form PGC bio-nanosheets were first investigated by observing the morphological changes (Figure 2a; Figure S3, Supporting Information). The CNFs-PGO complex intermediates displayed cellulose nanofibrils $\mathrm{({\approx}15n m)}$ , which were adsorbed on the PGO surfaces and formed interwoven networks. Note that if the MCC was only treated with $\mathrm{NaOH/}$ urea, and the resulting fibers were in much larger bundles (Scheme S2 and Figure S4, Supporting Information). A comparison of these two cases revealed that the PGO nanosheets promoted the extraction process of MCC and anchored the extracted CNFs on their surfaces. After subsequent freeze–thaw treatment of the CNFs-PGO complex, the CNFs transformed into a homogenous membrane-like structure, while the high transparency and wrinkles observed in the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image confirmed the formation of 2D planar PGC bio-nanosheets. An atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis indicated that the PGC bio-nanosheets were $50~\mathrm{nm}$ in thickness (Figure 2b; Figure S6, Supporting Information).  The accompanying crystalline structural change of the cellulose during the formation of the PGC bio-nanosheets was examined using X-ray diffraction (XRD), and the results are presented in Figure  2c, Figure S7 and Table S3, Supporting Information. The cellulose in the CNFs-PGO complex intermediates had the same crystalline type $(\mathrm{I}\beta)$ as MCC. However, the cellulose crystallinity $(38.9\%)$ of the CNFs-PGO complex was lower than that of the CNFs $(52.3\%)$ extracted from MCC without PGO, which further demonstrated that PGO played an effective role in breaking down the crystalline structure of the MCC and peeling off the CNFs. In contrast to the CNFsPGO complex, the PGC bio-nanosheets displayed the typical diffraction features of cellulose II crystalline, indicating the dissolution of CNFs in the CNFs-PGO complex and the formation of regenerated cellulose II on the PGO surfaces.[28] In addition, the intensity of the (110) crystal plane of the PGC bio-nanosheets was markedly higher than that of pure regenerated cellulose (RC), which demonstrated that the (110) crystal plane of RC was preferentially oriented on the PGC bionanosheets. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) characterizations revealed band shifting and absorbance change (Figure  2d; Figure S8, Supporting Information), supporting the hypothesis of the PGO-templated extraction and in situ regeneration processes. Moreover, FTIR spectra showed that the absorbance of the band at $3310{-}3230~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ of the PGC bionanosheets increased compared with that of RC, indicating that the intermolecular hydrogen bonding between cellulose chains increased (Figure 2d; Figures S9, S10, Supporting Information). This result suggests that the interactions between the PGO nanosheets and cellulose molecules were sufficiently strong; therefore, PGO can guide the regeneration of cellulose to form stable crystalline cellulose. The interactions between PGO and cellulose were evidenced by a density functional theory investigation (Figure S11, Supporting Information), which indicated the possibility for the formation of hydrogen bonding between the hydroxyl groups on the cellulose chains and the oxygencontaining groups of PGO.  The described structural characterizations provided insights into the mechanism of PGC bio-nanosheets formation (Figure  2g). During the regeneration process, the functional groups of the PGO nanosheets formed strong interactions with the hydroxyl groups of the dissolved cellulose molecules, preventing self-aggregation of the cellulose chains and inducing in situ regeneration of cellulose molecular sheets on the PGO surfaces (Figure $2\mathrm{g}$ (1)). The hydrophilic PGO nanosheets tuned the preferential arrangement of regenerated cellulose molecules related to the hydrophilic (110) crystalline plane with many exposed hydroxyl groups (Figure 2g(2,3);  The strategy of employing PGO nanosheets as 2D planar templates to guide the regeneration of cellulose II allowed tuning of the physical properties of cellulose-derived nanomaterials. Owing to its redox activity, and stabilizing and binding abilities,[30–32] polydopamine (PDA) is superior to typically used reducing agents to obtain conductive PGO. The PGO remained stable in both water and NaOH solutions without precipitation for at least 60  min (Figure S2, Supporting Information), which suggested that the PGO effectively solved the non-dispersible problem of generally used conductive nanofillers in cellulose solutions with high NaOH/urea concentration. As observed by TEM, the PGO had a typical transparent and wrinkled thin-sheet structure, and no aggregation was observed (Figure 2a). These results are consistent with those of previous studies,[33–35] demonstrating that PDA decoration can effectively prevent the aggregation of reduced-GO nanosheets, and lead to colloidally stable PGO nanosheets. In contrast, the hydroiodic acid-reduced-GO tended to aggregate in the NaOH solution at high concentrations because the high ionic strength reduced the repulsion between the rGO nanosheets.  Furthermore, the PGO nanosheets with specific catechol groups closely interacted with cellulose molecular chains, allowing the in situ intercalation of cellulose crystallites and regeneration of cellulose II crystals. To further demonstrate the advantage of PGO-templated in situ formation of PGC bio-nanosheets, the PGO nanosheets were directly mixed into a pre-regenerated cellulose solution to form GMC complex (see details in Scheme S3, Supporting Information). This method could not achieve homogenous nanosheets, instead of resulting in cellulose aggregates randomly distributed on the PGO surfaces (Figure S5a, Supporting Information). In short, the PGO nanosheet is an ideal agent to assist the dissolution of cellulose and is an attractive template for constructing conductive cellulose-PGO-hybridized nanosheets as bioelectronic materials with layered morphology, high mechanical modulus (Figure 2e), and good conductivity (Figure 2f; Figure S14, Supporting Information).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The as-produced PGC bio-nanosheets were prone to selfassembly into a jelly-like pre-gel (Figure  3a(1)). The ECH crosslinking of the pre-gel resulted in a shiny black freestanding solid PGCNSH (Figure  3a(2)). For further use, the PGCNSH was washed repeatedly until the dialysate was neutral. Rheological tests were conducted to characterize the viscoelastic behavior of the samples at different stages (Figure 3b; Figure S15, Supporting Information). Step-strain measurements showed that the PGCNS pre-gel ruptured at high strain $(200\%)$ , and then recovered within seconds at a subsequent low strain-induced flow $(0.1\%)$ (Figure  3b(1)); this indicates that the reversible non-covalent interactions between the PGC bio-nanosheets were the driving force of self-assembly to form the PGCNS pre-gel.[36] Dynamic frequency sweeps revealed a high storage modulus $(G^{\prime})$ over the full frequency range for the pre-gel (Figure  3b(2)). After ECH crosslinking, the PGCNSH was stronger with higher modulus values, and $G^{\prime}$ was higher than $G^{\prime\prime}$ over the entire testing range $(0.01{-}10~\mathrm{Hz})$ ), indicating that the PGCNSH had a stable crosslinked network. In summary, the rheological results supported the formation mechanism of the PGCNSH based on the physical interactions between the PGC bio-nanosheets and the ECHinduced chemical crosslinking of the PGC bio-nanosheets. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) micrographs confirmed that the wrinkled PGC nanosheets assembled into a homogeneous porous hydrogel structure (Figure  3c; Figure S16, Supporting Information). The PGCNSH exhibited excellent mechanical performances (Figure  3d; Figure S17, Supporting Information), with higher mechanical strength, stretchability, modulus, and toughness than those for pure regenerated cellulose-formed hydrogel (RCH) and GMC complex-formed hydrogel (GMCH) (see detailed preparation processes in Supporting Information).  The PGCNSH exhibited long-term underwater mechanical stability, which is conducive to its extended use in aqueous environments. The PGCNSH maintained its original volume after being soaked for 30 days in PBS, and the hydrogel maintained an almost constant weight throughout the immersion period (Figure  3e(1)). When the hydrogel was removed from the PBS, it exhibited elastic and resilient behavior, and bounced back from the floor when dropped (Figure 3e(2)). Furthermore, the hydrogel was sufficiently enough to sustain a loading weight of $_{100~\mathrm{~g~}}$ without noticeable deformation and was sufficiently flexible to be bent underwater (Figure 3e(3,4)). Moreover, the hydrogel maintained its tensile and compressive behaviors (Figure  3f(1,2)). The toughness of the hydrogel also did not decrease even after being soaked for 30 days (Figure 3f(3)). An overlapping of the cyclic compressive curves was observed during 50 cycles of loading–unloading at $15\%$ strain (Figure ${3}\mathrm{f}(4))$ , indicating that the hydrogel retained good recoverability. The biostable mechanical performance of the PGCNSH is attributed to the following aspects. First, the PGC bio-nanosheets were formed by in situ regeneration of cellulose on PGO surfaces, which ensured strong interfacial interactions between the PGO nanosheets and cellulose chains at the molecular level. Second, the PGC bio-nanosheets were physically and chemically dual-crosslinked to form PGCNSH, in which the PGC bio-nanosheets served as good “load carriers” to effectively transfer mechanical stress and contribute to energy dissipation throughout the hydrogel network during loading and deformation.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The PGCNSH also exhibited a stable electrical conductivity of $>6\mathrm{S}\cdot\mathrm{m}^{-1}$ throughout a 30-day incubation at $37~^{\circ}C$ in PBS (Figure  4a). The hydrogel was integrated with a circuit containing a light-emitting diode (LED), which remained illuminated when the hydrogel was immersed in water (inset in Figure  4b). Moreover, the stable PGCNSH showed a low impedance $(<35\mathrm{~\Omega~}\Omega)$ at physiologically relevant frequencies of $10^{2}{-}10^{5}~\mathrm{{Hz}}$ (Figure  4c). The PGCNSH showed a constant resistance change ratio at defined compressive strain even after immersion in PBS for 30 days (Figure S18, Supporting Information), further validating the long-term electromechanical stability of the PGCNSH in aqueous environments. Moreover, the immersed hydrogel exhibited sensitive electromechanical response under compression (Figure  4d). The sensitivity was defined as the gauge factor, which was 0.73, 1.18, and 1.76 in the strain ranges of $0-10\%$ , $10\mathrm{-}15\%$ , and $15{-}20\%$ , respectively. The gauge factor of PGCNSH is better than that of many conductive nanofiller-incorporated hydrogels $\left(\mathrm{GF}=0.48–0.63\right)$ ),[37,38] which is ascribed to the close-packed assembly of the intrinsically conductive PGC bio-nanosheets.  2.6. PGCNSH as Stable Epidermal Bioelectronic Devices  Owing to its combination of biostable conductivity, sensitivity, and mechanical properties, the PGCNSH is well-suited for use in epidermal bioelectronics to stably monitor various bodily movements and signals in an aqueous environment. First, the PGCNSH was attached to a finger as a strain sensor (Figure 4e). The flexibility of the PGCNS-sensor ensured firm contact with the skin even underwater, which accurately recorded the bending angle of the finger based on its resistance change. Second, the PGCNSH was fixed to the arm of a volunteer and integrated with a multichannel physiological signal acquisition and processing system (Figure  4f). An electromyogram (EMG) was precisely detected that matched to the command for the volunteer to contract or relax their arm. Importantly, the EMG signal was reproducibly and stably detected even after the PGCNSH was soaked in PBS for 30 days. Third, a wireless cardiac monitoring system was integrated with the PGCNSH, and the electrocardiogram (ECG) of a swimmer before and after swimming could be remotely recorded and displayed on a smart phone (Figure 4g; Figure S19, Supporting Information), which enabled us to analyze the heart rate in real-time during different human activities even under underwater conditions.  The PGCNSH with biocompatibility and long-term in vivo stability was applied in implantable bioelectrodes to monitor the in vivo deep muscle activity of a rabbit. To confirm its in vivo stability, the PGCNSH was subcutaneously implanted in rabbits for 30 days. The retrieved PGCNSH maintained its volume and structural integrity well (Figure  4h). The mechanical strength of the hydrogels remained unchanged, and the conductivity slightly increased owing to the penetration of physiological ions (Figure 4i). A histological analysis (H&E staining) demonstrated that the hydrogel had good biocompatibility, and did not cause fibrotic reactions or inflammatory responses to the surrounding tissues (Figure 4j). The signal detection performance of the PGCNSH was evaluated in vivo by implanting the hydrogel in a rabbit back. As shown in Figure 4k, distinguishable EMG signals were recorded during different behaviors of the rabbit, such as stillness, and chewing or looking states. These results demonstrate the potential of biostable PGCNSH as a reliable bioelectronic device when implanted and exposed to biofluids. Despite the potential of the conductive hydrogels for applications in bioelectronic devices, such as “E-skin”, the electronic stability over the lifetime of the hydrogel in vivo is still a challenge. Challenges exist because harsh environments, such as body fluids and salty environments, could interfere with the hydrogel electronics and cause in negative effects, such as liquid leakage, unstable electrical performance, and potential biocompatibility issues.[39,40] Heretofore, cellulose-based materials with high stability have been created for various biomedical applications, such as flexible/transparent substrates, tissue bioelectronics, and other flexible electronics.[16,25] The current study demonstrates that the cellulose-derived PGCNSH can function as a robust and stable conductive system, being unaffected by contact with biofluids in wet or harsh physiological environments.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The PGCNSH regulated cell behavior under an external electrical field owing to its cell affinity and stable conductivity in a physiological environment. To test the validity of the PGCNSHbased stimulator, myoblasts (C2C12 cells) were cultured on PGCNSH, and the cellular activity of the C2C12 cells was investigated under different electrical stimulation voltages (0, 300, and $900~\mathrm{mV})$ ) (Figure  5a). Pure cellulose hydrogels were also tested as controls. Confocal fluorescence images showed that few C2C12 cells adhered poorly to pure cellulose hydrogels with round morphology, and electrical stimulation did not affect the cell attachment on pure cellulose hydrogels (Figure  5b). Conversely, many C2C12 cells spread on the PGCNSH, and electrical stimulation further increased the spreading of cells on the PGCNSH. The results of the MTT assay indicated that the C2C12 cells highly proliferated on PGCNSH (Figure  5c). The proliferation of the cells increased at a low stimulation voltage $(300~\mathrm{mV})$ , but decreased at a high stimulation voltage $(900\mathrm{mV})$ ). These results indicated that the conductive PGCNSH and external electrical stimulation synergistically affected cell behavior.  The influence of the PGCNSH and electrical stimulation on cell adhesion was then characterized based on vinculin, a membrane-cytoskeletal protein in focal adhesion plaques, using a focal adhesion staining kit. As shown in Figure 5d, less vinculin staining was observed on the pure cellulose hydrogel. On the PGCNSH, focal adhesions of the C2C12 cells were much greater, and vinculin was not only distributed near the nucleus but also widely dispersed in the cytoplasm adhered to the hydrogel surface. The quantification of vinculin fluorescence intensity further indicated a higher intensity for C2C12 cells on PGCNSH than for cells on pure cellulose hydrogel. Upon electrical stimulation, the focal adhesion of the cells on PGCNSH was further enhanced (Figure S20, Supporting Information).  The differentiation of the C2C12 cells on the hydrogels was investigated by culturing cells-seeded hydrogels in a differentiation medium with different electrical stimulation voltages. After a 4-day incubation, the formation of myotubes was visualized using phalloidin-stained F-actin (green), an indicator for differentiating C2C12 cells. The C2C12 cells on the pure cellulose hydrogel remained round in shape, and no myotubes were formed (Figure  5e). Contrarily, the C2C12 cells on the PGCNSH exhibited elongated morphology and began to form myotube. Electrical stimulation markedly affected the myotube formation in C2C12 cells cultured on PGCNSH (Figure  5f).  These results suggest that the PGCNSH could serve as a cell stimulator to spatiotemporally manipulate cell behaviors, including cell morphology, proliferation, elongation, and differentiation under optimal external electrical signals. First, PGCNSH with good cell affinity facilitates better cellular attachment and spreading than pure cellulose hydrogels, which is a great improvement compared with that of previously reported cellulose hydrogels that lack of binding sites for cell attachment.[41] This is because the catechol groups of the PGCNSH provide active anchorage for C2C12 cells attachment and consequently promote cell-matrix interactions, which is essential for the formation of focal adhesion complexes.[42] Additionally, the catechol groups can adsorb the serum proteins with native configuration onto the hydrogel surfaces,[43,44] further enhancing cell adhesion and spreading. Second, appropriate electrical voltage provides physical stimulation on the cell proliferation and differentiation processes by increasing the synthesis of the protein and DNA in cells.[45–49] However, the electrical voltage should not be excessively high $(>900\mathrm{mV}$ in this study), because a high electrical voltage is harmful to cell behavior. Previous studies have demonstrated that a high electrical voltage intensity can induce cell apoptosis or death by causing electroporation of the cell membrane, thereby increasing the amount of intracellular $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ and reactive oxygen species that are toxic to cells.[48–52] In summary, upon adequate electrical stimulation $(\approx300~\mathrm{mV})$ , the PGCNSH strengthens the effect of electrical signals because the interconnected conductive network formed by the PGC bio-nanosheets inside the PGCNSH provides short circuit for the electrical current to pass through neighboring electrodes, and thereby spatiotemporally imparting biochemical and biophysical cues to manipulate cell behavior. Consequently, the electrical stimulation and PGCNSH synergistically enhance the myoblast maturation.
