) rich in small-sidechain amino acids Ala, Gly $(\approx60-75\%)$ and to a variable extent Gln, Ser, Pro, Tyr, and Arg.[6] The major ampullate (MA) silk proteins that comprise dragline silk are highly dynamic, intrinsically disordered proteins as stored in the MA silk gland prior to fiber spinning.[7] In the final spun fibers, the $\mathfrak{p o l y}(\mathrm{Ala})_{n}$ $(n=4-9)$ ) and flanking (Gly-Ala) repeats assemble into $\beta$ -sheet nanocrystallites $({\approx}2{-}7\mathrm{nm})^{\left[8\right]}$ dispersed throughout a Gly-Gly-X $\textstyle(\mathrm{X}=\operatorname{Gln}$ , Tyr, Pro, Ser, and Arg)-rich matrix that is non- $\cdot\beta$ -sheet and best described as a disordered helix that is not $\alpha$ -helical.[9] Aciniform spidroin 1 (AcSp1), the primary protein making up spider wrapping silk, is markedly different than dragline silk and is composed of a string of 14–20 identical subunits (depending on species) of about 200 amino acids (called a W unit for wrapping), flanked by nonrepetitive N- and C-terminal regions (Figure 1).[10] Solution-state NMR studies on a single recombinantly produced AcSp1 wrapping unit suggest that each 200 amino acid W subunit folds into a multidomain structure composed of a well-defined helix-rich globular domain and a glycine/serine-rich disordered linker region.[11] It was proposed that each W subunit forms a “beads on a string” model when the protein is stored in the gland.[11b] Synthetic versions of AC silk fibers have previously been generated from an aqueous dope in order to study the mechanical properties imparted by different buffers as well as differences in the number of W subunits.[12] Although recombinant AcSp1 W subunits have been well characterized as recombinant protein constructs in a solubilized state, a molecular-level understanding of the native AC silk protein after conversion to insoluble fiber is quite minimal. Recently, we applied solid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (SSNMR) techniques to isotopically-enriched native prey-wrapping silks and quantitatively correlated the secondary protein structures of Ala, Ser, and Val residues to the primary protein sequence of the W subunit. We proposed a hybrid $\alpha$ -helical coiled-coil $\upbeta$ -sheet hierarchical model for A. argentata and A. aurantia spider prey-wrapping silk fibers, in which 1) the globular helix-rich domains (bead) mostly retain their helical structures in the final fiber, 2) where the $\mathrm{Ala/}$ Ser-rich motifs from the string/linker region aggregate into $\beta$ -sheet nanostructures, and 3) a significant fraction of the protein remains disordered.[13] We intuitively argued that the high extensibility of AC silk fibers is a direct result of high $\alpha$ -helical content, while its moderate mean ultimate strength arises from $\beta$ -sheet nanostructures and possibly coiled-coil interactions between fiber-aligned helices.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Freshly-collected AC silk is shown as a dense mesh of fine $(\approx500\mathrm{nm}$ in diameter) fibers (Figure 2c). When we attempted to image silk on a wrapped and consumed prey (cricket), we were surprised to observe clear morphological changes to regions of the silk. Dense bundles of individual AC fibers remain, but areas of wrapping silk fibers appear to have fused together into fibrous sheets or mats, especially in regions where the silk makes direct contact with the cricket. Individual fibers clearly have fused together to form larger, uniaxial fibers and fibrous mats. Additionally, interfiber space appears to have collapsed. To determine the effect of water on AC fiber morphology, native prey-wrapping silk was briefly soaked in deionized water for $2\textrm{h}$ without mechanical agitation and dried for 1 day. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of our waterwetted silk bundles confirm that this observed cross-linking property is water-induced. The effect of hydration on silk structure has been investigated for several silk-based materials including native silk fibers, recombinant silk protein films, and peptides.[15] The effect of water on protein secondary structure has been conclusive, and results in an increase in $\beta$ sheet content of the materials, including silkworm silk fibers.[15a,c] However, these changes occur within a single fiber, not between adjacent strands. After allowing our AC silk fibers to air-dry, the material became noticeably stiffer and was no longer malleable like the native silk fiber. It is possible that other factors, such as dissolved salts and pH used in the native (wrapped cricket) fibers, may fine tune the local silk fusion and aggregation to minimize fluid loss from the prey. The appearance of nearly identical morphological structures in both native and watertreated samples, however, leads us to the conclusion that AC silk protein–water interactions drive the mechanism for fiber fusion and cross-linking. Finally, the fiber fusion and crosslinking process occurs rapidly with similar observations made for AC silks water treated for $2\textrm{h}$ and 30 s (Figure 2c).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): In summary, we have shown that prey-wrapping silks from the garden spider A. argentata undergo a remarkable cross-linking behavior when exposed to moisture that has not been observed in other silks to the best of our knowledge. Upon contact with water, SEM images reveal that individual silk fibers fuse together to form cross-linked fibrous sheets and mats. Comprehensive solid-state NMR data on $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ -enriched wrapping silks has uncovered the molecular mechanism of inter-fiber crosslinking. Orthogonal isotopic enrichment schemes (Ala-labeling and Val-labeling) were chosen to understand these structural changes for Ala, Ser, Gly, and Val residues, shedding light on which regions of the primary protein sequence are most susceptible to water-induced $\beta$ sheet crosslinking. Overall, the data reveal that water-induced fiber cross-linking is driven by an increase in $\beta$ sheet protein secondary structure at the expense of disordered and loosely-structured $\alpha$ -helical motifs, whereas wellordered coiled-coil structures remain in-tact for the most part. However, helical coiled-coil motifs also undergo partial $\alpha$ -to- $\boldsymbol{\cdot}\beta$ conversion with the addition of mechanical shear. This behavior is also seen in other structural protein-based biopolymers. For example, keratin has been shown to convert from $\alpha$ -to- $\cdot\beta$ when stretched in the presence of moisture.[28] Finally, WISE NMR data were used to estimate the $\beta$ sheet domain sizes and overall protein dynamics within water-hydrated wrapping silks. Even after water-induced crosslinking, $\beta$ -sheet regions within AC silks are smaller $({\approx}4-6~\mathrm{nm})$ ) than those found in BW dragline fibers $({\approx}11\mathrm{nm})$ ), suggesting that newly-formed $\beta$ -sheet structures are small in size and likely between two or more protein chains and potentially involving multiple fibers. Figure 6 shows a proposed model to illustrate the structural changes of AC silks after water treatment. The data support our prior conclusions that at a minimum there are three distinct domains in wrapping silks: 1) disordered regions that are easily plasticized by water, 2) rigid $\alpha$ -helices from highly-stable coiled-coil motifs, and 3) Ala and Ser-rich $\beta$ sheets are present but surprisingly minimal. Future work will further divulge the role of water in this process, turning a flexible silk fiber into a hardened matted sheet.
