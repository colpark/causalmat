- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Growth of Homogeneous zz-GNR Ensembles. Figure 1a displays a typical scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image of the mesa-structured SiC sample covered with a dense array of GNRs of around $10^{5}~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ . The sidewall zz-GNRs appear as bright lines on both facets of the mesa structures running parallel to the zz direction. In addition, the basal plane is partially covered by islands of dark contrast, which indicate the formation of epitaxial monolayer graphene.31 Statistical evaluation of the SEM contrast level distribution showed that the shares of GNRs and (parasitic) monolayer graphene (MLG) in the surface area amount to ${\sim}35\%$ and ${\sim}8\%.$ , respectively. These MLG patches are beneficial for the calibration of diffraction and photoemission intensities stemming from faceted areas (see the Supporting Information (SI)).  The profile shown in the inset of Figure 1b was taken along the orange line highlighted in the 2D topography of the mesa array (Figure 1b). Each of the mesa structures is $20\mathrm{nm}$ in height, and the pitch size amounts to $150~\mathrm{nm}$ . The sidewalls, which host the zz-GNRs (see the STM data below), are $30{-}40$ nm wide and exhibit a facet angle $(\alpha)$ of ${\sim}27^{\circ}$ , in good agreement with structural parameters obtained on previous micrometer-pitched mesa structures.5,22,28 Compared with the former studies, the total zz-GNR coverage is now considerably higher and was characterized in detail with diffraction techniques (see Figure S1 for more details of the diffraction analysis). Moreover, we performed high-resolution angleresolved photoelectron spectroscopy (ARPES), which revealed characteristic $\pi$ bands of monolayer graphene and confirmed the growth of suspended ribbons across the SiC facets (a detailed analysis of the bands is shown in SI section 2).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The morphology and in particular the electronic structure of the edges of zz-GNRs were investigated in detail by STM, scanning tunneling spectroscopy (STS), and also transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The 3D representation of a largescan STM image in Figure 2a shows a GNR crossing the entire SiC facet. This continuous overgrowth of atomic-sized steps on the SiC facets was reported earlier5,24,32 and corroborated by cross-sectional TEM measurements, shown in Figure 2b. A high-resolution image of the central part of the GNR is shown in Figure 2c. The image clearly reveals atomic resolution of the graphene lattice on top of a modulated background. The STS spectra taken in this area (cf. Figure 2d) on various zz-GNR samples are symmetrical, which indicates charge-neutral graphene. In addition, around the Fermi level, strong suppression of spectral weight and partly even gaplike features were found. The variations of the STS spectra are attributed to the slight inhomogeneity in the morphology, as can be observed in Figure 2c, as well as to variations of the average width. According to our tight binding calculations, a gap of around $120\mathrm{meV}$ is expected for a freestanding and $40~\mathrm{nm}$ wide GNR with perfect edges. Moreover, while the electronic gap is quite robust against variations of the average width, disorder easily smears out clear signatures of the bulk quantum well states (QWSs) (see SI section 5). The bulk QWS channels were previously identified and studied by surface transport experiments.24  Figure 2e shows a high-resolution STM image of the lower edge. Here the atomic lattice of graphene ends abruptly, as indicated by the dotted line. As the GNR is located on an inclined part of the surface, scanning of the edge is challenging and limited by the 3D apex structure of the tip (cf. Figure 2b inset). The abrupt disappearance of the graphene lattice implies that the edge of the GNR itself merges into the SiC substrate, as is apparent from our TEM images and already reported before33,34 (see SI section 6 for more information). It is obvious from Figure 2a that the edge is slightly meandering on a length scale of $5{-}10~\mathrm{nm}$ with an amplitude of $1{-}2\mathrm{nm}$ . On small scales, this edge appears atomically straight. For example, as shown in Figure 2e, the edge orientation is slightly misaligned with respect to the zigzag orientation. Thus, this ribbon has a chiral nature, and the bonding induces a curvature at the bottom of the GNR. The edge morphology influences the electronic properties of the GNR itself. Figure 2f presents STS spectra taken at the lower edge of various zz-GNRs. Most obviously, a peak is found at the Fermi energy $\left(E_{\mathrm{F}}\right)_{-}$ , which we call a topological 1D surface state; this state likely provides the ballistic and spin-polarized transport channel.22,35 The sequence of spectra shown in Figure 2f taken on the lower edge of different ribbons reveals a surface state at $E_{\mathrm{F}}$ .

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): A part of the band structure of a zz-GNR terminated by a zigzag edge as calculated with the presented tight binding model is shown in Figure 4a. For computational reasons, the calculation of the edge properties was done for a $20~\mathrm{nm}$ wide ribbon. Our findings do not change if the width is doubled (SI section 4). A surface state crossing the Fermi level is seen, with a finite bandwidth introduced by the Haldane term in the Hamiltonian. The energy of the surface state is plotted as a function of the transverse position of the surface state in Figure 4b. The Haldane surface state exhibits a splitting around the Dirac energy with increasing distance from the edge, corresponding to the experimentally observed states shown in Figure 3c. The splitting that this state exhibits in the transverse direction is in contrast with the zero-energy (2d surface) state, which appears on the zigzag edge without the complex next-nearest-neighbor hopping term, which decays exponentially toward the bulk and does not split at all. Although the splitting happens almost entirely close to the edge, the addition of a strain gradient widens the distance in which the state splits, closely agreeing with the splitting as measured with STS. This strain gradient was constructed in such a way that a large strain gradient is present near the edge, in addition to a global constant compressive strain in the transverse direction. The strain profile, which was fitted to the observed splitting, is a very rough estimate of the strain present in the actual sample, and the strain is purely incorporated with the modification of the nearest-neighbor hopping parameter using a method founded on empirical fits. Hence, the results obtained from the model should be viewed in a qualitative context, in which undeniably the right trend appears.  The Haldane surface state has a nonzero Chern number (SI section 4), providing an explanation for the robust ballistic transport that these ribbons exhibit.22 A notable difference between the measured and calculated spectra is the hard band gap observable in the calculated spectra, whereas the measured spectra exhibit a V-shaped gap. This could possibly be attributed to the fact that the tight binding Hamiltonian is a single-electron description, neglecting electron−electron interactions. Our toy model serves purely as an illustration of the presence of a chiral, topologically protected surface state, which exhibits a real-space dispersion that matches the splitting observed in the STS spectra. However, as TRS breaking is artificially introduced, this raises the question of how it can be broken in the ribbon without an applied magnetic field.
