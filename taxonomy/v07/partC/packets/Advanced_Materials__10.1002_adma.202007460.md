- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): An addressable solution for the aforementioned “silicon anode challenge” might be proposed by considering the volumetric behavior of silicon anode in terms of the gradually changing microenvironment settings of each individual silicon particle during charge/discharge cycles. 1) Silicon nanoparticles (or microparticles) are randomly and homogeneously distributed in silicon/ carbon/binder anode composites (the spheres in Scheme  1). 2) Subsequently, upon charging, volume expansion of the silicon particles occurs at each position of the individual nanoparticle, gradually setting its Si-micro-environments, that is, the “Si-μ-env,” during early cycles $(\approx20$ times) (Scheme  1b–i). 3) Later, the Si- $\mu$ -env created during the few early cycles of particle expansion will be “fixed and set” rather permanently by repeating cycles of charge/discharge afterward (red, Scheme 1j,m). This environment transition from early to later cycles can also be correlated with the swelling of an electrode. In the early cycles where Si- $\mu$ -env evolves while the drastic volume change of Si occurs and the SEI layer is newly formed in each cycle, the electrode swelling is more pronounced compared to the later cycles where Si- $\mu$ -env settles and the electrode swelling gradually saturates.  What chemical properties are required to fulfill the aforementioned tasks? Ideal binders should exhibit transitions showing “adaptive properties” that are suitable both at early stages and at subsequent cycles. In early cycling stages, each binder should be repositioned and reoriented upon gradual formation of the Si- $\mu$ -env via reversible noncovalent interactions (dark gray conjugates in Scheme 1). In contrast, after the early stages, the binder polymers should be covalently cured in an irreversible manner because the Si- $\mu$ -env is stably set and determined (red conjugates). For this process, we employed unique, plant-inspired phenol chemistry. Gallol (GA) moieties (Scheme 1), which have a chemical structure of three hydroxyl groups attached to aromatic ring, are found in vegetables, fruits, tea leaves, and cacao. In particular, gallol moieties show robust binding affinity to nearly all biomacromolecules of proteins and nucleic acids (DNA or RNA) via noncovalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions.[18,19] Additionally, the gallol moiety is adhesive to versatile material surfaces including silicon particles.[25–29] The gallol moiety exhibits smart characteristics in chemical binding transitions from noncovalent to covalent interactions. In detail, it has been reported that a polymer–gallol conjugate showed immediate gelation with reversible binding interactions so that the gallol gels showed shear-thinning behavior.[20] Later, gallol-to-quinone oxidation occurs slowly yet spontaneously, resulting in irreversible gallol-to-gallol crosslinking.[21] Thus, gallol-tethered polymeric binders can solve the aforementioned adaptive properties during battery cycles.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): We prepared three gallol (GA)-conjugated polymeric binders: hyaluronic acid (HA)-GA, alginate (Alg)-GA, and carboxy­ methyl cellulose (CMC)-GA. $\mathrm{Alg}^{[12,13]}$ and CMC[8,9] are chosen because of their widespread use in silicon anodes. However, we chose a new polysaccharide binder, HA, as we hypothesized that the good backbone flexibility of HAs might be a suitable property upon expansion of the silicon particles. The superior flexibility of HA allows its use in cosmetic fillers,[22] lubricants,[23] and soft hydrogels.[24] The gallol (GA) moieties were conjugated with a similar degree of substitution (Figure  1a). The carboxyl groups on each polymeric backbone activated by 1-ethyl-3-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)carbodiimide $\left(\mathrm{EDC}\right)/N.$ hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) chemistry reacted with amine groups in gallol-containing 5-hydroxydopamine under weakly acidic conditions $\mathrm{(pH~}5.0\mathrm{)}$ (Figure S1a, Supporting Information). The degree of substitution for the gallol tethered was calculated using the absorbance $(\mathrm{A}_{275})$ measured by a UV– vis spectrometer (Figure S1b, Supporting Information). The values were $11.6\%$ for HA–GA (red), $9.1\%$ for Alg-GA (blue), and $10.2\%$ for CMC-GA (black). The degree of substitution was also analyzed by $^1\mathrm{H}$ NMR spectroscopy (Figure S1c, Supporting Information). The relative integration of the peaks of four ethyl protons adjacent to the gallols was calculated on a basis of ten protons on HA backbone. The value was $11.5\%$ , corresponding to the calculated value, $11.6\%$ , through the UV–vis spectroscopic analysis.  To evaluate the capacities of the silicon anodes by formulating different binders of HA–GA, Alg-GA, or CMC-GA, we monitored the discharge capacities $(\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}})$ up to 300 cycles. Importantly, a high rate of 1 C was applied throughout the 300 cycles (Figure  1b) considering well-known studies with low rates from $0.05\mathrm{~C~}$ to $0.5\:\mathrm{C}$ .[9–13,15,27–29] For all three cases, the use of gallol-tethered binders (closed circles, Figure  1b) in silicon anodes showed capacity increases when compared to that of unmodified polymers (open triangles, Figure 1b). The capacity increase of those unmodified polymers during initial cycles (8th–11th cycle for HA, 8th–14th for Alg, and 6th–9th for CMC) might be due to certain activation processes related to their hydrogen bond formation with silicon nanoparticles (SiNPs).[30] However, interestingly, the discharge capacity increase was noticeably high for the HA–GA anode $\mathrm{(1498mAhg^{-1}}$ red filled circles, Figure  1b) compared to that of HA alone $(638\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}})$ . The delta $(\Delta)$ value (GA vs no GA tethering) in the HA backbone was significantly higher than that of Alg $(\Delta=332\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}})$ or CMC $(\Delta=354\mathrm{{mAhg^{-1}})}$ . We hypothesized that the increase $(\Delta)$ is due to the superior flexibility of the HA backbone compared to the other backbones. In fact, the persistence length of HA is just $5~\mathrm{nm}$ , whereas Alg and CMC have long persistence lengths: $15~\mathrm{nm}$ for $\mathrm{Alg}$ and $6\mathrm{nm}$ for CMC (Figure  1c).[31–36] Experimentally, we further demonstrated the unique flexibility of HA by tensile tests using a universal testing machine (UTM). We prepared each binder film with an area of $1\times2~\mathrm{cm}^{2}$ by evaporation at room temperature (Figure 1d). Each film was pulled with a speed of $5\mathrm{mm}\mathrm{min}^{-1}$ , and the HA film showed unparalleled extensibility to $1.63~\mathrm{mm}$ (red), which was three times higher than that of the other films: $0.56~\mathrm{mm}$ for Alg (blue) and $0.66~\mathrm{mm}$ for CMC (black). The flexibility and extensibility of HA might be critical properties for adaptation to the Si- $\mu$ -env by repositioning and reorientation during the early stages of charge/discharge cycles. Thus, the flexibility and extensibility of the HA–GA binder can contribute to the prevention of electrode pulverization, indicating that polymer flexi­ bility is an important factor in designing silicon anode binders. In addition, we designed a series of experiments to prove the importance of gallol conjugations. Note in Figure  1b that the discharge capacities were all increased by the presence of gallol conjugations independent of the choice of binder. Therefore, further studies were carried out with the HA–GA binder because it showed the largest delta $(\Delta)$ value in the capacity increase, as shown in Figure  1b. Previous studies have reported that gallol moieties strongly interact via noncovalent interactions, such as hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions, with a variety of hydroxyl group-rich biopolymers such as proteins and nucleic acids.[18,19] Thus, we first hypothesized that the gallol moieties show differential backbone binding forces depending on the chemistry of the polysaccharides; this is an important aspect for assessing the adaptive properties (the concept explained above) of a binder affecting stable network formation in the early stage of the Si- $\cdot\mu$ -env formation (Scheme  1b–1i and the top panel in Figure 2a). To investigate the intermolecular interactions between the gallols and polymeric backbones, surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis was performed (Figure 2b). SPR analysis has been typically used for demonstrating noncovalent interactions of biomolecules, including antigen−antibody binding, protein–polymer interaction, and polyphenol–protein interactions.[37–42] On the surface of gold sensor chips, gallol moieties (5-hydroxydopamine) were surface-immobilized, upon which each polysaccharide binder (HA, Alg, or CMC) solution was injected (Figure 2b). HA showed the highest affinity to the gallol-adsorbed sensor: 303 RU for HA (red arrow), 78 RU for Alg (blue), and 12 RU for CMC (black). The results demonstrate that the strong binding affinity of gallol to HA contributes to the early setting for the Si- $\mu$ -env, improving the electrochemical performance of the LIBs. Furthermore, the gallol moieties’ noncovalent interactions (i.e., hydrogen bonds) with SiNPs were detected by Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy and rheological analysis (Figure S2, Supporting Information). In the FT-IR result in Figure S2a, Supporting Information, the gallol hydroxyl $(-\mathrm{OH})$ groups were assigned at $3442~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ (vibrational mode, black). However, the vibrational peak in the presence of SiNPs (weight ratio of SiNPs to $\mathrm{HA-GA}=3\$ ) was shifted to $3393~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ (red). In addition, the $\mathrm{C}{-}\mathrm{O}$ stretching vibrational peak of alcohol/ether in $_\mathrm{HA-GA^{[17]}}$ observed at $1045~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ was shifted to $1038~\mathrm{cm^{-1}}$ , and the strong intensity of the peak at $1650~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ indicated that the ${\mathrm{C}}{=}0$ stretching of the quinone-bearing structure[43] was decreased, shifting to $1648~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ (red dashed line). These shifts were caused by gallol/ SiNPs hydrogen bond formation.[17,20,43,44] In addition, the complex viscosity of the HA–GA/SiNPs $22.89\mathrm{~Pa~s~}$ at $1.0~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ ) was one or two-order of magnitude higher than that of unmodified HA/SiNPs solutions $(1.32\mathrm{~Pa~s~}$ at $1.0~\mathrm{s}^{-1})$ , indicating binding affinity of the gallol moieties to SiNPs (Figure S2b, Supporting Information). For further discussion, carbon collectors (e.g., Super $\mathrm{P}$ of commercialized carbon materials)—which consist of stacked graphite—are utilized as additives in the anode. The aromatic rings of the gallol binder could have additional interactions with the carbon addtives through $\pi{-}\pi$ stacking/ hydrophobic interactions (Figure S2c, Supporting Information), exhibiting enhancement of viscosity in the HA–GA/Super P mixture (0.45 Pa s at $1.0~\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ for HA/Super $\mathrm{~P~}\to20.48$ Pa s for HA–GA/Super P).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Considering together the FT-IR, rheological behavior and SPR results, HA–GA exhibited not only strong binder-tobinder interactions but also strong binder-to-SiNPs adhesion, providing superior adaptation capability upon cycle-by-cycle gradual Si- $\mu$ -env formation.  Up to this stage, HA–GA binder interactions surrounding the Si- $\mu$ -env are mostly hydrogen bonds. An advantage of hydrogen bonds is the adaptability of the Si- $\mu$ -env during the early stages of expansion–shrinkage cycles of SiNPs (Scheme 1b–1i). However, hydrogen bonds are weak and reversible and are not suitable chemical interactions for several hundred long cycles of charge and discharge. The unique chemistry of a gallol moiety is its noncovalent-to-covalent linkage conversion. Oxidation of gallols generates galloquinone intermediates that subsequently form gallol-to-gallol covalent linkages (red lines, Figure  2a).[21] Thus, we also hypothesized that the gradual formation of gallolto-gallol covalent crosslinking has “setting and curing” effects for the Si-μ-env formed in earlier cycles, which is a smart adaptable characteristic of HA–GA binders (Scheme 1j–1m).  To test our hypothesis, we monitored gelation of HA–GA chains as a function of time. We measured rheological changes in HA–GA solutions after incubation for $^{4\textrm{h}}$ (black, Figure 2c) or $120\mathrm{~h~}$ (red). At an early stage of $^{4\textrm{h}}$ of incubation, the solphase of HA–GA was present, resulting in a viscous modulus ( $G^{\prime\prime}=3.2\mathrm{~P}\mathfrak{c}$ at $1\mathrm{Hz}$ [empty black circle]) that was higher than the elastic modulus ( $G^{\prime}=0.8$ Pa at $1\mathrm{Hz}$ [filled black circle]). However, in a long-term incubation of $120\mathrm{~h~}$ (i.e., 5 days), HA–GA gelation occurred, showing a $G^{\prime}$ value $(93.7\mathrm{~Pa}$ , filled red circle) that was higher than the $G^{\prime\prime}$ value (17.4  Pa, empty red circle) over the entire frequency range. One important remained question is the degree of completion from reversible hydrogen bond to irreversible covalent bond. The rheo­ meter result in Figure 2c indicates that the covalent conversion might not be completed for all tethered gallol moieties, which is a reasonable hypothesis at this stage. In Figure  2c, the $G^{\prime}$ value (red filled symbol) slightly increased over the range of frequency, which differs from the results from the fully covalent crosslinked polymeric chains that retain constant value of $G^{\prime}$ . Thus, the HA–GA might have partial hydrogen bonds even after $120\mathrm{~h~}$ during cell cycles. In particular, the spontaneous gelation of HA–GA was comparable to the rheological behavior of catechol-conjugated ones (Figure S3, Supporting Information). The gelation of catechol-conjugated HA (HA–CA) was not observed even after $120\mathrm{~h~}$ , indicating $G^{\prime}$ value $\mathrm{^{\prime}0.53~P a}$ at $1~\mathrm{Hz}$ ) lower than $G^{\prime\prime}$ value $(1.22\mathrm{~Pa})$ . The result might be also due to weaker interactions of catechol binder with silicon particles and other electrode components (e.g., carbon) than gallol binder.[25]  To characterize the covalent crosslinking formation in detail, the crosslinked products were injected into a liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC/MS) system (Figure 2d). The model compound that we chose was the small molecule propyl gallate because crosslinking analysis by gel permeation chromatography using gallol-conjugated HA polymers typically does not provide clear evidence. Propyl gallate was eluted at $8.6\mathrm{min}$ from the C18 column (Figure  2d, top black), and the corresponding molecular mass of the eluate was 213  m/z $\mathrm{[M+H]^{+}}$ , where M is propyl gallate with a molecular mass value of 212 (Figure 2d, middle). After $48\mathrm{~h~}$ of incubation with propyl gallate under weakly basic conditions $\mathrm{(pH\approx9)}$ , the main peak in the LC experiment appeared at 8.2 min. Corresponding to the mass analysis of the eluate was a dimeric galloquinone form $\left[\mathrm{M}{+}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{q}\right]$ , as shown in Figure  2d (bottom, red structure). The molecular mass of dimeric galloquinone $\left[\mathrm{M}{+}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{q}\right]$ is 420, and mass spectrometry showed protonation and methyl fragmentation, $\begin{array}{r}{[\mathrm{M}{+}\mathrm{M}\mathrm{q}{-}\mathrm{C}\mathrm{H}_{3}{+}\mathrm{H}]^{+}}\end{array}$ , indicating an $m/z$ of 407 (red structure). Thus, these results indicate that gallol crosslinking to the dimeric form occurs to a relatively high degree as a function of time.  Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis focusing on the cross-sectional morphology of the HA–GA/SiNPs mixture showed interconnected, mesh-like structures in the LIB anode (Figure 2e). As shown by the yellow arrows in the bottom image, reversible hydrogen bonding interactions allow HA–GAs to form stable networks not only between binders (i.e., cohesive) but also between binders and SiNPs (i.e., adhesive). The reversible adhesions are suitable for establishing individual SiNPs-specific microenvironments. In contrast, the mixture of SiNPs/HA–GA at $0\mathrm{~h~}$ had no such binder-associated entanglement (top), directly demonstrating that the gallol-mediated reversible interactions and their adhesive forces gradually formed at the early stage of the network (Scheme 1a–1i).

We describe in Figure 3d a new chemical mechanism of gallol moieties to explain how the chemistry effectively prevents the well-known pulverization of the SiNP anode in LIB systems. In an early, adaptive stage, gallol-binder has diverse noncovalent, reversible interactions with SiNPs and other gallols/backbones, which is a pre-stage for establishing the $\mathrm{Si}{\cdot}\mu$ -env (Figure  3d, panel (1)). Subsequently, a structural expansion of the Si anode occurs due to the intercalation of a large amount of lithium ions during a lithiation step (panel (2)). When compared to the common binder of silicon-based LIB systems, the gallol binder can prevent morphological crack formation of the Si anode through the flexible/stretchable properties of its polymeric backbone and its dynamic, reversible interactions with SiNPs. Although the overall structure of the anode reshrinks in the following delithiation step, the gallol binder partially retains its crosslinked network via rapid oxidation (red symbols), providing Si- $\mu$ -env for further lithium exchange (Figure  3d, panel (3)). In later stages of repeated lithiation/delithiation cycles, the generation of Si- $\mu$ -env by the gallol binder continues through an increase in the covalent crosslinking (e.g., conversion of black gallol symbols to red symbols), stabilizing and strengthening the adapted $\mathrm{Si}{\cdot}\mu$ -env to enhance the LIB capacity (panels (4) and (5)).
