- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Niobium Superconducting Electromechanical Transducer. We explore the optomechanical interaction of microwave photons and nanomechanical phonons by fabricating a LC microwave resonator circuit made of superconducting niobium (Figure 1a) where a free-standing, $130~\mathrm{nm}$ -thick nanomechanical resonator forms a vacuum-gap capacitor with a $100~\mathrm{nm}$ -thick bottom electrode (Figure 1b). This membrane resonator is connected to a spiral inductor, and the bottom electrode is connected to the circuit ground. The diameter of the membrane is $12.6\mu\mathrm{m},$ and the bottom electrode is $10\mu\mathrm{m}$ in diameter. The physical mass, $m_{,}$ , of the membrane is ${\sim}140$ pg based on its dimensions. Our device is a typical setup for studying microwave cavity electromechanics $19^{\pm}-21,23,24,33$ via the dispersive electromechanical coupling, which induces the shift of the microwave resonant frequency by the motion of the membrane. The nanomechanical resonator is suspended over the bottom electrode with a $130~\mathrm{nm}$ vacuum gap by releasing a sacrificial layer of polymethyl-methacrylate (PMMA) (Figure 1c and Supporting Information). To characterize the microwave and mechanical responses of our device, we perform all the measurements at the liquid helium temperature $(\sim4.2\mathrm{~K~})$  (Figure 1d). The microwave resonator is capacitively coupled to the input and the output ports to drive and measure the responses of the device. From a microwave transmission measured with a network analyzer, we obtain $\omega_{c}$ $(\approx2\pi\times3.777$ $\operatorname{GHz})$ and the total decay rate of the microwave resonator, $\kappa$ $(\approx\kappa_{o}+\kappa_{\mathrm{ext}}=2\pi\times960\mathrm{kHz})$ with intrinsic loss $\kappa_{o}$ $\stackrel{\prime}{\approx}2\pi\times330$ kHz) and external loss $\kappa_{\mathrm{ext}}$ $\'\approx2\pi\times640~\mathrm{kHz})$ (Figure 1e, Figure S1, and see Supporting Information for their evaluation). We characterize the mechanical responses by measuring a noise spectrum around $\omega_{c}$ . This spectrum results from the frequency up-conversion of the thermomechanical noise around $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$ when the device is excited by a red-detuned microwave drive at $\omega_{\mathrm{d}}=\omega_{\mathrm{c}}-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$ . This microwave drive is weak enough to induce negligible optomechanical back-action effects. We also obtain $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$ $\approx2\pi\times8.4\:\mathrm{MHz})$ and the intrinsic mechanical damping rate, $\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}$ $\langle\approx2\pi\times810~\mathrm{Hz})$ (Figure 1f). Note that this $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$ corresponds to the fundamental flexural mode of the membrane, as the bottom electrode provides an isotropic electrostatic force distribution. The ratio of $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/\kappa\sim9$ implies that our system is in the sideband resolved regime, meaning that only one of the two sidebands (Stokes or antiStokes) is dominant, depending on $\Delta$ .

To investigate the optomechanically induced behavior of our nanomechanical resonator, we excite our device in the red-detuned (blue-detuned) regime and measure the total mechanical displacement noise spectrum, $S_{x}(\omega)_{\cdot}$ , of the up-converted (down-converted) sideband signals. To see the effects of the dynamic back-action in our electromechanical device, we vary the detuning frequency, $\Delta=-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}+\delta,$ from $\delta$ $\c=-1$ to $1~\mathrm{MHz}$ (Figure S2). The signal-to-noise ratio (SNR), $S_{x}^{m e c h}(\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})/S_{x}^{b g}(\bar{\Omega}_{m})$ , also changes with increasing $n_{\mathrm{d}},$ manifesting the interplay of the thermomechanical noise, the measurement imprecision, and the dynamic back-action (Figure S3a). We obtain the best force sensitivity of 400 $\mathrm{{aN/Hz^{1/2}}}$ at $n_{\mathrm{d}}~\approx~4~\times~10^{6}$ , which is limited by the thermomechanical noise (Figure S3b). On the other hand, down-converted $S_{x}(\omega)$ at $\Delta=\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$ shows line width narrowing with increasing peak amplitudes, indicating the enhancement of quality factor $Q$ (Figure 2b). We perform quantitative analyses of $S_{x}(\omega)$ and extract phonon numbers of our mechanical resonator (see Supporting Information). The thermal occupation factor of the nanomechanical mode at $T_{\mathrm{bath}}=4.17\mathrm{~K~}$ is $n_{\mathrm{ph}}=[\mathrm{exp}(\hbar\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}/k_{\mathrm{B}}T_{\mathrm{bath}})-1]^{-1}\approx1.04\times10^{4}.$ In the red-detuned regime $\left(\Delta=-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\right)$ , we obtain $n_{\mathrm{ph}}\approx188$ at the maximum drive power $(n_{\mathrm{d}}\approx6.5\times10^{8})$ (Figure 2c). We also extract the single-photon coupling rate, $g_{0}$ $\phantom{0}{\approx}2\pi\times3.3$ $\operatorname{Hz},$ , from this cooling experiment (see Supporting Information). Though this value is below the one obtained from aluminum-based electromechanical systems ,19 it is large enough to enable back-action effects. The coupling can be further enhanced by increasing the microwave resonant frequency and decreasing the vacuum gap distance. The ability to tune $Q$ and measure the nanomechanical motion simultaneously is a unique aspect and could be harnessed in nanomechanical sensors to achieve enhanced SNR.34

Engineering the transmission of microwave signals is also another distinct feature of superconducting electromechanical devices. 24−27 A related phenomenon to this is the electromechanically induced transparency24,25 (EMIT), which is analogous to the electromagnetically induced transparency found in atom-based cavity quantum electrodynamics.35 EMIT leads to a transparency window for the probe signals near the microwave resonant frequency $\left(\omega_{\mathrm{c}}\right)$ . The probe signal in addition to the reddetuned drive $\mathbf{\rho}^{\prime}\Delta=\mathbf{\rho}-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}})$ resonantly excites the nanomechanical motion. This driven nanomechanical motion induces the anti-Stokes sideband of the red-detuned drive around the microwave resonant frequency, resulting in destructive interference with the probe signal and the onset of the transmission window. In our device, the two-port geometry leads to electromechanically induced reflection (EMIR), which denotes a reflection window in the transmission resonance (Figure 3a). In the resolved sideband limit, the transmission spectrum of the probe signal for a two-port electromechanical device is formulated by considering the Heisenberg picture of the linearized optomechanical Hamiltonian in the rotating frame at $\omega_{\mathrm{d}}.^{12,21}$ The equation for the transmission $\mathcal{T}$ is given by  where $\Delta_{\mathrm{p}}=\omega_{\mathrm{p}}-\omega_{\mathrm{d}}$ is the probe detuning. $\kappa_{R}$ and $\kappa_{L}$ denote the input and output couplings and equal to $\kappa_{\mathrm{ext}}/2$ . We learn from this equation that the line width and depth of the reflection window strongly depend on the drive power, $n_{\mathrm{d}}$ . To systematically study the EMIR effect in our device, we measure the transmission spectrum of a weak probe signal at $\omega_{\mathfrak{p}}$ with the red-detuned drive $\left(\Delta=-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\right)$ , while we vary $n_{\mathrm{d}}$ from $2\times$ $10^{4}$ to $6.5\times10^{8}$ (Figure 3b). The line width of the reflection window, $\Gamma_{\mathrm{EMIR}}$ corresponds to the damping rate of our nanomechanical resonator and broadens up to $32~\mathrm{kHz}$ at the maximum $n_{\mathrm{d}}$ (Figure 3c). Niobium is also expected to enable reliable operation of superconducting electromechanical devices in magnetic fields, which could enable studying and harnessing, for example, spin−phonon interactions 29−32 in hybrid quantum devices. To support this idea, we study the responses of our electromechanical device to an external magnetic field, B. We apply the field in the direction parallel to the device and measure microwave transmission while varying $B$ from 0 to 0.8 T, with 0.089 T steps (Figure $4\mathsf{a}$ ). We find that $\omega_{c}$ decreases from 3.778 GHz $\overset{\prime}{B}=0\mathrm{~T~},$ ) to $3.744~\mathrm{GHz}$ ( $\overset{\cdot}{B}=0.8\mathrm{~T~},$ and $\kappa$ increases from $960~\mathrm{kHz}$ $\overset{\vartriangle}{\boldsymbol{B}}=0\mathrm{~T~},$ ) to $31~\mathrm{MHz}$ $\overset{\prime}{B}=0.8\mathrm{~T~}$ ) (Figure 4b). This increase of $\kappa$ mainly originates from quasiparticle generation in niobium.36 $^{36}\kappa$ becomes comparable to $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$ around $0.5\mathrm{~T~}$ and starts to exceed $\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}$ . In this regime, the resolved sideband approximation becomes irrelevant as the contribution of the Stokes sideband is not negligible. To characterize electromechanical responses, we measure the EMIR spectra for different $B$ by driving the device in the reddetuned regime $\left(\Delta=-\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}\right)$ . The highest $B$ at which we observe the EMIR effect is $0.8\mathrm{~T~}$ with $A_{\mathrm{EMIR}}$ of ${\sim}0.4$ dB at $n_{\mathrm{d}}\approx$ $3.35\times10^{7}$ (Figure $4c$ ). Even at $B=0.8\mathrm{~T~}$ , we still observe a non-negligible electromechanical back-action damping effect with $C~\sim~0.46$ . $\Gamma_{\mathrm{EMIR}}$ exhibits reduced electromechanical effects as $B$ increases (Figure 4d). This mainly originates from the decrease of the single-photon electromechanical cooperativity, $C_{0}={4g_{0}}^{2}/(\Gamma_{\mathrm{m}}\kappa)$ , due to the increase of $\kappa$ . To check this, we estimate $g_{0}$ for different B from $C_{0},$ and it remains almost constant up to $0.62\mathrm{~T~}$ and starts to decrease as the resolved sideband approximation becomes invalid (Figure 4d inset). Based on the data obtained from the EMIR experiments, we also estimate the cooling performance of our niobium device in terms of $n_{\mathrm{ph}}$ for different $B$ and observe its decrease as $B$ increases (Figure S5).
