- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To address these fundamental questions, face-centered cubic (fcc) high-entropy alloys (HEAs) were chosen because of the strong bubble formation resistance, i.e., the inhibited growth of HPHNBs [20-24], and the enhanced radiation tolerance compared to pure metals [25-27]. The sluggish helium diffusion nature of fcc HEAs allows a wide process window for helium ion implantation to prepare HPHNBs [28]. Moreover, fcc HEAs have extensive mechanical and functional applications at small dimensions, including mechanical metamaterials [29,30], catalysts [31,32] and soft magnets [33]. Additionally, we envisage that introducing self-growing HPHNBs into fcc HEAs could generate internal stress fluctuation, in addition to the fluctuations originated from the chemical short-range order [34] and local atomic strain [35], to tailor the nanoscale defect structures and deformation behavior. Thus, this strategy might add a new dimension to enhance the strengthductility synergy of fcc HEAs, beyond the metallurgical approaches via tuning the phase metastability [36], second-phase nanoparticles [37] and concentration fluctuations[38].  Here, we successfully prepared sub- $4\mathrm{nm}$ HPHNBs in FeNiCoCr HEA, a prototypical alloy for fcc HEAs [29], via controllable helium ion implantation. Helium density and pressure inside individual HPHNBs were quantitatively measured by low-voltage monochromatic electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS). Using in situ transmission electron microscopy (TEM) nanomechanical testing, we showed that HPHNBs could produce the stacking-fault-mediated deformation in FeNiCoCr HEA, contrary to the full dislocation slip and/or deformation twinning observed in our HPHNB-free counterparts and bulk samples [39,40]. It was demonstrated that HPHNBs could not only increase the strength by serving as dislocation obstacles but also attain a two-stage hardening mechanism via facilitating the multiplication and interaction of interwoven stacking faults. Nudged elastic band (NEB) method based on molecular dynamics (MD) simulations was further performed to uncover the role of internal helium pressures in the extraordinary mechanical performance of present HEA.  The equiatomic FeNiCoCr HEA was prepared by arc-melting ${\bf\rho}_{>}99.99\$ wt.% purity), homogenized at 1373 K for $5\mathrm{~h~}$ , water quenched, and mechanically polished to a mirror-like surface. Then, the homogenized FeNiCoCr sample (Fig. 1a and b) was implanted under vacuum (below $10^{-6}$ torr) with $275\mathrm{keVHe^{+}}$ ions at $773\mathrm{~K~}$ to a fluence of $5.14\times10^{16}$ ions $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ at a beam flux of $1.8\times10^{12}$ ions $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}s^{-1}$ .  The temperature was well-controlled according to the feedback of thermocouples welded on the sample. Damage level and helium concentration (Fig. 1c) were predicted by the Stopping and Range of Ions in Matter (SRIM-2013) code under the option of the quick Kinchin-Pease mode with a displacement threshold energy of $40~\mathrm{eV}$ [41]. FeNiCoCr HEA containing HPHNBs is hereafter referred to as HPHNB-HEA, and for comparison, the unirradiated counterpart was also investigated, denoted as U-HEA. To avoid the sink effect of grain boundaries on HPHNB formation, all lamellas for further microstructural investigations and in situ experiments were extracted from the interior of the same grain. For in situ TEM nanomechanical testing, HPHNB-HEA samples were fabricated from depths between 0.5 and $0.8~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ and U-HEA samples were fabricated from depths between 1.0 and $1.3~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ as indicated in Fig. 1c.

The dislocation nucleation rates, especially the HPHNBs surface dislocation nucleation, become gradually significant “after the yielding" (Fig. 8b). With further increasing strain, partial dislocations and associated secondary deformation stacking faults were generated in different {111} slip planes and interact with each other (hardening stage Il; Figs. 5b and 7b). MD simulations also indicated that stacking faults could likely prevent the glide of other dislocations across stacking fault planes [65]. Also noting that the coalescence [14] and fragmentation [46] of HPHNBs were not detected during straining (Fig. 5b), demonstrating that our HPHNBs are very stable. This facilitates to impede the dislocation motion to increase the dislocation storage capacity [37], which further enhances the strain hardening capacity of FeNiCoCr HEA in the present work.
