- caption: Figure 1. DNA-STROBE design and fabrication. a) Schematic of DNA silicifcation for core-satellite gold nanoassemblies, a model system for the DNA-STROBE. - Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To substantially enhance the stability of the nanoassembly, DNA silicification was utilized. Specifically, all DNA structures immobilized on the gold nanoassembly, including the DNA double helix at core–satellite gaps, were conformally encapsulated with an ultrathin silica shell. Silica was selected because of its well-known inertness, biocompatibility, thermal stability, and optical transparency. Despite the apparent conceptual simplicity, such a strategy is difficult to realize in practice owing to the electrostatic repulsion between anionic DNA phosphate backbones and silanol precursors[60,61] used in Stober method.[62,63] Among various options proposed to minimize the electrostatic repulsion,[27,28,60,61] we selected positively charged N-trimethoxysilylpropyl-N,N,N-trimethylammonium chloride (TMAPS) to serve as a costructure directing agent, which can be electrostatically adsorbed to the polyanionic DNA phosphate backbones. It is, therefore, the siloxane functional groups of TMAPS, rather than DNA backbones, that provide direct cocondensation sites for the ultrathin silica shell to grow, as schematically shown in Figure 1a. Success of the DNA silicification procedure was validated by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) measurements of the ultrathin silica shell conformally encapsulating the oligonucleotides-functionalized gold nanoparticle assembly as shown in Figure  1b–d. Expanding on this demonstration, we have fabricated various hierarchical nanoassemblies with increasing complexity from dimer to core–satellite–satellite constructs (Figure  2), which underscores the versatility of the DNA-STROBE method.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To evaluate the stability of the DNA-STROBE specimen, a standard PBS buffer solution with a $\mathrm{pH}$ of 7.4 and a hydrochloric acid (HCl) solution with a $\mathrm{pH}$ of 4.0 were prepared to recapitulate the neutral pH in normal intracellular environments and the acidic interior of lysosomes, respectively. Subsequently, both DNA-STROBE samples and their counterparts without DNA silicification were incubated in each solution for $2\textrm{h}$ . The DNA–silica hybrid nanoassemblies displayed excellent chemical inertness, as evidenced by the well-dispersed colloids and the consistent absorption spectra under both neutral and acidic conditions in Figure 3. In contrast, the core–satellite gold nanoassemblies without DNA silicification became aggregated under the acidic condition, as reflected by the colloidal color change in Figure  3 and the dramatically modified absorption spectra in Figure S2 (Supporting Information). These results confirm that the DNA silicification strategy significantly enhances the chemical inertness and structural stability of these core–satellite gold nanoassemblies paving the way for broader application of the DNA-STROBE constructs in a wide range of biological studies.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Consistent with the previous model system, the DNA silicification process was applied to the gold nanodimers to grow an ultrathin silica shell. One can visualize the gap condensation induced by DNA silicification in the pair of representative SEM images given in Figure  4a,b. Statistical analysis further confirmed that the center-to-center distance decreased from $42.1\pm6.7$ nm $(n=33)$ ) to $40.7\pm2.3~\mathrm{nm}$ $(n=22)$ following DNA silicification. Remarkably, reduction of the size variation, as evidenced by the decrease in standard deviation from 6.7 to $2.3\mathrm{nm}$ , suggests a significant improvement in the gap control. It is worth noting that the satellite gold nanoparticles used (commercially obtained from BBI Solutions) have a diameter of $39.9\pm3.2\mathrm{nm}$ thereby virtually eliminating any possible role of the gold nanoparticles in the gap condensation effect.  We note that the center-to-center distance should have been ${\approx}46~\mathrm{nm}$ if the DNA double helix of 19 base pairs $(\approx6\mathrm{nm}$ in length) was naturally stretched and perfectly aligned with the dimer axis. These discrepancies are rather revealing, and underscore the importance of careful interpretation of the length encoded in DNA sequences. We attribute the distance discrepancies and accompanying variations to the elastic nature of a DNA molecule, especially its capability to fold and bend.[64] Nevertheless, the discrepancies and variations were effectively mitigated by DNA silicification, as shown in Figure  4c and Figure S3 (Supporting Information). The gap condensation effect and the associated improvements in gap control further underpin the role of DNA silicification in fabricating these deep-subwavelength plasmonic nanocavities.

- path: /home/aid1/Documents/causalmat/matmech/Advanced_Materials/10.1002_adma.202005133/images/30610506b1b5c49376f563c219f4234f59e34fd4f74e53418fc7f9c66b597c78.jpg
- caption: Figure 6. Single-molecule and super-resolution measurements with DNA-STROBE. a) Schematic illustration of stochastic single-molecule event at a specific time point. Here, one Raman-active molecule flows in/out of the hotspot volume at a time, switching the SERS signal on or off. b) Time trace of the single-molecule SERS intensity fluctuation (Movie S1, Supporting Information). c) Diffraction-limited and d) super-resolution imaging of plasmonic hotspots that are spatially distributed on the DNA-STROBE core–satellite nanoassembly (Movie S2, Supporting Information).
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The DNA-STROBE constructs offer a possible solution, as the ultrasmall mode volume of plasmonic nanocavities, schematically depicted in Figure  6a, promotes single-molecule occupancy at elevated background molecular concentrations. In this proof-of-concept study, single-molecule events were determined from the temporal fluctuations in SERS intensity at $0.1\times10^{-6}\mathrm{~M~}\mathrm{R}6\mathrm{G}$ solution (background) concentration (Figure 6b). Similar temporal fluctuation-based measurements have been extensively used to identify single-molecule events using super-resolution fluorescence microscopy as well as with plasmonic imaging at the diffraction-unlimited lengthscale.[74,75] Here, we repurposed the standard total internal reflection fluorescence (TIRF) microscope to acquire Raman maps of R6G enhanced by the DNA-STROBE. Briefly, we used a 647 nm laser at ${\approx}100\mathrm{mW}$ for wide-field excitation and a combination of a $660~\mathrm{nm}$ dichroic mirror and a 700 center wavelength emission channel, where the latter window was selected to avoid the R6G fluorescence signal. The SERS signals in the spectral range of $663{-}738~\mathrm{nm}$ corresponding to a Raman shift of $373\mathrm{-}1906~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ were collected on the detector.  From a molecular point of view, temporal fluctuation in SERS intensity seen in Figure  6b can be attributed to the dynamics of a single R6G molecule diffusing in and out of the plasmonic hotspot volume and/or the intrinsic SERS blinking behavior[76,77] of a single R6G molecule lingering inside the hotspot volume. In either case, each SERS emission event can be unambiguously traced back to a plasmonic hotspot. It follows that each SERS emission event encodes information that can be used to pinpoint the plasmonic hotspot beyond the diffraction limit. Indeed, such a framework forms the theoretical foundation of single-molecule localization-based super-resolution SERS imaging.[5,41,74,75]  SERS images were captured with an acquisition time of $10~\mathrm{s}$ , and the DNA-STROBE constructs were presented as blurred spots, as seen in the representative image of Figure  6c. However, by fitting the point spread function to a model function (such as a 2D Gaussian), the emitting center (i.e., plasmonic hotspot) could be uniquely determined at the centroid of the fitting function. The precision of the centroid determination primarily depends on the number of emitted photons for each single-molecule SERS event. The fitting process, performed using ThunderSTORM, was repeated for hundreds of temporal SERS image frames till all plasmonic hotspots were accounted for. Ultimately, the diffraction-unlimited spatial profile of the plasmonic hotspots was reconstructed by superimposing all the determined centroids.  The map of the plasmonic hotspots was obtained with a $20\mathrm{nm}$ resolution ( $20\pm6$ nm localization precision) after reconstruction from a total of 250 SERS image frames, as shown in Figure  6d. The intensity map shows that the SERS centroids, visible as bright discrete spots scattered around an annular ring, in the reconstructed image bear a striking resemblance to the spatial profile of the core–satellite gaps in the DNA-STROBE nanoassembly of Figure 1b. These experimental results demonstrate a proof of concept in the precise localization of the SERS emission in the DNA-STROBE constructs, and direct visualization of the spatial distribution of the otherwise indiscernible hotspots.
