- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure 1a presents a schematic illustration of the fabrication procedure of the RGB fiber phOLEDs based on the dip-coating method. From a cathode to a phosphorescent emission layer (EML), these layers were uniformly coated using the dip-coating method, making it possible to concentrically coat the cylindershaped PET fibers. After that, an exciton blocking layer (EBL), a hole injection layer (HIL), and an anode were thermally deposited on one side of the PET fiber. The RGB fiber phOLEDs were designed with the same layers except for red, green, and blue phosphorescent EML to maximize the efficiency of manufacturing by optimizing the unity of the device structure of the RGB fiber phOLEDs.  The successful realization of fiber phOLEDs relied on an OLED structure considering the cylinder-shaped structure of the fiber substrate and the combination of materials related to phosphorescence. In our previous works,[21,28] it was demonstrated that the inverted structure was more suitable for cylinder-shaped fiber than the normal structure when the same fluorescent EML, namely, Super Yellow,[31–33] which is a representative commercial fluorescent polymer, was used in both structures. The reason for the difference was the shape of an EIL deposited on the fiber. In the case of the inverted structure, a zinc oxide (ZnO)/polyethylenimine (PEI) bilayer,[34] which has been widely used as solution-processed EIL in OLEDs, was uniformly dip-coated onto the fiber. On the other hand, the normal structure had an unevenly thermally deposited 1-nm ultrathin EIL (e.g., 8-hydroxyquinolatolithium (Liq))[35,36] on one side of the fiber, resulting in unbalanced electron injection. Thus, as seen in scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images (Figure 1b), the RGB fiber phOLEDs were designed on the basis of the inverted structure with a $Z\mathrm{nO/}$ PEI bilayer:poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene):-polystyrenesulfonate (PEDOT:PSS) (cathode, ${\approx}100~\mathrm{nm}$ )/ $\mathtt{Z n O}$ NPs/PEI bilayer (EIL, ${\approx}10\mathrm{nm})/\mathrm{RGB}$ phosphorescent EML $(\approx30\mathrm{nm})/4,4^{\prime},4^{\prime\prime}$ tris(carbazol-9-yl)-triphenylamine (TCTA) (HTL and EBL, ${\approx}40~\mathrm{nm}$ )/molybdenum oxide $\left(\mathrm{MoO}_{3}\right)$ (HIL, ${\approx}10~\mathrm{nm}$ )/aluminum (Al) (anode, ${\approx}100\mathrm{nm}$ ) on the cylinder-shaped PET fiber substrate with a diameter of $300~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ (Figure S1, Supporting Information). All materials used in the RGB fiber phOLEDs were carefully selected in consideration of important factors related to solution-processed phOLEDs, such as solubility, solvent orthogonality, electron–hole charge balance (e–h charge balance), and the exciton confinement effect, which prevents the loss of excitons from the EML to the adjacent layers. Our considerations will be discussed in detail later.  To form the phosphorescent EML, making it possible to achieve remarkable device performance, it was considered that the electron–hole charge recombination efficiency in the EML depends on the electron–hole charge balance in the EML.[37,38] Accordingly, multiple host materials were configured so that electron–hole flowing into the EML could be transmitted in balance. Figure 1c shows the materials used in the RGB phosphorescent EMLs to achieve high charge recombination efficiency. The phosphorescent EMLs for red, green, and blue were composed on the basis of poly(N-vinyl carbazole) (PVK) polymer host material with the hole transport property as the role of the bind host material in the co-host system.[37] In the case of the green and blue phOLEDs, the mixed host materials of PVK and 2,6-bis(3-(9-carbazol-9-yl)phenyl)pyridine (26DCzppy) with both electron and hole transport (i.e., bipolar transport) properties were used to solve the imbalance of electrons and holes in the EML (Figure S2, Supporting Information).[38] In the case of the red phOLED, instead of 26DCzppy, 1,3,5-tris(N-phenylbenzimidazol-2-yl)benzene (TPBi) with the electron transport property was mixed with PVK. TPBi replaced 26DCzppy in the red phosphorescent EML because TPBi (electron mobility: $3.3–8.0\times10^{-5}~\mathrm{cm}^{2}~\mathrm{V}^{-1}~\mathrm{s}^{-1})$ has higher electron mobility than 26DCzppy (electron mobility: $1.0{-}\bar{2}.0\times10^{-5}~\mathrm{cm}^{2}~\mathrm{V}^{-1}~\mathrm{s}^{-\mathrm{i}})$ .[39,40] This resulted in better performance by injecting more electrons, which were insufficient in the red phOLED (Figure S3, Supporting Information). In addition to the efficient charge balance enabled by carefully selected materials, one should note the significance of the exciton confinement effect, which prevents the loss of excitons. Because of the long lifetime of triplet excitons, it was known that triplet exciton can be diffused from the EML to the adjacent layers if there is not an EBL, which has a higher triplet state than that of a dopant.[41–43] In this case, the non-radiative decays of diffused triplet excitons can degrade device performance. Therefore, to confine the triplet excitons in the EML, a TCTA with a high triplet state was inserted between the EML and the HIL. As a result, the  TCTA simultaneously acted as the HTL and the EBL and enabled more radiative decays, providing remarkably improved optoelectronic performance, including brightness and CE by having more luminescence with less current density at the same applied electric field (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Based on these delicate considerations, Figure  1d shows the resulting RGB fiber phOLEDs, which were successfully woven into daily clothes, and their microscope images in operation at $3.5~\mathrm{V}$ bias, demonstrating that it is possible to display a perceivable level of visual information at low driving voltages (Figure S5, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure  1e, the red, green, and blue fiber phOLEDs had peak wavelengths at 470, 509, and $600\mathrm{nm}$ , respectively.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure 2a,d,g present illustrations of ITO-coated planar glass-based phOLEDs, and the proposed fiber phOLEDs with the inverted bottom-emission structure for red, green, and blue devices, respectively (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The conventional glass-based phOLEDs and the fiber phOLEDs used ITO and the conductive polymer PEDOT:PSS as the cathode, and the other layers were the same. It is worth noting that the cylinder-shaped RGB fiber phOLEDs exhibited optoelectronic performance similar to those of conventional phOLEDs fabricated on ITO-based planar glass, as shown in Figure  2b,c,e,f,h,i. In particular, the fiber phOLEDs exhibited unprecedentedly high CE values of 16.3, 60.7, and 16.9  cd $\mathrm{A}^{-1}$ for red, green, and blue devices, respectively, which are the highest CE values of fiber-based light-emitting devices ever reported (Table S1, Supporting Information). In the case of the brightness, because the PEDOT:PSS used as a cathode instead of the rigid ITO to ensure flexibility has relatively low conductivity, it was observed that the maximum luminance of the RGB fiber phOLEDs was lower than those of the ITO glass-based phOLEDs (Table S2, Supporting Information). However, even when compared with the brightness of commercial display product, the RGB fiber phOLEDs showed a sufficient level of brightness to display visual information; the maximum luminance values of the red, green, and blue fiber phOLEDs were 4462, 11482, and $1,199\mathrm{cd}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$ , respectively (cf., max. brightness of iPhone 11 pro: $800~\mathrm{cd}~\mathrm{m}^{-2}.$ ). This means that the fiber phOLEDs have the great potential to visualize information even in bright environments, such as under sunlight.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure 3a shows the 3D twistable behavior of the resulting fiber phOLED that can be caused by the curvature of the human body or arbitrary motions (Movie S1, Supporting Information). Figure 3b presents fiber phOLED woven on a garment using a needle. As shown in Figure  3a,b, these outstanding deformable behaviors that are difficult to achieve with the conventional planar structure are attributed to the unique geometry characteristics of the 1D cylinder-shaped fiber. In more detail, the unique geometry characteristics are attributed to the circular cross-section of the fiber. Theoretically, when vertical loading $(P)$ perpendicular to the longitudinal direction of the substrate is applied to the substrate, the moment $(M)$ is formed in the vertical direction of loading. Under the moment $(M)$ , the normal stress distribution on the substrate depends on the second moment of inertia, which is determined by the shape of the cross-section and the distance from the neutral axis. From this principle, it can be explained that the fiber substrate with a circular cross-section has the same second moment of inertia even if vertical loading is applied in any direction. However, the conventional planar substrate with a significant difference in thickness and length has significantly a different second moment of inertia depending on the direction of applied vertical loading (Figure S7a, Supporting Information). Therefore, the fiber phOLEDs are expected to stably exhibit advanced flexural behaviors, such as 3D twistability and bendability in all directions, unlike the convention planar devices. For example, if vertical loading is applied in the thickness direction of the film, unstable flexural behavior that causes the destruction of devices, such as flexural buckling, may occur (Figure S7b, Supporting Information). As seen in Figure  3c, finite-element analysis (FEA) results show that the circular cross-section of the fiber has the same von Mises stress distribution even if vertical loading is applied in several directions $(\theta=0^{\circ}$ , $45^{\circ}$ and $90^{\circ}$ ). From these FEA results, to validate flexural behaviors of the fiber phOLEDs, its flexural properties were evaluated on the basis of bending tests, which were conducted under several strains and directions (Figure S8, Supporting Information). In the normal direction, the optoelectronic performance of the fiber phOLEDs rarely changed until $1.75\%$ tensile strain after experiencing 1000 bending cycles (Figure  3d). Furthermore, even for $45^{\circ}$ and $90^{\circ}$ as in the normal direction, the fiber phOLEDs were shown to have similar optoelectronic performance under the environment in which $1\%$ tensile strain was repeatedly applied for 1000 cycles (Figure 3e). The results demonstrated that fiber phOLEDs can show a higher level of conformability over the conventional planar device, which is only capable of uni- or bi-directional behaviors.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Given both the low driving voltage and the unprecedentedly high CE for low power consumption, high brightness that is clearly distinguishable, and form factor advantages of the cylinder-shaped fiber, it was expected that the fiber phOLEDs would have enormous potential to visualize information as well as provide advanced wearability. However, to display visual information that is not just light-emitting, a matrix-addressable scheme, which can drive multiple pixels independently, was essential. Figure 4a schematically presents the fabrication procedure of the fiber phOLED with addressability for the passive matrix-type textile displays based on fibers consisting of conductive fibers (i.e., scan lines) with Al $\scriptstyle(\approx100\mathrm{nm})$ thermally deposited and fiber phOLEDs with four independent pixels (i.e., data lines). After the dip-coating process among the fabrication procedure of the fiber phOLED, Liq, commonly used as an ultrathin EIL in OLEDs, was partially deposited by thermal evaporation. Then, TCTA $(\approx40\mathrm{nm})/\mathrm{MoO}_{3}$ $(\approx10\mathrm{nm})/\mathrm{Al}$ $(\approx100~\mathrm{nm})$ ) were thermally deposited on wider areas containing each area where Liq ${(\approx40\mathrm{nm})}$ was deposited. Figure 4b presents the cross-section view consisting of the contact region and the emission region, which are divided by the Liq layer. It was confirmed by STEM images that each region was successfully multi-stacked on the fiber. The contact region has higher resistivity than the emission region because the Liq layer, used as ultrathin EIL in OLEDs, is located between the EML and the HTL. Therefore, if a forward bias is applied to the luminescent date line from the conductive scan line that is contacted above the contact region, current flows into the emitting region (i.e., pixel) because the current flows to the place with relatively low resistivity (Figure  4c and Figure S9, Supporting Information).  In this way, without an additional fabrication process to form the contact region, the contact region was easily fabricated to prevent damage to the pixel by physical scratches when the conductive scan line comes into direct contact with the pixel. In this regard, Figure  4d demonstrates that light-emission appeared only in the pixels of the luminescent data line even though the electrical connection occurs in which the conductive scan line is intersected on the contact region of the luminescent data line. This means that the pixel is stable for the stress caused by the physical contact for realizing addressability. Figure $\mathsf{4e-g}$ shows the difference between the optoelectronic performance of the single-type fiber phOLED and those of the fiber phOLED with addressability, which is operated by a combination of the luminescent data line and the conductive scan line. Although the fiber phOLED with addressability showed the same current density values at higher voltages as that of a single type due to both contact resistance and series resistance of the lines, it still showed performances that are sufficient to visualize information.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure 5a presents the proposed fiber-based textile display, directly integrated into daily clothes, which was composed of 4 conductive scan lines and 4 luminescent data lines. Each conductive scan line and luminescent data line were intersected in accordance with each contact region. Then, to secure a reliable electrical connection between the conductive scan line and the luminescent data line, interlocking fibers, which were bare PET fibers with a diameter of $100~\mathrm{um}$ , were used to robustly fasten the lines by their entanglements. Finally, to protect against moisture and oxygen, $\mathrm{Al}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{3}$ $(50~\mathrm{nm})$ ) encapsulation was carried out using the atomic layer deposition (ALD) process, which could cover all areas of the volumetric structure, such as the cylinder-shape as well as the planar area (Figure S10, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure  5b, the proposed fiber-based textile display was operated in forms of the passive matrix, which is that all pixels can be independently controlled. It is operated via a microcontroller board (Arduino Uno), wirelessly receiving information from a computer when a user enters information into the computer. When the fiber-based textile display is addressed in the form of a passive matrix, the on-pixels are subjected to a $5\mathrm{~V~}$ forward bias, and the off-pixels are subjected to a $5\mathrm{~V~}$ reverse bias, among 16 pixels (Figure 5c). To confirm stability in the situation of reverse bias, Figure  5d shows that the fiber phOLED can be stably operated without a breakdown in a range from $-5\mathrm{~V~}$ to $5\mathrm{~V~.~}$ In addition, due to the inherent fast response time of OLEDs, it was possible for the device to successfully emit light without flicker induced by the slow driving speed. As a result of the aforementioned characteristics that are required for the visualization of information, Figure  5e indicates that the fiber-based textile successfully displayed visualized letter information, which was clearly distinguishable in bright and dark environments (Movie S2, Supporting Information).
