- caption: Fig. 1. Characterization of phases at the (Ni)/NiAl interface after annealing for 1h at $650^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ by SEM and STEM, (a) combined $\mathrm{IPF{+}I Q}$ map of an EBSD scan of the phases, (b) STEM BF and corresponding STEM EDX map of Al together with an extracted concentration profile of Al; the black arrows and the dashed line mark the position of the Kirkendall plane after annealing.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The EBSD scan and STEM EDX maps in Fig. 1 show the microstructure of the grains of the nucleating phase(s) at the (Ni)/NiAl interface in a diffusion couple. Due to unbalanced, intrinsic volume fluxes of the components Ni and Al, respectively, in opposite direction, the initial (Ni)/NiAl interface is subject to a displacement during annealing and is designated as Kirkendall plane hereafter [31]. NiAl is located at the top, and (Ni) at the bottom of the image. This convention is kept for all micrographs in the following. In order to visualize the microstructure, a gray scale image quality (IQ) map and a colored inverse pole figure (IPF) map are superimposed in the orientation map (Fig. 1a). As the reference direction of the IPF color code, the direction perpendicular to the (Ni)/NiAl interface plane was chosen that is parallel to the bulk diffusion flux during annealing. Since all detected phases possess a cubic crystal structure, only one IPF color code is displayed. The style of color coding and map composition is also maintained for the orientation maps in Fig. 4.  According to Fig. 1a, the newly formed grains at the (Ni)/NiAl interface are arranged in two to three layers. Since EBSD analyses generally do not detect superlattice reflections that discriminate the $\mathrm{L}1_{2}$ phase $(\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al})$ from the fcc phase (Ni), and since the lattice constants of the phases $(\mathsf{a}_{\mathrm{Ni}3\mathrm{Al}}=3.572\mathring{\mathbf{A}}$ and $a_{\mathrm{Ni}}{=}3.525\mathring{\mathrm{A}}^{\cdot}$ ) are very similar, these phases cannot be distinguished by EBSD. The Al distribution in the STEM EDX map (Fig. 1b) and the corresponding concentration profile clearly show that the upper two layers consist of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ grains exhibiting a concentration of $20...25\mathrm{at}\%$ Al. The third layer underneath contains about $5a\mathrm{t}\%$ Al and is the (Ni) solid solution. No evidence has been found by EBSD or EDX for the presence of the orthorhombic $\mathrm{Ni}_{5}\mathrm{Al}_{3}$ phase after 1h of annealing that is also stable at temperatures below $700^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ [10,32,33]. After annealing, the position of the Kirkendall plane is clearly visible in the combined IPF and IQ map (Fig. 1a, black arrows). It is located between the two layers of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ grains indicating the growth of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ phase towards both of the initially present phases (Ni) and NiAl. This is consistent with earlier observations documented in the literature [5,7,8] also reporting a different grain morphology on the two sides of the Kirkendall plane inside $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ [343-36].  In order to evaluate the grain size in the three newly forming layers, the equivalent circular diameter of the grain projected area is used as a measure [37]. As seen in Fig. 1a, the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ grains growing into the NiAl phase are larger and exhibit a mean grain size of $0.82\upmu\mathrm{m}$ and an average layer thickness of $1.35\upmu\mathrm{m}$ . The $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer growing into the (Ni) phase exhibits a considerably lower mean grain size $(0.48\upmu\mathrm{m})$ and layer thickness $(0.5\upmu\mathrm{m})$ . The grains of the discontinuous third layer underneath the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layers in Fig. 1a are characterized by an increased solute concentration of $-5a\mathrm{t}\%$ Al in form of a plateau (see concentration profile in Fig. 1b). This layer has an average thickness of $1\upmu\mathrm{m}$ and consists of grains with a mean size of $1.6\upmu\mathrm{m}$ that are much smaller than the grains in the (Ni) phase prior to annealing $(50...500\upmu\mathrm{m})$ . Such formation of (Ni) grains with some Al in solid solution and has not been reported so far for diffusion couples in the Ni-Al system. These Alenriched (Ni) grains are interpreted to have formed by diffusion induced recrystallization [38,39], a process which is related with diffusion induced grain boundary migration [40,41], where diffusion of a solute into a polycrystalline specimen induces migration of newly formed grain boundaries that leave solute enriched regions behind. The comparatively large size of the Al-enriched (Ni) grains is due to the fact that diffusion induced recrystallization is based on grain boundary diffusion [383–41] which is prevailing at the relatively low annealing temperature of $650^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ The occurrence of cell structures in the pure (Ni) confirms this process (STEM BF image in Fig. 1b). Earlier observations on the growth kinetics in (Ni)/NiAl and $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}/$ NiAl diffusion couples [7,10] came to the conclusion that grain boundary diffusion has a considerable influence on the phase formation at temperatures below $900^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ .

- caption: Fig. 2. Thicknesses of the two $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layers (red and green) and the layer of Alenriched (Ni) grains as a function of annealing time at $650^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ ; the linear fits indicate an essentially parabolic growth for all layers.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The growth of the two $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layers and the Al-enriched (Ni) grains at $650^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ has been examined by evaluating their layer thicknesses after annealing times of 1h, 4h, 6h, 18h, and 48h. According to Fig. 2, all the layers show a parabolic growth behavior that can consequently be attributed to diffusion-controlled growth. An extrapolation of the current data to lower annealing times (dashed lines in Fig. 2) cannot resolve differences in incubation times. To gain reliable information about the incubation times further analyses applying annealing times below 1h are necessary.  The different slopes of the linear fits (Fig. 2) confirm a higher growth rate of the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer into the NiAl phase (red) than into the (Ni) phase. The differences in thickness and grain size of the two $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layers are attributed to a shift of the Kirkendall plane inside the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ phase in cobination with different velocities of the $\mathrm{NiAl}/\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ and $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al/(Ni)}$ interfaces during growth of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ . The shift of the Kirkendall plane is related to a difference of the intrinsic diffusion coefficients of the components Ni and Al, respectively inside the growing $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ phase [42,43]. Prior investigations on NiAl/(Ni) diffusion couples at $1000^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ [34] reported the intrinsic diffusion coefficient of Ni to be five times higher than that of Al in $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ . The higher Ni flux substantiates the migration of the Kirkendall plane towards the (Ni) phase, resulting in a lower thickness of the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer at the (Ni) side. Additionally, the different velocities of the NiAl $/\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ and the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al/(Ni)}$ interface depend on the diffusion fluxes across each of the two interfaces NiAl $/\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ and $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}/(\mathrm{Ni})$ towards the growing $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ phase, i.e. on the interdiffusion coefficients in NiAl, $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al},$ and (Ni) and the concentration gradients at both sides of all moving interfaces [44]. The $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}_{I}$ NiAl interface is migrating faster than the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}/(\mathrm{Ni})$ interface, since the concentration gradients at both sides of the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al/NiAl}$ interface are significantly steeper than those at the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al/(Ni)}$ interface, see the slope of the concentration profile in Fig. 1b. At the same time, the interdiffusion coefficient in NiAl is ${\sim}10$ times higher than in (Ni) [8,45,46]; the interdiffusion coefficient in $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ at the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al/NiAl}$ interface is also higher than that at the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}_{}$ /(Ni) interface [47,48]. It can be concluded that the larger flux across the $\mathrm{NiAl}/\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ interface leads to an increased $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer thickness for growth into the NiAl phase. However, each of the two layers of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ grains in Fig. 1a consists of 58 grains, indicating a similar nucleation rate at both sides of the interface, despite different crystallographic relations and interfacial energies.

- caption: Fig. 3. Crystallographic texture of different layers of newly forming phase by comparison of pole figures in adjacent phases NiAl (black) and (Ni) (gray) and by highlighting the special orientation relationships in the IQ map, (a) first $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer (red) growing into NiAl, (b) second $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer (green) adjoining (Ni), and (c) layer of Al-enriched (Ni) grains (blue). - Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): In this section the crystallographic textures and corresponding orientation relationships of the grains nucleating at the interfaces are presented. A large fraction of the nucleating grains is found to exhibit a crystallographic texture that is specific for each of the three layers that form at the interface (compare Fig. 1a). In Fig. 3, the texture and the corresponding orientation relationships to the initial phases NiAl and (Ni) are characterized using pole figures. A particular color is assigned to each layer that is also applied to highlight grains in specific orientations in the corresponding IQ map. The upper $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer adjoining the NiAl phase is represented in red (Fig. 3a). The $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer growing towards the (Ni) phase is displayed in green (Fig. 3b). The discontinuous layer of Al enriched (Ni) grains is colored in blue (Fig. 3c). In Fig. 3a, an area fraction of ${\sim}45\%$ of the upper $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer is found to be close to one of the 24 possible K-S orientation relationships [49] to the NiAl phase. These grains are highlighted red in the IQ map. The corresponding pole figures underneath display multiple coincidences of the {110} poles of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ (red) with the {111} poles of NiAl (black) and vice versa according to the K-S relationship, as indicated by triangles pointing up and down, respectively (Fig. 3a and eq. 1). To clearly illustrate the orientation relationship, the pole figures of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ are generated merely from grains close to K-S variants corresponding to the red highlighting in the IQ-map. According to the pole figures of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ , all {110} and {111} poles display nearly similar probabilities, indicating the presence of all K-S variants instead of a preferential selection of a specific variant. Additionally, there are $\sim16\%$ of the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ grains in a twin relation to grains with a K-S relation to NiAl. These grains are not highlighted for the sake of clarity. Overall more than $60\%$ of the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ grains adjoining NiAl hold an orientation relationship – a finding that has not been documented so far for macroscopic diffusion couples due to a lack of a detailed crystallographic analysis. The second $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer (Fig. 3b) growing into the (Ni) phase contains ${\sim}41\%$ of grains with a near cube-oncube orientation (eq. 2) or a twin relationship to that accented by green color in the corresponding IQ map. The two corresponding {100} pole figures of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ (green) and (Ni) (gray) demonstrate a misorientation within $15^{\circ}$ as indicated by the square annotations correlating with a high fraction of small-angle boundaries at the $(\mathrm{Ni})/\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ interface. The dashed squares mark the {100} poles of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ with a twin relationship. The increased presence of immobile small-angle and twin boundaries at the $(\mathrm{Ni})/\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ interfaces also slows down grain growth in the second $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer [50]. The third discontinuous layer of Al-enriched (Ni) grains underneath exhibits a pronounced texture as visible in the {100} pole figures (blue in Fig. 3c). This texture is misaligned to the (Ni) phase by $\sim26^{\circ}$ , as indicated by the tilted squares in both $\lbrace100\rbrace$ pole figures. Including twinning (tilted dashed squares in PF), the textured grains cover $\sim60\%$ of the Al-enriched (Ni) layer, as visualized by the blue highlighting in the corresponding IQ map (Fig. 3c). Furthermore, these (Ni) grains have a misorientation of less than $15^{\circ}$ to the texture in the adjoining second $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ layer, resulting in small-angle boundaries at the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}_{i}$ /(Ni) interface. At the transition from the recrystallized Al-enriched (Ni) grains to pure (Ni), the grains possess a larger misorientation of $\sim26^{\circ}$ , resulting in mobile grain boundaries that facilitate swift growth of the (Ni) grains. For all analyzed (Ni)/NiAl interfaces, $\sim50\%$ of the newly formed grains exhibit an orientation relationship to one of the adjoining phases (Ni) or NiAl after 1h of annealing. After 6h of annealing, the grown layers were found to contain a higher area fraction of up to $80\%$ with grains in orientation relationships.  These complex orientation relationships of the newly forming grains revealed by a thorough crystallographic characterization substantiate the preference for oriented nucleation at (Ni)/NiAl interfaces. These new findings indicate a significant influence of interfacial and strain energies on the nucleation also at lower temperatures.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Early stages of the formation of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ at the (Ni)/NiAl interface are displayed in Fig. 4. The two orientation maps $(\mathrm{IPF{+}I Q})$ display the NiAl phase at the top and the (Ni) phase at the bottom. Fig. 4a and Fig. 4b demonstrate an abrupt change concerning the presence of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ where grain boundaries reach the Kirkendall plane. The changing crystallographic orientation at such a grain boundary in NiAl (Fig. 4a) and in (Ni) (Fig. 4b) results in a different crystallographic character of (Ni)/NiAl interface that apparently has an impact on the nucleation behavior of the newly forming phase.  The grain boundaries displayed in Fig. 4 are random high-angle grain boundaries with misorientation angles of $21^{\circ}$ between the NiAl grains (Fig. 4a) and $40^{\circ}$ between the (Ni) grains (Fig. 4b), i.e. there are distinctly different misorientations between NiAl and (Ni) on the two sides of the grain boundaries.  The crystallographic relationships between grains of the two initial phases (Ni) and NiAl and the newly forming phases in combination with the number of newly forming grains and the grain size distributions have been determined to examine the possible influence of the interface energy and interface diffusivities on nucleation rate and growth of the $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ phase. The influence of the interface crystallography on nucleation and growth has been statistically evaluated by analyzing ${\sim}2.2\mathrm{mm}$ of the (Ni)/NiAl interface, capturing 21 EBSD maps. Overall, 20 different (Ni)/NiAl interface configurations (misorientation relationships） and more than 3000 grains at the interface were recorded. The statistics includes all grains formed at the interface, since the EBSD pattern evaluation cannot distiguish the crystal structures of (Ni) and $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ , and SEM EDX does not provide the necessary lateral resolution to discern both phases. To reduce the interference of triple junctions that may act as preferred nucleation sites, sections of $5{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ in the vicinity of triple junctions were not considered. Additionally, only interfaces with a length of at least $20\upmu\mathrm{m}$ were evaluated to provide representative data. The analyzed (Ni)/NiAl interfaces are categorized by the grain density, i.e. the number of grains per unit length of the interface, to determine the nucleation rate (Fig. 5a), and to compare the grain size distributions for the evaluation of grain growth and diffusion (Fig. 5b).

The frequency distribution of the grain density at the (Ni)/NiAl interfaces (Fig. 5a) is of a bimodal character. Accordingly, the analyzed the (Ni)/NiAl interfaces were categorized in two classes, representing interfaces with low and high nucleation rates, respectively. The class with low nucleation rate (gray in Fig. 5) represents (Ni)/NiAl interfaces occupied with a density of $\mathrm{Ni}_{3}\mathrm{Al}$ grains below 1.5grains/μum, as shown at the left hand side of Fig. 4b. (Ni)/NiAl interfaces exhibiting more than 1.5grains/μm are classified as interfaces with high nucleation rate (red in Fig. 5) according to the right hand side in Fig. 4b. However, specific crystallographic features of the initial (Ni)/NiAl interface (that e.g. may be accompanied by a variation of interfacial energies) were not found to influence the different nucleation rates or the different textures of the new grains at the interface:  In Fig. 5b, representative grain size distributions at (Ni)/NiAl interfaces with low (gray) and high nucleation rate (red) are compared. In order to achieve sufficient statistics for the interfaces with low nucleation rate, the distribution was cumulated over 7 (Ni)/NiAl interfaces containing nearly $80\%$ of all grains recognized in this class. The grain size distribution resembles a log-normal distribution, indicating similar grain growth conditions at each interface with low nucleation rate. A pronounced difference of the two grain size distributions in Fig. 5b is not observed. Grain growth appears to be little sensitive to the interface crystallography. The tendency to a higher fraction of larger grains at the (Ni)/NiAl interfaces with high nucleation rates indicates some advanced growth of the newly forming grains that may be due to faster grain boundary and interface diffusion [53] that depend on interface crystallography [54].
