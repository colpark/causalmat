- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The schematic images in Figure 1a show a battery embedded in a soft contact lens made of a hydrogel and an electrode structure. The hydrogel acts as not only a structural material to form a soft contact lens but also a gel electrolyte of a battery. The battery doesn‘t have the critical safety issues of electrolyte leakage or exposure of reactive substances on the eyes because it can operate in the tear solution without an additional electrolyte. CNTs are used for current collectors and as a conductive addition to the slurry and polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) is used for the polymer matrix. The crosssectional scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of the 50 $\mu\mathrm{m}$ thick electrode shows that the active materials are uniformly distributed in the porous matrix (Figure 1b).  Film electrodes of the PBA nanocomposite prepared by doctor blading (Figure S1) are cut by a cutting plotter into Cshapes (Figure S2) and released from the substrate when dipped into deionized (DI) water. The C-shaped electrodes are then reformed into the shape of a hemisphere using molds in which the hydrogel is polymerized by UV radiation (Figure 1c). The inner diameter is large enough to be visible through the hydrogel without being blocked by the electrode, whereas the outer diameter is smaller than the diameter of a conventional soft contact lens (Figure 1d,e).28 Figure 1f shows an optical microscope image of the cross section of the full cell embedded in the hydrogel. The electrodes are enclosed and well separated by the hydrogel. The total thickness of the electrodes and hydrogel is ${\sim}220\mu\mathrm{m},$ which is within the thickness range of conventional contact lenses.29

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): made in the laboratory. The artificial tears solution contained 0.15 M sodium chloride (NaCl) and 0.02 M potassium chloride (KCl) in DI water and the pH value was adjusted to 7 using a sodium hydroxide solution. We used artificial tears to characterize the electrodes because these two salts are major inorganic compounds and those concentrations are within the range of salt concentration in human tears.26,27 Figure 2a shows a schematic of the working principle of PBAs in the tears. The formal potential of the PBAs for K-ions is higher than that for Na-ions.30 When both ionic species coexist in an aqueous electrolyte, K-ions are intercalated first, leaving no intercalation sites for Na-ions. To investigate the ions’ charge carrier behavior in the tears, the cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of copper hexacyanoferrate (CuHCFe) and iron hexacyanoferrate (FeHCFe) electrodes were measured with a scan rate of $0.1\mathrm{mV}/s$ in the tears, $1\mathrm{~M~NaCl}$ , and 1 M KCl (Figure 2b). The CVs show formal potentials of CuHCFe and FeHCFe at ${\sim}0.86$ and ${\sim}0.36\mathrm{~V~}$ (versus SHE), respectively. The redox peak in the tears is observed between those in $1\textbf{M}$ KCl and $1\mathrm{~M~}\mathrm{~NaCl}$ . The higher formal potential of the electrode in the tears than in $1\mathrm{MNaCl}$ implies that Na-ions in the tears do not participate in the redox reaction. The formal potential of the electrode in the tears is lower than that in $1\textbf{M}$ KCl because the concentration of KCl in the tears is $0.02\mathrm{~M~}$ . The significant K-peak but negligeable $\mathrm{Na}$ -peak in energydispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) for the fully reduced CuHCFe (Figure S3) also support that the charge carriers in the PBA-based battery operating in the tears are K-ions. Because oxygen is dissolved in the tears, oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) and oxygen evolution reaction (OER) are the limiting factors when selecting the species of PBAs for the cathode and anode.31 If the redox potential of anode is lower than the potential of ORR ( $\mathrm{0.7}52\mathrm{V}$ versus SHE), the anode is oxidized which leads to a undesirable self-discharge of the battery. On the other hand, the redox potential of the cathode cannot be higher than the potential of OER $\mathrm{\Delta}_{0.910\mathrm{~V~}}^{\prime}$ versus SHE). (See details in Supporting Information, Section 7.) Hence, we believe FeHCFe and CuHCFe are the optimum materials for obtaining a high voltage in an oxygen-permeable aqueous battery.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure 3 shows the performance of a full cell embedded in a hydrogel contact lens immersed in the tears. Carbon wires were connected to nanocomposite electrodes during UV polymerization of the hydrogel (Figure 3a). The battery has a discharge capacity of $155.4\mu\mathrm{Ah}$ at $100\mu\mathrm{A}$ with cutoff voltages of 0.2 and $0.9\mathrm{~V~}$ (Figure 3b). Volumetric capacity of a full cell calculated by dividing the capacity by the total volume of electrodes was $15.5\mathrm{mAh}/\mathrm{cm}^{3}$ . The capacity was retained at $72.8\%$ and $63.3\%$ at the 50th and $100\mathrm{{th}}$ cycles, respectively (Figure 3c). At the first cycle, it shows relatively low Coulombic efficiency (CE). This may come from K-ion intercalation into FeHCFe filled with Na-ion at half of the site. The CE was ${\sim}97.5\%$ and was less than $100\%$ due to the ORR and OER even if a narrow potential window was set. Selfdischarging of the battery occurs and we observed $8.5\%$ capacity of a fully charged battery was self-discharged for $^\textrm{\scriptsize6h}$ (Figure S6). Thus, the loss of CE is unavoidable. We believe that the $2.5\%$ loss of CE is more beneficial than a smaller potential window in terms of electrical energy. As the usage-life of soft contact lenses is recommended for 1 month due to contamination, the lifespan of our battery will be sufficient throughout the life of soft contact lenses even though the capacity retention of the battery is lower than that of state-ofthe-art batteries. PBA-based batteries have very stable capacity retention and high CE in highly concentrated aqueous electrolytes with sodium perchlorate and sodium nitrate salts. Here, we used tears, which have mild salinity and contain mainly chloride ions, as the electrolyte. We consider the low salinity and predominance of chloride ions as the two factors that constrain battery performance. The rate capacity performance was measured by applying currents from 100 (0.65C-rate, where 1C is $155\mu\mathrm{A},$ ) to $600\mu\mathrm{A}$ (3.9C-rate) in GCPL (Figure 3d). The maximum power was $0.318~\mathrm{mW}_{;}$ , and the maximum energy was $285~\mathrm{mJ}$ . The GCPL curves of each current rate are available in Supporting Information (Figure S7). Our battery is comparable with the previous rechargeable batteries for smart contact lenses, for example, solid-state thin film battery (35 $\mu\mathrm{Ah}$ at 0.1C-rate) and serpentine-type of polymer electrolyte battery (73.5 μAh at 441 μA and 900 μAh at 90 μA).15,16 Figure 3e shows the Nyquist plot of the EIS measurement of the battery. The cell resistance was $347~\Omega$ which is a relatively high internal resistance compared with conventional batteries because the conductivities of the CNT current collector and the carbon wire are low. The photo of the electrical connection between the battery and the measurement is available in Supporting Information (Figure S8). The resistance can be lowered when the battery is integrated with smart contact lenses with a proper electrical connection.  We tested the mechanical stability of the battery under bending, which is frequent during handling of soft contact lenses. The open circuit voltage (OCV) of the battery under manual deformation by fingers showed quick recovery after releasing the deformation (Figure 3f). OCV change under deformation might be caused by a loosened electrical connection between the electrode and the carbon wire, not the degradation of the PBAs, because its recovery time was faster than the time scale of ionic diffusion in PBAs. The battery under repeated deformation was tested by applying compressive force in the radial direction of the contact lens (Figure $3\mathrm{g},$ ). The structure of the battery fully recovered when the compressive displacement was released. The GCPL curves of the second and 51st cycles during the mechanical testing are available in Supporting Information (Figure S9). The battery could stably operate over 100 charging−discharging cycles (Figure 3h). The capacity of the battery that has undergone mechanical testing decreases faster than that of the pristine battery because the electrical connection between active materials and CNTs are loosened under repeated bending. In this experiment, the battery was bent more than 20 000 times, which is a harsher condition than the regular use of contact lenses. Considering much less change of bending during the lifetime of the contact lenses, the battery has sufficient mechanical stability to be applied as a power source for smart contact lenses.
