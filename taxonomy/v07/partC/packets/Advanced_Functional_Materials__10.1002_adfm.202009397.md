- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The morphology of synthesized CG at different temperatures was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). SEM images of CG particles prepared at different temperatures displayed a similar wrinkled 3D ball-like shape (Figure 1a; Figure S2, Supporting Information). Specifically, the SEM image of the CG obtained at $1000~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (denoted as CG-1000) showed myriad CG particles with a size of $200{-}600~\mathrm{nm}$ (Figure  1a). Since the surface of the nebulized GO-containing water droplet served as a template for crumpling the GO sheets during the drying process, CG-1000 showed a quasi-spherical morphology consisting of a smooth surface and sharp ridges as shown in the inset of Figure 1a. Such a smooth surface of CG can be utilized as an electrochemically active surface area for the surfacecontrolled charge storage mechanism. On the other hand, the sharp ridges formed through the partial restacking of the graphene sheets provide the structural stability of CG (Figure 1b). In addition, the high-resolution TEM (HRTEM) image of the ridge on CG-1000 showed the distorted carbon lattices with $d$ -spacing profiles of $0.372–0.375\mathrm{nm}$ (Figure  1c). The selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern for the smooth surface showed graphene (100) diffraction spots as shown in the inset 1 of Figure  1d. However, the SAED pattern of the ridge on CG-1000 showed strong graphite (002) diffraction spots with dim graphene (100) diffraction spots as shown in the insets 2 and 3 of Figure  1d. Such strong graphite (002) diffraction spots corresponded to a $d$ -spacing of ${\approx}0.37$ nm, which is consistent with the HRTEM result.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The surface chemistry of CGs was studied using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) to identify the structural changes in oxygen functional groups during the aerosol drying process. XPS survey scan of CGs showed that the atomic ratio of oxygen to carbon $(\mathrm{O}/\mathrm{C})$ gradually decreased from ${\approx}0.5$ to ${\approx}0.1$ with the increase of the heating temperature (Figure 2a). Highresolution XPS C 1s spectra of CGs can be deconvoluted into five components, including $\operatorname{sp}^{2}$ -hybridized carbon $\scriptstyle(\mathrm{C}=\mathrm{C})$ , $\operatorname{sp}^{3}.$ - hybridized carbon $(\mathrm{C}{-}\mathrm{C})$ , hydroxyl or epoxy $(\mathrm{C}{-}\mathrm{O})$ , carboxyl (COOH), and carbonyl $\scriptstyle(\mathrm{C}{=}\mathrm{O})$ groups located at $284.5\pm0.1$ , $285.2\pm0.1$ , $286.7\pm0.1$ , $288.4\pm0.2$ , and $290.2\pm0.2\mathrm{eV},$ respectively (Figure  2b). All oxygen functional groups gradually decreased with increasing heating temperature, while the ratio of $\operatorname{sp}^{2}$ -hybridized carbon $(\mathrm{C}{-}\mathrm{C})$ progressively increased. In particular, the dominant $_{\mathrm{C-O}}$ peak was considerably reduced by increasing the heating temperature above $500~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ due to the removal of large amounts of unstable hydroxyl and epoxy groups. FTIR further confirmed this trend by showing the various oxygen functional groups on CG-250, but significantly reduced the functional groups on CG-1000 (Figure S3, Supporting Information). These results indicate that the heating temperature is an effective processing parameter for controlling the amount of oxygen functional groups of CG. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) was used to investigate the stacking structure of CG with respect to heating temperature (Figure  2c). CG-250 displayed a broad (002) diffraction peak at $2\theta=11.8^{\circ}$ with a $d$ -spacing of ${\approx}0.75~\mathrm{nm}$ . The center position of the (002)  peak gradually upshifted with increasing heating temperature, showing $2\theta=\approx25.7^{\circ}$ with a $d$ -spacing of ${\approx}0.35$ nm for CG-1000. This trend indicates that effective removal of oxygen functional groups with higher heating temperatures leads to the recovery of the graphitic structure at the ridge of CG. The defect structure of CG was further investigated by Raman spectroscopy (Figure 2d). All CGs displayed a broad D peak at $\approx1360~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ in addition to a sharp G peak at $\approx1580~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ , which indicates the presence of the structural defects in the $\operatorname{sp}^{2}$ -hybridized carbon systems. The integrated ratio of D to G peak $(A_{\mathrm{G}}/A_{\mathrm{D}})$ for CG samples was calculated to be 0.74 for CG-250, 0.7 for CG-500, 0.68 for CG-800, and 0.66 for CG-1000, indicating the gradual formation of defects on CG. The increase in these defects at higher heating temperatures may be attributed to the evolution of CO, ${\mathrm{CO}}_{2}.$ and $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ during the thermal reduction process of oxygen functional groups in GO.[17]

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To evaluate the electrochemical characteristics of CGs, we prepared the free-standing electrodes by a vacuum-filtration process of a mixture of $90\mathrm{~wt}\%$ of CG and $10\mathrm{wt}\%$ of FWNT in isopropanol (Figure S4a, Supporting Information). In this study, sub-millimeter long FWNT with high electrical conductivity and superior mechanical stability was used as a conductive binder for immobilizing CG particles.[18] SEM image of the free-standing electrode showed that the CG particles were entangled within the 3D FWNT network (Figure S4b, Supporting Information). It should be mentioned that CG retained its unique 3D structure after the solution processing with isopropanol and continuous vacuum-filtration process, indicating the remarkably aggregation-resistance property of CG in both solution and solid states.[19] Then, we first investigated the charge storage performances of CGs with $1\textbf{M}$ lithium hexafluorophosphate $\left(\mathrm{LiPF_{6}}\right)$ in ethylene carbonate (EC):ethyl methyl carbonate (EMC):methyl propionate (MP) $(20{:}60{:}20\mathrm{~\:~v~}/\mathrm{v}\%)$ at room temperature. This electrolyte system is known to maintain high ionic conductivity at low temperatures below $-60~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}.^{[20]}$ The CG synthesized at the lowest temperature of $250^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (CG-250) showed a poor initial Coulombic efficiency (CE) of ${\approx}16.6\%$ (Figure 3a). Such large initial capacity loss is due to the formation of a SEI layer and the possible irreversible Li-ion traps in the bulk of the carbon and oxygen functional groups.[21] The initial CEs increased to $34-35\%$ for the CGs prepared at higher heating temperatures (CG-500, CG-800, and CG-1000). Interestingly, the initial discharge curve of CG-250 displayed significantly higher irreversible Li trapping above $^{1\mathrm{~V~}}$ versus $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ compared to CG-1000 (Figure 3a). This clearly indicates that oxygen functional groups, especially hydroxyl or epoxy groups, are responsible for the irreversible capacity loss at high potential region $(>1\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}})$ before the formation of the SEI layer. The capacities of all CG samples were stabilized within five cycles. However, during the initial low rate cycling at $0.05\mathrm{Ag}^{-1}$ , the capacity loss of CG-250 is higher than other CGs obtained at higher temperatures. This high capacity loss of CG-250 can be attributed to the large amount of oxygen functional groups that can catalyze SEI formation beyond the first cycle.[15b,22]  At a slow rate of $0.1\mathrm{A}~\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ CG-1000 delivered a capacity of ${\approx}341$ mAh ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ , which is higher than those of other electrodes $(\approx325\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ for CG-800, ${\approx}323\$ mAh ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ for CG-500, and ${\approx}282\operatorname{mAh}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ for CG-250) (Figure 3b). It should be noted that the FWNT electrode without CG delivers a capacity of $\mathrm{\tilde{\simeq}}25\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ at $0.05\mathrm{Ag}^{-1}$ (Figure S5, Supporting Information). This value is approximately $5\%$ of the total capacity of CG-1000, indicating the minor capacity contribution of FWNT to the composite electrodes. All samples exhibited a sloping charge and discharge profile, which is a characteristic of the surface-controlled charge storage mechanism. The high sloping capacity of CGs at potentials higher than ${\approx}0.1\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ , the Li intercalation potential of graphite, can be attributed to Li-ion adsorption on various defective sites of CG.[23] However, the relatively flat plateau below ${\approx}0.3\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ is also observed, which can be associated with the diffusion-controlled intercalation reaction of Li-ion into the partial restacked regions in the ridge sites on CG. The sloping potential profile can be explained by natural variation in Li-adsorption energies at various defect sites of CG, including graphene edges, oxygen functional groups, Stone– Wales, single-, and double vacancies.[15b,24] At a moderate rate of $0.5\mathrm{~A~g~}^{-1}$ , the difference in capacity between the electrodes became larger: ${\approx}285\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ for $\mathsf{C G}{\cdot}1000,\approx237\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ for $\mathrm{CG}{\cdot}800,{\approx}206\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ for CG-500, and ${\approx}88\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ for CG-250 (Figure 3c). At a high rate of $10\mathrm{Ag}^{-1}$ , CG-1000 delivered a significantly higher capacity of ${\approx}206~\mathrm{mAh}$ ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ compared to those of other samples $(\approx166\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ for CG-800, ${\approx}109$ mAh ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ for CG-500, and ${\approx}22$ mAh ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ for CG-250) (Figure S6, Supporting Information). The results suggest that the effective reduction of oxygen functional groups on CG at higher heating temperatures is critical to improve the rate-capability of the CG electrode. Therefore, the remarkable rate-capability of CG-1000 can be attributed to the combination of effective utilization of the surface-controlled charge storage mechanism and facilitated ion and electron transport through the 3D conductive matrix. The room-temperature cycling stability of CG-1000 was assessed up to 300 cycles at a current density of $0.1\mathrm{A~g}^{-1}$ without potential holding (Figure 3e). CG-1000 quickly stabilized within 20 cycles and maintained a high capacity ${\approx}305~\mathrm{mAh}$ ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ up to 300 cycles with high Coulombic efficiencies $(98.1-99.2\%)$ . A comparison of the charge and discharge profiles between 25 and 300 cycles showed negligible changes without loss of capacity (Figure 3f).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Motivated by the excellent rate-capability of CG-1000 at room temperature, the low-temperature performance of CG-1000 was investigated. The temperature-dependent capacity of CG-1000 was first evaluated by galvanostatic charge/discharge (GCD) cycles at 0.01 A ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ from 20 to $-60~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (Figure 5a,b).  CG-1000 showed steady-state specific capacities of $\approx380\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ at $20~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , ${\approx}290\mathrm{mAh}$ ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ at $0~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ $(\approx76\%$ of its room-temperature capacity), ${\approx}215$ mAh ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ at $-20~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ $(\approx57\%)$ , ${\approx}135\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ at $-40^{\circ}\mathrm{C}(\approx36\%)$ , and $\approx48\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ at $-60~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ $(\approx13\%)$ . It is interesting to observe that the capacity decay from 20 to $-40~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ was mainly due to the capacity loss associated with the diffusioncontrolled Li-ion intercalation reaction in the low potential region below $0.3\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ . However, a significant decrease in the capacity at an extremely low temperature of $-60~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ may be due to the low ionic conductivity of 1 M $\mathrm{LiPF_{6}}$ in EC:EMC:MP at $-60~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . Specifically, the conductivity measured at $-60~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ showed about a 10-fold reduced value of $\approx0.6\times10^{-3}\mathrm{Scm^{-1}}$ compared to that measured at $20~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ $(\approx1.2\times10^{-2}\mathrm{Scm^{-1}})$ (Figure S8, Supporting Information). Such a good correlation between the capacity of CG-1000 and the ionic conductivity of the electrolyte suggests that the low-temperature performance of CG-1000 can be further improved by using an electrolyte with higher ionic conductivity under extremely low-temperature conditions. After the low-temperature tests down to $-60~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , CG-800 successfully recovered its steady-state capacity at $20~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (before/after lowtemperature tests: ${\approx}254\mathrm{mAh}$ $\mathrm{g^{-1}}/{\approx}242\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}})$ (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Such excellent resiliency indicates that the performance of CG anode is not deteriorated by low-temperature operation, which can dramatically improve the operation stability under low-temperature conditions. Furthermore, the measured temperature-dependent charge capacity of CG-1000 was compared to that of a conventional graphite anode from 20 to $-60~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (Figure 5c). The graphite anode delivered a high charge capacity of $\approx340\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ at $20~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , but it was steeply fallen when the temperature dropped below $0~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and lost the most of its charge storage capability at $-40~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (Figure S10, Supporting Information). CG-1000, on the other hand, showed an almost linear decay with decreasing temperature, achieving significantly higher capacity than the graphite anode at all temperatures. The results clearly show that the surface-controlled charge storage mechanism has an advantage over the diffusion-controlled charge storage mechanism in improving the charge storage capability under low-temperature conditions. It should be emphasized that a low-temperature performance of CG-1000 is superior to previously reported anodes for low-temperature operation (Table S1, Supporting Information). Such a remarkable charge storage capability of CG-1000 at low-temperature conditions can be attributed to the combination of effective utilization of the surface-controlled charge storage mechanism and facilitated ion and electron transport through the 3D conductive matrix.  The rate-dependent capacity of CG-1000 was further evaluated at a low temperature of $-40~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (Figure 5d). At a low current density of 0.01 A ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ $(\approx0.037\mathrm{~C~})$ , CG-1000 exhibited a capacity of ${\approx}127$ mAh ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ . At a high current density of 2 A ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ $\left({\approx}7.4\mathrm{~C}\right)$ , CG-1000 still delivered a steady-state capacity of $\approx52\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ , corresponding to ${\approx}33.5\%$ of that at $0.01\mathrm{A}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ . As the current density resumed to 0.01 A ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ , the specific capacity of CG-1000 was restored to ${\approx}154\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ . Such superior rate-performance under ultralow-temperature conditions indicates the fast charge storage kinetics of CG-1000 based on the surface-controlled charge storage mechanism. Furthermore, at a low temperature of $-40~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , the cycling stability of CG-1000 was assessed at 0.01 A ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ up to 100 cycles (Figure  5e). CG-1000 maintained its steady-state capacity of ${\approx}154$ mAh ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ with high Coulombic efficiencies $(>99.1\%)$ for 100 cycles (Figure  5f). The excellent cycling stability of CG-1000 can be attributed to its predominant surface-controlled charge storage mechanism that does not involve substantial volume changes. It should be noted that, for CG-1000, an anode with a sloped profile during charge and discharge can be a safety advantage as it reduces the possibility of metal plating under low temperature conditions. In this regard, we investigated the structural changes of CG-1000 during the cycling processes by the SEM images before and after cycling at both room- and low-temperature conditions (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Despite the formation of the SEI layer on the surface of graphene, the CG particles retained its quasi-spherical morphology with a relatively smooth surface after the cycling processes. In addition, the CG particles were securely entangled within the electrically conductive FWNT matrix without any significant structural degradation. Such structural stability of the CG-1000 electrode during the cycling process is due to the unique morphology of CG that allows the effective utilization of the surface charge storage mechanism.
