- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Fig. 1 highlights the differences between the pyrochlore and fluorite phases and three conventional routes to induce the ODT. The ideal pyrochlore (space group 227, $F d\bar{3}m\cdot$ is an ordered (cations and anions) $2\times2\times2$ variant of the disordered fluorite unit cell. This ordered ${\tt A}_{2}{\tt B}_{2}{\tt O}_{7}$ pyrochlore structure, where the ${\mathsf{A O}}_{8}$ polyhedron is a distorted scalenohedron and the ${\tt B O}_{6}$ polyhedron is a perfect octahedron, is signified by the $48f$ oxygen positional parameter $x=0.3125$ . Disordering initiates as the $48f$ site migrates towards $x=0.375$ (or $3/8$ , the ideal oxygen position in a disordered fluorite phase [55]). It is energetically unfavorable for the $48f$ oxygen in the pyrochlore phase to have $x\gtrsim0.36$ due to an increasing O-0 repulsive force. Instead, the $48f$ oxygen begin to populate the 8a vacancy sites, thus initiating the disordering process [60]. In the schematic of the fluorite structure, the ${\mathsf{A O}}_{8}$ polyhedron approaches a perfect cube while the ${\tt B O}_{6}$ polyhedron becomes a distorted octahedron. However, the perfect polyhedrons are not reached in the real disordered fluorite structure as the cations disorder leads to the formation of $(\mathsf{A},\mathsf{B})0_{7}$ polyhedrons on average (instead of the perfect $\mathsf{A O}_{8}$ and ${\tt B O}_{6}$ polyhedrons).  The three most common routes to induce the ODT are via increasing temperature, the difference in the cation radii, and nonstoichiometry [66]. First, most rare earth zirconate and hafnate pyrochlores undergo temperature-induced disordering transition at $\geq1600^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ [67]. Second, the ordered pyrochlore structure is preferred if the size difference between the A- and B-site cations is significant, while the disordered fluorite structure is preferred if the cations are of similar radii. Here, the critical radius ratio for the ODT is approximately 1.46 (disordering if $r_{A}^{V I I I}/~r_{B}^{V I}<\sim1.46),$ , where the radii of the A- and $\mathtt{B}$ -site are assumed to be eight- and sixcoordinated [57,66]. Third, non-stoichiometry arising from an imbalance in the concentration of A- and B-site cations, of the form $A_{2\pm y}B_{2\pm y}O_{7\pm\delta}$ , can induce an ODT. For rare earth zirconates and hafnates, the ODT (disordering) occurs when $y>~0.1-0.2$ [67].

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The XRD patterns of the P1N1 series are shown in Fig. 2(a). Fig. 2(b) shows the logarithmic intensity of the peak evolution of the (331) superstructure peak for the ordered pyrochlore phase. All specimens exhibit a single high-entropy solid solution phase (of either pyrochlore or fluorite structure). No visible secondary phase peak was present in any XRD patterns. It is evident that the lattice parameter variation is negligible in this series with changing $x$ in the mixed $\mathrm{P}1_{1-x}\mathrm{N}1_{x}$ compositions.  Here, we use the high-entropy nomenclature [75] loosely (as it becomes more vague for ceramics with two cation sublattices). The calculated ideal mixing entropies for the pyrochlore and fluorite phases for the P1N1 series are shown in Fig. 2(c). Here, we assume Sc atoms occupy the B-site (as shown by STEM EDS discussed later) and all rare earth elements prefer to occupy the Asite for ordered pyrochlore phases. However, a small fraction (up to $\sim10\%$ if we “force” N1 to be pyrochlore) of rare earth elements have to occupy the B-site to maintain the site balance, where we assume that they maintain the same relative ratios on A- and Bsites for simplification (which will result in only small errors given only low fractions of anti-site rare earth elements on the B site). As shown in Fig. 2(c) the ideal mixing entropies are $1.60{-}1.91k_{\mathrm{B}}$ per cation for the fluorite phases with one cation sublattice, and they are $1.13\substack{-1.48k_{\mathrm{B}}}$ per cation for the pyrochlore phases with two cation sublattices (albeit that most of them are “high-entropy” on the A sublattice with $1.56{-}1.73k_{\mathrm{B}}$ per cation for $0.1\leq x\leq0.75$ so they may be claimed as HECs based on the definition in Ref. [23]). More rigorously, the duodenary pyrochlore phases should be called compositionally-complex ceramics (CCCs) following our recent discussion [23-25].  Fig.2(c)also showed that the ODT is not driven by the difference in the ideal mixing entropy. Overall, the difference in the mixing entropies of ordered pyrochlore and disordered fluorite oxides does not change significantly. Interestingly, $(\Delta S_{m i x}^{F l u o r i t e}-\Delta S_{m i x}^{P y r o c h l o r e})$ is in fact smaller when the disordered fluorite phase is stable $(0.71k_{\mathrm{B}}$ to $0.62k_{\mathrm{B}}$ per cation for $x=0.9$ to 1) than that when the ordered pyrochlore phase is stable $(0.78k_{\mathrm{B}}$ to $0.87k_{\mathrm{B}}$ per cation for $x=0$ to 0.75). Thus, this ODT (disordering) is not driven by the mixing entropy, but it is likely driven by the difference in the average radii or the $\mathsf{A}/\mathsf{B}$ ratio, as discussed further in $\S4.4.$ .  We also note that we do not claim that these (pyrochlore and fluorite) solid-solution phases are entropy stabilized. Interestingly, for the majority of compositions in the P1N1 series $(x=0–0.75)$ , the medium-entropy pyrochlore phases $1.13{-}1.48k_{\mathrm{B}}$ per cation on average for A and B sublattices) are more stable than the high-entropy fluorite phases $(1.91-1.34k_{\mathrm{B}}$ per cation), as shown in Fig. 2(c).

It is important to note that the existence of anti-site defects is inevitable because of both entropy effects and the stoichiometrically constraint. Short-range ordering and (some levels of) clustering can exist.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The $r_{A}/r_{B}$ ratios for specimens are shown in Fig. 6(a). Prior studies, based mostly on stoichiometric ternary oxides, suggested that the ordered pyrochlore structure should form when $r_{A}/r_{B}>1.46$ with an estimated error of $\pm\:0.003$ [79]. Our results of duodenary oxides showed an ODT (or disordering) occurred at higher threshold of $r_{A}/r_{B}\sim1.48$ in the P1N1 series. The observed higher tendency to disorder can be explained from two facts. First, highentropy solid solutions with severe lattice distortion promote disordering. Second, the prior threshold of ${\sim}1.46$ was proposed based on stoichiometric ternary oxides. - Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Fig. 7(a) displays the measured room temperature Young's modulus of all the duodenary oxides. Young’s modulus has a noticeable increase in all mixed compositions of P1N1 above the rule-ofmixture (RoM) averages. More interestingly, sharp increases occur at low concentrations of mixing at both sides. Such sharp increases was explained from a strongly negative excess volume of mixing or a negative enthalpy of mixing in a prior study [80]. The negative excess volume of mixing is unlikely in this case because the measured lattice parameters do not deviate significantly from Vegard’s law, as shown in Fig. 7(b). A recent study suggested that a negative enthalpy of mixing in pyrochlore-fluorite $\mathrm{Ho}_{2}\mathrm{Ti}_{2-x}\mathrm{Zr}_{x}\mathrm{O}_{7}$ resulted in short-range ordering [64]. We hypothesize that similar short-range chemical or structural orders, which are almost inevitable for such complex high-entropy oxides according to a recent thermodynamic analysis [81], can explain our observation of increased stiffness in this duodenary P1N1 series. Moreover, a softening in modulus is also seen after the ODT, which is expected as the pyrochlore is generally stiffer than the fluorite [82]. At intermediate doping levels, the trend is nearly linear.  The measured thermal conductivity $(k)$ ,as shown in Fig. 7(c), also sharply changes at low concentrations of mixing on both ends, in comparison with the RoM values. However, the change is negative (with more reduced $k$ from RoM) at the P1 side, but positive at the N1 side. A more considerable drop in thermal conductivity is seen after the ODT, which may be related to lattice softening. Similar to prior discussion, possible formation of short-range chemical or structural orders may help explain the sharp decrease in thermal conductivity [83,84], at least in the P1 end. Reductions in thermal conductivity may also be due to so-called “locon” effects where certain atoms may profoundly distort the local field that allows for localized, non-propagating phonons [85-88]. The increase in thermal conductivity at the N1 end can be attributed to the increased modulus.  Notably, the $E/k$ ratios are enhanced above the RoM values consequently, compared to the two endmembers (P1 and N1, which have similar $E/k$ ratios of ${\sim}175~\mathrm{GPa}\cdot\mathrm{m}\cdot\mathrm{K}\cdot W\stackrel{{-}}{\sim}1\cdot$ . This effect is more pronounced on the P1-rich side. At intermediate concentrations, the changes are typically linear. In all cases, the measured properties tend to deviate significantly from the RoM values. The ability to increase the $E/k$ ratio is scientifically interesting, as the reduction of thermal conductivity typically accompanies with decreasing modulus due to both of their strong dependence on phonon group velocity. While we acknowledge that a low modulus (E) will help with stress compliance for TBC applications practically, the $E/k$ ratio thus serves as a great descriptor to gauge the phonon scattering rate (or potential) of a composition.
