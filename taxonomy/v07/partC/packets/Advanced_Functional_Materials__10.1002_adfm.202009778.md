- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): We investigated the efficiency of Na deposition on the graphite, hard carbon, soft carbon, and bare Cu electrode hosts under $2\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ for $2\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , respectively. As shown in Figure 1a, the graphite electrode host exhibits superior Na plating–stripping efficiency, achieving a stable average efficiency of $99.86\%$ over 900 cycles (superior to most reported results, see Table S1 in the Supporting Information). By contrast, those control cells display much fluctuant efficiency and less cycling stability. To study the effects of cycling stability of Na ions storage on Na deposition efficiency, the galvanostatic charging-discharging (between 0 and 2.5 V) behavior of graphite was compared with hard carbon and soft carbon anodes (Figure S1a–c, Supporting Information), and Na intercalation capacity was controlled in parallel levels. Before the Na deposition begins, [Na-DEGDME] was cointercalated into graphite above 0  V,[19–20] whereas the $\mathrm{Na^{+}}$ was absorbed or/and intercalated into soft carbon or hard carbon.[21] The three carbon anodes show comparable cycling stability (Figure S1d, Supporting Information). Therefore, the discriminative Na deposition efficiency is not affected by their Na ions storage performance.  The reversible capacity of [Na-DEGDME]+ cointercalation is near $0.3\mathrm{mAh~cm^{-2}}$ at a current density of $2~\mathrm{mA}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ (Figure  1b). With the discharging process further persisting, the cointercalated graphite hosts induced a low Na nucleation overpotential of $15~\mathrm{mV}$ and a deposition potential of $-10~\mathrm{mV}$ in the first cycle (Figure 1c). When the cycle number increased up to 900, the Na deposition potential on graphite experienced a gradual and modest decline from $^{-10}$ to $-25~\mathrm{mV},$ whereas the Na nucleation overpotential maintained almost constant. Notably, the initial Na nucleation overpotentials of hard carbon and soft carbon are much lower than that of bare Cu (over $90\mathrm{mV})$ ) (Figure S2a, Supporting Information). Although the Na deposition potential on the other three electrodes remained stable during the previous 200 cycles (Figure S2b–d, Supporting Information), their efficiency fluctuated severely (Figure  1a). This may be attributed to the formation of unstable SEI and inactive Na metal during Na plating–stripping, which is analogous to previous studies.[10b,17] Stable Na deposition on graphite electrode was also achieved at $1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ with an area capacity of $1~\mathrm{mA}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ (Figure  1d). As the area capacity climbed up to $5\mathrm{mAh~cm^{-2}}$ , efficiency hold steady less than 100 cycles. With the current density rising from 1 to $5\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , the graphite delivered decreased cointercalation capacity and exhibited a lower Na deposition potential of $-25\mathrm{mV}$ (Figure  1e). During Na deposition-stripping processes, the interfacial resistance of graphite | Na cell degraded gradually and remained low in principle (Figure 1f), which was responsible for the low deposition potential over stable 900 cycles.

- path: /home/aid1/Documents/causalmat/matmech/Advanced_Functional_Materials/10.1002_adfm.202009778/images/af10b2ec0ef8ed54d5522266942f0e048ebdce2966e9facec13b0cc4f191b242.jpg
- caption: Figure 5. DFT calculations for Na plating behavior on the graphite surface. During initial plating stages, Na atoms tend to deposit on the lateral surface of [Na-DEGDME]+ intercalated graphite (Path 1). As the plating persists, approximate three Na cells will be accommodated on neighboring two graphite layers perpendicular to the basal surface due to low lattice mismatch between Na and cointercalated graphite.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To reveal the underlying mechanism of uniform Na electrodeposition, we performed DFT calculations for Na plating behavior on the graphite surface. In the simulation, double stacked [Na-DEGDME]+ complexes are intercalated into the graphite lattice,[19a] and Na can be deposited on its lateral surface. Figure 5 illustrates two potential reaction paths for Na deposition at an atomistic level. For the first path, Na is adsorbed on the lateral surface of graphite. For the second path, Na is bonded to the Na crystal. To determine the favored path, the adsorption energy of Na on the graphite surface is calculated, taking the form  For the first scenario, one Na atom is adsorbed on the surface of graphite cointercalated with stacked [Na-DEGDME]+ complexes. The Na atom is located between the graphite sheet and [Na-DEGDME]+ complex after equilibrium. The calculated adsorption energy is $-1.67\mathrm{~eV}_{\mathrm{{i}}}$ , which indicates that the Na adsorption is energetically favored. Furthermore, it implies that compared to Path 2, Path 1 is the dominant process in the early stages of Na plating. As a result, Na has the propensity to cover the surface of graphite with [Na-DEGDME]+ complexes due to their strong interactions. Therefore, the preferred Na adsorption contributes to uniform Na electrodeposition.  When two Na atoms are adsorbed on the graphite surface, the average adsorption energy is $-1.35~\mathrm{eV}_{\mathrm{i}}$ , showing a good adsorption property. The two Na atoms are both relaxed to the locations between the graphite sheets and [Na-DEGDME]+ complexes. As depicted in Figure  5, the equilibrated structure presents an interlayer spacing of $12\textup{\AA}$ for graphite with double stacked [Na-DEGDME]+ complexes,[19a] and the two Na atoms adsorbed have a distance of $8.6\textup{\AA}$ . In our DFT calculations, the lattice constant of Na crystal is $4.2\mathring{\mathrm{~A~}}$ . For the lateral surface of graphite, three Na cells could be approximately held over two neighboring atomic layers, which leads to a lattice mismatch of $4.76\%$ . The low lattice strain helps homogeneous Na plating and growth on the graphite surface. In a previous study, it has been demonstrated that a low lattice mismatch leads to uniform nucleation and growth of metal anodes, thereby improving their electrochemical properties.[23]

To further establish stable Na deposition on graphite anodes, we constructed full cells pairing graphite anodes under three different state of charge with $\mathrm{Na}_{4}\mathrm{Fe}_{3}(\mathrm{PO}_{4})_{2}(\mathrm{P}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{7})$ cathodes. $\mathrm{Na}_{4}\mathrm{Fe}_{3}(\mathrm{PO}_{4})_{2}(\mathrm{P}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{7})$ cathode was controlled with an area capacity of $1.5\mathrm{mAh~cm^{-2}},$ , and the mass load of graphite anode was around $3~\mathrm{mg~cm}^{-2}$ corresponding to a cointercalation capacity of $0.3{-}0.4\mathrm{mAhcm^{-2}}.$ . Thereby, in the full cell construction, a great proportion of Na source from cathode will be deposited on the graphite anode during deep charging except coinserting into graphite. As shown in Figure  6a, the pristine graphite paralleled with $\mathrm{Na}_{4}\mathrm{Fe}_{3}(\mathrm{PO}_{4})_{2}(\mathrm{P}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{7})$ cathode displayed a larger sloping region (1–3  V) capacity than the other two during charging, corresponding to the cointercalation process. The graphite samples discharged to $0\mathrm{~V~}$ and predeposited $0.5\mathrm{mAh~cm}^{-2}$ exhibited analogous voltage profiles to the $\mathrm{Na}_{4}\mathrm{Fe}_{3}(\mathrm{PO}_{4})_{2}(\mathrm{P}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{7})$ | Na half cells (Figure S6a, Supporting Information), indicating the occurrence of Na deposition on graphite from the beginning charging. The calculated energy density of full cell is ${\approx}245$ Wh $\mathrm{kg}^{-1}$ based on the total mass of $\mathrm{Na}_{4}\mathrm{Fe}_{3}(\mathrm{PO}_{4})_{2}(\mathrm{P}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{7})$ , graphite and 0.5 mAh $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ predeposited Na. All the full cells exhibited a capacity deterioration in the initial 10 cycles (Figure  6b). Other than the Na metal plating– stripping loss, the cycling attenuation of $\mathrm{Na}_{4}\mathrm{Fe}_{3}(\mathrm{PO}_{4})_{2}(\mathrm{P}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{7})$ cathode (Figure S6b, Supporting Information) is also responsible for the capacity loss. A reversible capacity of $95\mathrm{mAh~g^{-1}}$ (based on the mass of $\mathrm{Na}_{4}\mathrm{Fe}_{3}(\mathrm{PO}_{4})_{2}(\mathrm{P}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{7})$ was still obtained at a current density of $0.75\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ over 300 cycles, corresponding to a capacity retention of $91\%$ for the $0.5\mathrm{mAh~cm}^{-2}$ Na predeposited graphite sample. By contrast, the full cell using pristine graphite anode only delivered a reversible capacity of $33\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}}$ $(\mathrm{Na}_{4}\mathrm{Fe}_{3}(\mathrm{PO}_{4})_{2}(\mathrm{P}_{2}\mathrm{O}_{7})$ after 200 cycles, then remaining steady in the following cycles induced by the cointercalation process. This is ascribed to the gradual Na loss during plating–stripping processes, and then the cointercalation process became dominant. As the cycling goes on, the voltage profile of full cell using predeposited graphite evolved with imperceptible changes (Figure  6c), meaning remarkable cycling stability superior to the pristine graphite (Figure 6d).
