- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Herein, we demonstrate that the wet ball-milling technique can be successfully used with bulk $\alpha$ -germanium crystals to produce $\alpha$ -germanium NLs ( $\alpha$ -Ge NLs) in a process that can be easily upscaled to gram production. This procedure only requires a mixture of isopropyl alcohol $\mathrm{(^{i}P r O H)}$ -water. Importantly, it does not need the addition of further surfactants that, in many cases, results in products difficult to manipulate. Besides, the $\alpha$ Ge NLs can be easily redispersed using a shear-mixer, yielding very stable suspensions, over weeks, of micrometer-scale NLs even in ambient conditions. As we shall see, the so-formed $\alpha$ Ge NLs show the absence of side-products, no significant oxidation, and high-crystallinity. Remarkably, theoretical calculations predict a thickness-dependent bandgap of these NLs that is confirmed further by electron energy-loss spectroscopy (EELS) of $_{\alpha-\mathrm{Ge}}$ NLs with different orientations. This work, summarized graphically in Figure 1, evidences the possibility of creating a very general class of new 2D structures using LPE and covalent crystals as precursors.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): We also confirmed the crystallinity and phase purity of the $\alpha$ Ge NLs by powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD). Figure 3a shows a comparison between the PXRD patterns of the bulk $\alpha$ -germanium crystals and the $\alpha$ -Ge NLs, showing that after the exfoliation process, the NLs do not suffer any measurable change on their crystalline structure compared to that expected for the canonical $\alpha$ -phase of germanium (JCPDS card no. 03-065-0333). There is a clear change on peaks relative intensity on passing from bulk to NL, which is indicative of passing from an isotropic crystal to preferential orientations typical of layered material. In this case, the relative intensity of the (111) and (311) peaks increases. This agrees with the fact that the preferential orientations seem to be along the (111) and (311) directions.  PXRD does not provide information on the chemical state of the NL surface. In order to gain insight on this issue, we carried out X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using synchrotron light of $675~\mathrm{eV}$ and Mg $\mathrm{K}_{\alpha}$ radiation ( $(1253.6~\mathrm{eV},$ see also Section S8, Supporting Information) on $\alpha$ Ge NLs deposited on highly oriented pyrolytic graphite. We measured the Ge $3\mathrm{p}$ core level with a binding energy of $122~\mathrm{eV}$ (Figure  3b and Figure S12, Supporting Information). The surface sensitivity is different for each photon energy, as the electron mean free path changes from ${\approx}1.1~\mathrm{nm}$ (for $h\nu=675~\mathrm{eV},$ to ${\approx}1.9~\mathrm{nm}$ (for $h\nu=1253.6\mathrm{eV}$ ). The Ge 3p core level is deconvoluted in three components 1, 2, and 3 (Figure 3b and Figure S12, Supporting Information). Component 1 is assigned to elemental germanium (unoxidized). Component 2 (shift of $2.5~\mathrm{eV}$ with respect to (wrt) Ge) corresponds to $\mathrm{Ge}^{3+}$ , but it may include a contribution from $\mathrm{Ge}^{2+}$ .[10] Component 3, with a shift of $4.0~\mathrm{eV}$ wrt to Ge, matches $\mathrm{Ge^{4+}}$ , i.e., $\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ .[10] The ratio $\mathrm{Ge}/\mathrm{GeO}_{2}$ changes dramatically when the escape depth increases, revealing that the ${\mathrm{GeO}}_{2}$ corresponds to a surface layer of ${\approx}1~\mathrm{nm}$ thickness. This value is compatible with the expected thickness for a layer of native oxide on the Ge flakes due to air exposure before the XPS measurements.[10]

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure 5a shows the calculated bandgap structure for singleand four-layer $\alpha$ -Ge NLs with the abovementioned orientations. The germanium surface atoms have been saturated with H atoms to suppress dangling bonds (similar results are obtained upon OH saturation, see Section S10, Supporting Information) following our experimental observations. It is worth mentioning that a direct bandgap is obtained up to six to seven monolayers, i.e., $\alpha$ Ge NLs of ${\approx}3~\mathrm{nm}$ in thickness with an appreciable energy variation upon a diminution of NL thickness down to $1\textrm{n m}$ (Figure 5b). This variation is significant in the case of the (110) and (112) surfaces with the bandgap increase up to $2.0~\mathrm{eV}$ at $1\textrm{n m}$ thickness and almost negligible in the (111) case. Note also that we are presenting results using a standard approximation (generalized gradient approximation) to our functional. It is well known that bulk germanium does not present a gap in this approximation, as it can be already anticipated in Figure 5b for the (111) surface where the gap is almost closed. Improved calculations using a hybrid functional (Section S10, Supporting Information) open a gap in the bulk structure and increase the gap of the slabs, but do not change the relative gap evolution with thickness in this range. Figure  5c depicts the bandgap of the sample as a function of the thickness for a flake oriented along the [112] direction. The bandgap value on the edges of the sample is higher than that in the bulk, being about $0.85~\mathrm{eV}$ on the edges and decreasing down to $0.65\mathrm{eV}$ on thicker $\alpha$ Ge NLs. Figure 5d displays the lateral variations of the thickness of the [112] oriented $\alpha$ Ge NLs, in layers down to a thickness of $2.5\mathrm{nm}$ (notice that measurements below this value show a large dispersion in bandgap values as a consequence of the oxide layer). Similar measurements carried out in $\alpha$ Ge NLs with [111] orientation do not show a bandgap dependence with the thickness but rather a constant value (Figure S14, Supporting Information). These experimental results agree with the theoretical bandgap calculations in which only for very small thicknesses a bandgap variation is expected for the [111] orientation, out of the experimental detection limit, while a larger variation is predicted for the [112] orientation (Figure  5b). As already mentioned, flakes oriented so that the electron beam lies down the [112] direction probably present oriented surfaces or terraces in the $\langle110\rangle$ directions, since they are energetically more favorable. In any case their gap behavior with thickness is rather similar and in qualitative agreement with the predictions.
