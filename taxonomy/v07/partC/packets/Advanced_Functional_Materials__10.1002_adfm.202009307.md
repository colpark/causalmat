- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The affinity of PFS-CNT to transition metals was first evaluated in electrosorption experiments containing $1\mathrm{mm}$ of a metal oxyanion sodium salt $(\mathrm{Na}_{2}\mathrm{HAsO}_{4},$ ${\mathrm{Na}}_{2}{\mathrm{MoO}}_{4},$ or ${\mathrm{NaVO}}_{3}$ ) and $20\mathrm{mM}$ sodium perchlorate supporting electrolyte. Under chronoamperometric conditions, PFS-CNT showed rapid uptake kinetics, reaching equilibrium within $20~\mathrm{{min}}$ for all oxyanions tested (Figure 2b). PFS-CNT achieved uptakes of $65.7\mathrm{mg~g}^{-1}$ As at $1.0\:\mathrm{V}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ , $30.8\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{g}^{-1}\mathrm{V}$ at $0.8\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ , and $19.1~\mathrm{mg~g^{-1}}$ Mo at $0.8~\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ , demonstrating that PFS-CNT can successfully capture these oxyanions in the presence of excess competing perchlorate anions. Even in the absence of supporting electrolyte, PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT still display comparable kinetics with $1\mathrm{mm}$ of transition metal oxyanion in solution, achieving equilibrium uptake within $20\mathrm{min}$ (Figure S4.9, Supporting Information)—which confirms applicability of these redox-systems even for metal recovery in lowsalinity systems.  Arsenate was taken first as a model oxyanion for electrode performance due to its importance for environmental remediation, and prior studies regarding its electrosorption performance.[2a,23] At tested potentials between 0 and $1.0\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ , PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT displayed similar uptake capacities of arsenate, with both PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT achieving a maximum capacity of approximately $60~\mathrm{mg~g^{-1}}$ As at potentials higher than $0.6~\mathrm{V}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ (Figure 2c). The electrosorption of arsenate was verified with X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS), showing a clear peak for arsenic on the electrode after adsorption which was absent in the spectra for the unused electrode (Figure 2e). Fe 2p XPS of PFS-CNT after electrosorption agrees that uptake reaches a maximum at $0.6~\mathrm{V}$ $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ . The Fe 2p region displays strong spin-orbit splitting with Fe 2p binding energies of 707.8 and $720.6~\mathrm{eV}$ for the $2\mathrm{p}_{3/2}$ and $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$ transitions, respectively, for unoxidized Fc (referenced to $\mathrm{C-C\subset}$ 1s at $284.8~\mathrm{eV}_{\cdot}$ .[24] When oxidized to $\mathrm{Fc^{+}}$ , these binding energies shift upward in binding energy to 711.6 and $725.4~\mathrm{eV}$ for the $2{\tt p}_{3/2}$ and $2\mathrm{p}_{1/2}$ transitions, respectively (Figure 2d), and these values are consistent with previously reported $\mathrm{F}\mathrm{c}^{+}$ binding energies.[25] At $0.4\mathrm{~V},$ a significant degree of Fc is still unoxidized, as shown by the peaks at 707.8 and $720.6~\mathrm{eV.}$ At $0.6~\mathrm{V}$ and above, the peaks at 707.8 and $720.6~\mathrm{eV}$ disappear with only the peaks corresponding to $\mathrm{Fc^{+}}$ (711.6 and $725.4\mathrm{eV})$ ) remaining, suggesting that most of the Fc sites have been oxidized at $0.6~\mathrm{~V~}$ and may explain why raising the potential above $0.6~\mathrm{~V~}$ did not significantly increase the uptake capacity of PFS-CNT. Based on XPS survey quantification, arsenate and perchlorate were present on the surface in a ratio of 0.81 As:1 Cl (Figure S2.5, Supporting Information). This ratio is much higher than the initial ratio of arsenate to perchlorate in solution $(1\mathrm{As}{\cdot}20\mathrm{Cl})$ ) and indicates that PFS, like PVF,[2a] can also selectively target arsenate in perchlorate-containing solution.  Upon application of a reducing potential, $\mathrm{Fc^{+}}$ can be reduced back to Fc, and the bound anion is expelled. A series of release conditions were tested with arsenate from 0.0 to $-0.4\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ with both PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT (Figure  2f). After electrosorption was performed in a solution containing $1~\mathrm{mm}$ arsenate and $20~\mathrm{mm}$ perchlorate, a reducing potential was applied, and anions were expelled into the same solution that was used to test electrosorption. PVF-CNT showed better release, regenerating up to $70.5\%$ of the captured arsenic upon application of $-0.4\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ after $^{1\mathrm{~h~}}$ . Maximum release of arsenate from PFS-CNT was $47.5\%$ at $-0.2\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ . The incomplete release of arsenate is likely due to the incomplete reduction of PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT (Figure S2.11, Supporting Information). In addition, the lesser release of arsenate from PFS-CNT than from PVF-CNT appears to be due to PFS-CNT having difficulty reducing $\mathrm{Fc^{+}}$ back to Fc. As measured via XPS, $2.4\%$ of the $\mathrm{F}\mathrm{c}^{+}$ was reduced at $0.0~\mathrm{V}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ and $2.5\%$ was reduced at $-0.4\mathrm{~V~}$ in PFS-CNT. In contrast, for PVF-CNT, $86\%$ of the $\mathrm{Fc^{+}}$ was reduced at $0.0~\mathrm{V}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ and $84\%$ of the $\mathtt{F c^{+}}$ was reduced at $-0.4\:\mathrm{V}$ (Figure S2.11, Supporting Information). Shorter cycle times between $0.8\mathrm{~V~}$ oxidation and $-0.4\mathrm{~V~}$ reduction seemed to improve the $\mathrm{F}\mathrm{c}^{+}$ reduction in PFS-CNT (Figure S2.12, Supporting Information). Following a cycle of $0.8~\mathrm{~V~}$ for $20\mathrm{min}$ and $-0.4\mathrm{~V~}$ for $40\mathrm{min}$ , $32\%$ of PFS-CNT was in a reduced state. When this cycle was shortened to $0.8~\mathrm{V}$ for 1 min and $-0.4\:\mathrm{V}$ for 2 min, $57\%$ of PFSCNT was in a reduced state, as measured by XPS. Recent work suggested that reactive oxygen species could potentially oxidize ferrocene-containing systems under continued use,[26] and thus could hamper $\mathrm{Fc^{+}}$ reduction. PFS in particular has shown a higher sensitivity to secondary reactions when oxidized.[27] Thus, we expect that further chemical modifications and operation-parameter screening under both aerobic and anaerobic conditions are required to provide insights on enhancing regeneration in the future.  When electrochemically releasing into a clean solution without any electrolyte, PVF-CNT showed comparable release behavior, releasing $62.8\%$ , while PFS-CNT was able to release $96\%$ of the bound arsenic (Figure S4.10, Supporting Information). PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT release kinetics were typically slower than adsorption kinetics, requiring up to $2\textrm{h}$ to fully release bound oxyanions depending on the transition metal (Figure S4.10, Supporting Information). The slower release kinetics relative to adsorption has been observed before using in situ TEM studies with PVF-films on gold for chromate adsorption.[2a] While adsorption can be fast due to enhanced electrostatics between oxyanions and the ferrocenium cation moiety, oxyanion release from a reduced redox-polymer is mostly entropic and thus much slower.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The selectivity of PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT was screened for the six heavy metal anions $(\mathrm{HAsO}_{4})^{2-}$ , $\mathrm{CrO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ , $\mathrm{SeO}_{4}{}^{2-}.$ $\mathrm{MoO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ , $\mathrm{VO}_{3}^{-}$ , and $\mathrm{ReO_{4}}^{-}$ ) in binary competitive adsorption tests. PVFCNT or PFS-CNT was immersed in a solution containing $1\mathrm{m}\mathbf{M}$ of two oxyanion sodium salts (2 mm total, e.g., 1 mm $\mathrm{Na}_{2}\mathrm{MoO}_{4}$ and $1\mathrm{m}\mathbf{M}$ ${\mathrm{Na}}_{2}{\mathrm{CrO}}_{4})$ , and electrosorption was induced at various potentials. Selectivity was measured using a separation factor, defined between two species (A and B) to be:  where the $N_{\mathrm{A,ads}}$ is the molar quantity of A adsorbed onto the electrode, $N_{\mathrm{B,ads}}$ is the molar quantity of B adsorbed onto the electrode, $N_{\mathrm{A,sol}}$ is the molar quantity of A remaining in solution, and $N_{\mathrm{B,sol}}$ is the molar quantity of B remaining in solution. Separation factors greater than 1 indicate higher selectivity for A as compared to B, while separation factors less than 1 indicate higher selectivity for B as compared to A.  Figure  3a,b shows binary ion-selectivity heat maps for the separation factors obtained using PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT at  $0.8~\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ with the anion labeled for each row as species A and the anion labeled for each column as species B in $\alpha_{\mathrm{A,B}}$ . The goal was to construct a comparative performance map, which can guide polymer selection in multicomponent separations when multiple metals are present within the same mixture. Figure  3c shows the separation factor ratios obtained for PFS-CNT compared to those obtained for PVF-CNT. Boxes highlighted with a green square mark separations where PFS-CNT would yield higher selectivity than PVF-CNT for the specific recovery of the row anion over the column anion. The criteria for higher selectivity is that the separation factor of PFS-CNT for the particular ion combination is higher than that of PVFCNT, and at the same time, the PFS-CNT separation factor is greater than 1 for that particular binary separation. Boxes highlighted with a yellow square mark separations where PVF-CNT would yield higher selectivity for recovery of the row anion. Due to these differences in selectivity performance, we envision the ability to tailor polymer structures for targeted metal recovery processes, depending on the stream compositions (Figure 3d,e).  In 8 of the 10 binary adsorption tests at $0.8~\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/}$ AgCl, PVF and PFS favored the same anion. For example, $\alpha_{\mathrm{VO3-,CrO42-}}$ was 5.2 for PVF-CNT and 39 for PFS-CNT, indicating $\mathrm{VO}_{3}^{-}$ had a greater affinity toward PVF-CNT and PFSCNT than $\mathrm{CrO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ , but PFS was almost an order of magnitude more selective. In the remaining two binary adsorption tests $(\mathrm{MoO}_{4})^{2-}+\mathrm{CrO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ , $\mathrm{HAsO}_{4}{}^{2-}+\mathrm{ReO}_{4}{}^{-})$ ), PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT favored different ions $(\alpha_{\mathrm{MoO42-,CrO42-}}=0.60$ and $\alpha_{\mathrm{HAsO4}2-,\mathrm{ReO4}-}=$ 0.91 for PVF-CNT, $\alpha_{\mathrm{MoO42-,CrO42-}}=2.4$ and $\upalpha_{\mathrm{HAsO42-,ReO4-}}=5.2$ for PFS-CNT), demonstrating that a change in structure from a pendant ferrocene polymer to a main-chain ferrocene polymer can alter the ion-selectivity trend and furthermore, demonstrating that redox-active polymers can be structurally tuned for targeted ion selectivity. $\mathrm{SeO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ was omitted from the heat maps because $\mathrm{SeO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ removal was not significant and resulted in separation factors toward infinity and its inclusion would suppress the colors of other heat map elements (Figure S4.8, Supporting Information). $\mathrm{SeO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ adsorption and removal has been noted to be especially difficult due to its lack of affinity to conventional adsorbents.[28] Even adsorbents that strongly adsorb selenite ions $(\mathrm{SeO}_{3}{}^{2-})$ often cannot effectively adsorb $\mathrm{SeO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ .[29] It has been noted that selenite ions adsorb strongly by forming innersphere complexes while the $\mathrm{SeO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ anions adsorb by forming outer-sphere complexes.[30] The lack of affinity of the metallopolymers to capture $\mathrm{SeO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ may suggest that ferrocene is unlikely to directly ligate or form outer-sphere complexes with $\mathrm{SeO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ . Further chemical design may be needed to create efficient $\mathrm{SeO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ -specific electrosorbents.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Quantum mechanical calculations were performed to elucidate the mechanism of binding. $\mathrm{F}\mathrm{c}^{+}$ and $\mathrm{FcSi}(\mathrm{CH}_{3})_{3}^{+}$ were used to study the effect of an electron donating trimethyl–silane substituent on anion-binding behavior (Figure 4a,b, and Figure S6.1, Supporting Information). Calculated binding energies (ΔG) to $\mathrm{Fc^{+}}$ and $\mathrm{FcSiCH}_{3})_{3}^{+}$ for all six anions are shown in Figure 4c,d. The binding energy to $\mathrm{FcSi(CH_{3})_{3}}^{+}$ was lower than to $\mathrm{F}\mathrm{c}^{+}$ for all six anions (e.g., 9.87 kcal $\mathrm{mol}^{-1}$ for $[\mathrm{Fc-MoO_{4}}]^{-}$ , $7.96\mathrm{kcal~mol^{-1}}$ for $[\mathrm{FcSi}(\mathrm{CH}_{3})_{3}{-}\mathrm{MoO}_{4}]^{-})$ (Figure 4c,d). The lower binding energies to $\mathrm{FcSi(CH_{3})_{3}}^{+}$ are likely due to the electron donating properties of the silane group, which pushes electron density onto the ferrocene and delocalizes its positive charge. Within equivalent structures of [Fc–Anion] and $[\mathrm{FcSi}(\mathrm{CH}_{3})_{3}{-}\mathrm{Anion}]$ , the iron has a much greater positive charge in the former structure (e.g., $0.184\mathrm{~e~}$ in $[\mathrm{Fc-MoO_{4}}]^{-}$ and $0.118\mathrm{~e~}$ in $[\mathrm{FcSi}(\mathrm{CH}_{3})_{3}{-}\mathrm{MoO}_{4}]^{-})$ (Table S6.1, Supporting Information). In the $[\mathrm{FcSi}(\mathrm{CH}_{3})_{3}{-}\mathrm{Anion}]$ structures, much of the positive charge was predicted to be located on the silicon atom (0.406 e in $[\mathrm{FcSi}(\mathrm{CH}_{3})_{3}{-}\mathrm{MoO}_{4}]^{-})$ and as a result, the methyl groups on the silane participated in the binding to anions (Figure  4b). While all tested anion binding energies to $\mathrm{FcSi(CH_{3})_{3}}^{+}$ were lower than to $\mathrm{Fc^{+}}$ , the binding energies shifted by different amounts, which would result in differing anion selectivities between $\mathrm{FcSi(CH_{3})_{3}}^{+}$ and $\mathrm{F}\mathrm{c}^{+}$ .  There appeared to be a negative correlation between the ionization energy of the anion (the energy required to remove an electron from the anion) and the difference in binding energy between that anion and $\mathrm{Fc^{+}}$ and $\mathrm{FcSi(CH_{3})_{3}}^{+}$ (Figure  4e). We hypothesize that a charge-transfer binding mechanism may be dictating this correlation.[2a] Anions with a low ionization energy may be more willing to give up electron density to the cationic binding site, and as such, the electronegativity of the binding site strongly affects the binding energy and subsequent selectivity. In contrast, anions with a high ionization energy may be unwilling to give up electron density and, therefore, would be agnostic to the identity of the binding site. Instead, the binding energy of anions with high ionization energies may be primarily the electrostatic reward of bringing two oppositely charged ions together. The charge-transfer modulation shown by the addition of the silane substituent demonstrates the opportunity for tunable ferrocene polymers with specific substituents for highly targeted ion capture.  It must be noted that while quantum mechanical calculations are powerful to elucidate atomistic binding and charge-transfer mechanisms, they do not account for more macroscopic pheno­ menon that may affect the ability of the electrodes to capture ions. For example, previous electrosorption work has shown that variables such as pore size, ion size, and hydrated ion size play a role in how ions can adsorb to porous charged electrodes.[32] $\mathrm{VO}_{3}^{-}$ , despite having relatively low calculated binding energies $(5.44\mathrm{kcalmol^{-1}}$ with $\mathrm{FcSi(CH_{3})_{3}}^{+}$ and $8.11\mathrm{kcal}\mathrm{mol}^{-1}$ with ${\mathrm{Fc^{+}}}.$ ), showed the greatest overall affinity for both PVFCNT and PFS-CNT as $\alpha_{\mathrm{VO3-,B}}>1$ for all anions B and all potentials except $\alpha_{\mathrm{VO3-,ReO4-}}$ at $0.6~\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ . This may be because $\mathrm{VO}_{3}^{-}$ forms polyanion chains of $[\mathrm{VO}_{3}]_{n}{}^{n-}$ $(n>1)$ .[33] Attachment of one $\mathrm{VO}_{3}^{-}$ to the electrode could include additional $\mathrm{VO}_{3}^{-}$ units in a chain, and allow even over-stoichiometric $\mathrm{VO}_{3}^{-}$ adsorption relative to Fc sites.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The selectivity of PFS-CNT and PVF-CNT changes depending on the applied potential (Figure  5, and Figure S4.3–5, Supporting Information). Figure  5a,b highlights two special cases where ion preference switched for the ferrocene polymers at different potentials. $\alpha_{\mathrm{MoO4}2-,\mathrm{CrO4}2-}$ at an applied potential of $0.5~\mathrm{~V~}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ was 0.28 and 0.43 for PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT, respectively, with more $\mathrm{CrO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ adsorbed than $\mathrm{MoO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ . At $1.0~\mathrm{V}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ , more ${\mathrm{MoO}}_{4}{}^{2-}$ was adsorbed and $\alpha_{\mathrm{MoO42-,CrO42}}$ −was 1.5 and 4.6 for PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT, respectively. A similar swap occurred between $\mathrm{ReO}_{4}^{-}$ and $\mathrm{MoO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ , with $\alpha_{\mathrm{MoO42-,ReO4-}}$ changing from 0.17 for PVF-CNT and 0.48 for PFS-CNT at $0.5\mathrm{~V~}\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ to 1.8 for PVF and 35 for PFS at $1.0~\mathrm{V}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ . Tracking the anion speciation as a function of $\mathrm{pH}$ and applied potential on Pourbaix diagrams, it seems unlikely that the potential-dependent selectivity is a result of changing anion speciation (Figure S5.4,5,  Supporting Information). To support this, there is an absence of anion redox activity on the electrode between different applied potentials, as detected by XPS (Figure 5c–f, and Figure $\mathrm{S}2.6{-}9$ , Supporting Information). The Mo $3\mathrm{d}_{5/2}$ peak at $232.9~\mathrm{eV}$ confirms the oxidation state of ${\mathrm{MoO}}_{4}{}^{2-}$ at all tested potentials while the Cr $2{\tt p}_{3/2}$ peak at $577.2~\mathrm{eV}$ suggests $\mathrm{CrO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ has reduced to a $\mathrm{Cr}(\mathrm{III})$ species (likely between the experiment and characterization since $\mathrm{CrO}_{4}{}^{2-}$ is a strong oxidizing agent) (Figure 5c–f).[34]  A combination of electrostatics and charge-transfer may contribute to the potential-dependent selectivity, in addition to other microscopic pore/solvation effects. We observed that when comparing the separation factors at 0.6 and $1.0\ V$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ , the separation factor shifts in favor of the anion that transfers more charge, an effect seen in 16 out of the 20 anion pair tests at $1.0\:\mathrm{V}$ versus $\mathrm{Ag/AgCl}$ . That is, $\alpha_{\mathrm{A,B}}$ , with anion A transferring more charge than anion B, increased when increasing potential from 0.6 to $1.0~\mathrm{V}$ for 16 out of the 20 anion pair tests (10 anion pairs,  PVF-CNT and PFS-CNT) (Figures S4.3–5 and S6.2,3, Supporting Information). We hypothesize that this may be the result of a driving force toward satisfying charge neutrality at a microscopic level. As the potential of the electrode increases, the charge density on the surface of the electrode also increases and ions that transfer more charge to the electrode surface may begin to outcompete ions that transfer less charge by better satisfying microscopic charge neutrality. A swapping of ion preference $(\alpha_{\mathrm{A,B}}$ crossing 1) was not observed for all anion pairs, however, these results correlate with prior observations that electrode charge density can impact ion-preference at different potentials.[32a,35]  However, other mechanisms for potential-dependent selectivity possibly play a role, due to the complex nature of the electrode–solution interface, so further studies into this correlation are warranted. Previous electrosorption work has shown that the effect of surface change density on the preference of ions adsorbed to that surface is dependent on several factors including ion size, hydrated ion size, and pore size.[32,35] Nearneighbor effects, solvation, and ion-crowding within pores may play a key role in dictating selectivity. Future work using in situ characterization methods will aid in the elucidation of more complex mechanisms for ion selectivity, and shed light into the interplay between structural and potential-dependent selectivity. We believe that potential-dependent selectivity of these polymer electrosorbents introduces a unique approach for controlling ion selectivity, orthogonal from the selectivity that arises from polymer structure—thus allowing for smart, field-controlled strategies for multicomponent ion recovery.
