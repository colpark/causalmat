- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Fig. 2a,b shows TEM bright-field images of a carbon extraction replica of the Ref steel. Coarse cuboidal particles ( $100–500\mathrm{nm}$ in size) are observed (Fig. 2a) and are identified as Ti(C,N) carbonitride by EDs (inset of Fig. 2a). In addition, there exist fine spherical precipitates $\zeta{-25}\mathrm{nm}$ in size) which are identified as TiC carbide by TEM-EDS (inset of Fig. 2b). These Ti(C,N) and TiC particles also exist in the Mo steel because of the identical Ti content of both steels (Table 1). In multi-alloyed systems such as $\left(\mathrm{Mo+Ti}\right)$ - alloyed HSLA steels, Mo tends to adhere to the already-formed TiC carbides. The Mo on the precipitate surface stabilizes the interfacial energy, thereby retarding the coarsening kinetics of precipitates during heat treatment such as the coiling simulation process [48,49]. Because Mo adheres mainly to the pre-existing TiC, it hardly affects the number fraction of precipitates, whereas the average precipitate size decreases slightly from $10.5\pm2.6$ to $9.0\pm$ $2.8~\mathrm{nm}$ within the error range.

- path: /home/aid1/Documents/causalmat/matmech/Acta_Materialia/10.1016_j.actamat.2021.116661/images/39920c53efc83acb647f0bfc8b1b7c95578992ec913e07619821a95ac1df4006.jpg
- caption: Fig. 9. SEM images, IPF maps, and IQ $^+$ IPF maps of the half-sectioned area beneath the fracture surface of the SSRT specimen at the current density of $3\mathrm{mA}\cdot\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ for the (a-c) Ref and (d-f) Mo steels.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): An SEM image of the half-sectioned area beneath the fracture surface of the SSRT specimen (current density: $3\mathrm{mA}.\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ )for the Ref steel is shown in Fig. 9a. Beneath the fracture surface, a few microcracks are observed, as indicated by the arrows. The initiation and propagation characteristics of the microcracks were examined by conducting EBSD analysis on an area identical to the microcracked region. Fig. 9b and c show the IPF and $\mathrm{IQ}\mathrm{~+~}\mathrm{IPF}$ maps, respectively, and the PAGBs are indicated by the black dotted lines in Fig. 9b. The crack path mainly follows the PAGBs, as indicated by the yellow arrows in Fig. 9c. This observation reveals that diffusible H atoms are mainly accumulated at high-angle PAGBs during deformation to reduce the boundary cohesion energy. In this process, an H-enhanced local plasticity (HELP) mechanism resulting from the interaction of H atoms and dislocations works cooperatively to accomplish H-assisted failure along the PAGBs. The H diffusion distance $(d)$ along the lattices is proportional to the diffusivity at a given time (t) according to the relationship $d={\sqrt{2D t}}$ When the specimens pre-charged with H are plastically deformed, dislocations accelerate the movement of $\mathrm{H}$ to increase the H diffusivity via mutual interactions. In the state pre-charged with H, as the charging time is sufficiently long and the charged H content is similar in both steels, it is presumed that the H content trapped at trapping sites such as grain boundaries and dislocations is nearly identical in both steels. The diffusivity of H then becomes a major factor in its interaction with dislocations; thus, the higher H mobility in the Ref steel leads to a higher possibility of combining with dislocations. The dislocation movement is also promoted by the formation of an H-atmosphere, which weakens the typical repulsive forces between dislocations and obstacles [85-87].As the dislocation movement is further enhanced and the repulsion between dislocations and boundaries decreases, more H atoms are transferred to grain boundaries and are concentrated along with dislocations. Furthermore, the continuous transportation of H towards the PAGBs accelerates the attraction of H by generating a local strain field [88]. As a result, microcracks initiate at the weakened PAGBs by the localization of both H and strain to form long cracks with intergranular paths, which produces intergranular facets on the fracture surface (Fig. 7b,c).  Fig. 9d-f show the observation results of the microcrack path that occurred in the SSRT specimen of the Mo steel. A few microcracks also appear beneath the fracture surface (arrow marks in Fig. 9d). According to the EBSD analysis of the crack path and orientation, the microcrack shows transgranular characteristics as it initiates and propagates through prior austenite grain interiors instead of PAGBs (red arrows in Fig. 9f). The primary difference between the Ref and Mo steels is the sufficient post-elongation in the Mo steel. The post-elongation depends on pre-existing damages such as microvoids and void clusters, as shown in Fig. 7f. This process is directly related to the interaction of solute Mo with the mobility of dislocations and vacancies. According to the previously described H-assisted failure mechanism in Ref steel, the lowered diffusivity of H in Mo steel reduces the frequency of interactions between H atoms and dislocations, thereby retarding the active Hand strain-localization towards the PAGBs. These localizations are further suppressed by a solute-drag effect of Mo in a solid solution, which inhibits the movement of dislocations.
