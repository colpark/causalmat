- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The Cu-MOF (HKUST-1, $\left[{\bf C u}_{3}({\bf B T C})_{2}({\bf H}_{2}{\bf O})_{3}\right]$ ; $\begin{array}{l}{{\mathrm{BTC~}=}}\end{array}$ 1,3,5-benzenetricarboxylic acid) was deposited on the surface of an NLS microchannel by a hydrothermal method to form HKUST-1/NLS (Figure 1a and Figure S1),30 and the structure of the Cu-MOF was determined by X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Figure S2a). A scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image demonstrates that the Cu-MOF with a cubic octahedral morphology ( ${\bf\tilde{\rho}}{\sim}2\mu\mathrm{~m~}$ in size) was successfully synthesized, and a 3D unit cell crystal of Cu-MOF also shows a unique cubic structure (Figure S3). The NLS fibers structured with microchannels have a rough surface and a diameter in the range of $20{-}50~\mu\mathrm{m}$ (Figure $\mathrm{S}4\mathrm{a}$ ). Subsequently, the device of ${\mathrm{Cu@C~NPs}},$ /carbonized loofah sponge was prepared through a pyrolysis and carbonization process by using Cu-MOF/NLS as a precursor (Figure 1a). The XRD patterns of $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}$ are shown in Figure S2b. A broad peak at $23^{\circ}$ is typical of a graphitic carbon, which should be generated by the pyrolysis of Cu-MOF/NLS,31 and other diffraction peaks can be properly indexed to metallic ${\mathrm{Cu}}$ .32 The SEM images were obtained to determine the morphology of CLS and $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}$ (Figure 1b and Figure S4b), in which NLS transformed into a microchannel CLS architecture with highly interconnected networks. The porous surface of $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}$ is wrinkled due to the formation of graphene layers in NLS during the pyrolysis process. Moreover, the corresponding element mappings of $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}$ confirm the uniform distribution of Cu (Figure S5).  To better understand the microstructure of $\mathrm{{Cu(\mathrm{{\it{a}})\mathrm{{C/\mathrm{{CLS}},}}}}}$ TEM measurements were performed. It can be seen that nanosized Cu@C NPs with an average diameter of $15~\mathrm{nm}$ are homogeneously distributed on the CLS at a high density (Figure S6a,b). Notably, high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images (Figure 1c,d) show that ${\mathrm{Cu}}$ NPs are wrapped in ultrathin graphene layers, and the wellresolved crystal lattice with $d\cdot$ -spacing values of $0.208\mathrm{nm}$ can be attributed to the (111) plane of $\mathrm{Cu},^{32}$ which is well in agreement with XRD patterns.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The Raman spectrum in Figure 2a further confirms the structural composition of $\mathrm{Cu}@\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{CLS},$ , in which a D band $(1339\mathrm{cm}^{-1})$ ) represents the defects and a G band $\phantom{0}{1594}\mathrm{cm}^{-1},$ ) indicates a highly ordered graphite. The intensity ratio of $I_{\mathrm{D}}/$ $I_{\mathrm{G}}$ (0.97) indicates that there is a high degree of graphitization.34 In contrast to CLS (Figure S8), a broad two-dimensional (2D) band at ${\sim}2707~\mathrm{~cm^{-1}}$ implies the formation of a few-layer $\left(3-4\right)$ graphene in $\mathrm{{Cu(\mathrm{{\it{a}C/\mathrm{{CLS}},}}}}$ which can be ascribed to encapsulation layers over Cu NPs.35 To investigate the light absorption properties of NLS, CLS, and $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS},$ , a UV−vis−NIR (NIR $=$ near-infrared) spectrometer measurement was performed. As shown in Figure 2b, the light absorption of CLS $(96\%)$ in a carbonized state is significantly increased by $33\%$ compared with the low light absorption of NLS $(72\%)$ from the wavelength of 300−2200 nm. In the presence of $\mathrm{Cu}(\partial\mathrm{C},$ the light absorption is elevated to almost $99\%$ over that of $\mathrm{{Cu@C/CLS}}$ due to the LSPR effect of $\mathrm{Cu}~\mathrm{NPs},^{36}$ which further lays foundations for an efficient photothermal conversion.  The chemical states of elements, particularly for ${\mathrm{Cu}}$ in $\mathrm{Cu}/\AA$ CLS and $\mathrm{{Cu}}@\mathrm{{C}}/\mathrm{{CLS}}_{,}$ , were studied with X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). $\mathrm{{Cu@C/CLS}}$ possesses a weak N 1s peak at $400.1~\mathrm{eV}$ after a carbonization treatment, corresponding to $\mathrm{C-N/N{-}H}$ (Figure 2c).4 Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) also confirmed the existence of nitrogen functional groups after carbonization (Figure S9). These functional groups that come from carbohydrates and proteins are beneficial to hydrophilicity,4,37 and the water contact angle of Cu@C/CLS is determined to be 58° (Figure S10).38 Notably, the oxidation of surface ${\mathrm{Cu}}$ NPs occurred even in fresh $\mathrm{{Cu}}/\mathrm{{CLS}}$ (Figure S11). In Figure 2d, satellite peaks at 942.2 and $962.4~\mathrm{eV}$ in a $\mathrm{Cu}2\mathfrak{p}$ spectrum of $\mathrm{{Cu}}/\mathrm{{CLS}}$ emerged, which is assigned to $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ caused by an easy oxidation of Cu NPs.39 By contrast, because of the absence of satellite peaks in the Cu $2\mathrm{p}$ spectrum for $\mathrm{Cu}@\mathrm{C}/\mathrm{CLS},$ the signal could be attributed to ${\bar{\mathbf{C}}}\mathbf{u}^{0}$ or ${\mathrm{Cu}}_{2}\mathrm{O}$ . As ${\mathrm{Cu}}^{0}$ and ${\mathrm{Cu}}_{2}\mathrm{O}$ have almost the same binding energy of $\mathrm{Cu}2\mathfrak{p},$ an LMM X-ray excited Auger electron spectroscopy study was performed. As shown in Figure S12, the peak at $918.3\mathrm{eV}$ is attributed to ${\mathrm{Cu}}^{0}{\mathrm{:}}$ , indicating that metallic Cu NPs were successfully maintained,40 which agrees well with the results of HRTEM. These results indicate that graphene layers effectively protect Cu from oxidation in ambient conditions, which is beneficial to realize the plasmonic heating of ${\mathrm{Cu}}^{0}$ during solar vapor generation.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Solar evaporation rates were measured carefully to quantitatively evaluate the performance of $\mathrm{{Cu@C/CLS}}$ by recording the mass loss under the simulated solar irradiation (1 kW $\mathrm{m}^{-2}$ , 1 Sun) (Figure 3a).41 The evaporation rates were calculated from the slope of the mass change curve with time. Though less solar energy was absorbed, as indicated by its natural color, the NLS increases the mass loss rate of water compared with that of pure water (0.55 vs $0.31\mathrm{kg}\mathrm{m}^{-2}\mathrm{h}^{-1})$ . After a carbonization treatment, the evaporation rate over CLS significantly increases to $1.20\mathrm{kg}\mathrm{m}^{-2}\mathrm{h}^{-\hat{1}}$ , which is much higher than those of the naturally carbonized biomaterial due to the unique 3D porous and open elongated microchannel structure.26 A evaporation rate of $1.\stackrel{\smile}{3}9\mathrm{kgm}^{-2}\mathrm{h}^{-1}$ was achieved in the first hour over $\mathrm{{Cu}}/\mathrm{{CLS}}$ without a graphene layer protection. However, this rate decreased to $1.2\breve{3}\mathrm{kgm}^{-2}$ $\mathrm{h^{-1}}$ during the subsequent few hours, which should be caused by a failure of the LSPR effect due to the oxidation of Cu.42 Remarkably, the evaporation rate of $\mathrm{{Cu@C/CLS}}$ maintained at $1.54\mathrm{kg}\mathrm{im}^{-2}\mathrm{h}^{-1}$ after the introduction of plasmonic $\mathrm{CuNPs}$ encapsulated in graphene layers into CLS, which were 1.25 and 4.97 times as high as its counterpart $\mathrm{(Cu/CLS}$ and pure water). These results demonstrate the crucial role of stabilized Cu NPs in an efficient water evaporation. The water evaporation rate of $\operatorname{Cu}(\omega\mathrm{C}$ is $\sim0.45~\mathrm{kg}~\mathrm{m}^{-2}~\mathrm{h}^{-1}$ (Figure S13). This should be caused by the evaporation system of $\mathrm{Cu}@\mathrm{C}$ without a substrate partly transforming from an interface into volume, which results in an inevitable heat loss.2  The photothermal conversion efficiency is defined as $\eta\:=\:$ $m H_{\mathrm{lv}}/I_{\mathrm{)}}^{\mathbf{\frac{3}{6}},43,44}$ where $m$ denotes evaporation rate and $H_{\mathrm{lv}}$ and $I$ represent the enthalpy of the liquid−vapor phase change and the incidence light power $(1\mathrm{~kW~m~}^{-\frac{1}{2}})$ ,6 respectively. The calculated conversion efficiencies and evaporation rates of samples under $1\mathrm{Sun}$ illumination are shown in Figure $^{3\mathrm{b}}$ and Calculation S1. After the correction of enthalpy is taken into account and the evaporation rate of samples under dark conditions is deducted, the efficiencies of solar evaporation systems are calculated to be $71.9\%$ and $73.8\%$ for CLS and $\mathrm{Cu}/\AA$ CLS, respectively. It is worthy to point out that an efficiency of $90.2\%$ is achieved over ${\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}}$ under the same conditions, which is among the top-notch plasmonic metal systems reported (Figure 3c, Calculation S1 and Table S1). Meanwhile, the corresponding calculated heat loss is ${\sim}12.3\%$ (Calculation S2), which basically agrees with the value of $9.8\%$ obtained from an experimental photothermal conversion efficiency.41 In addition, $\mathrm{{Cu@C/CLS}}$ shows a good stable and durable evaporation performance over $^{7\textup{d}}$ (two cycles per day) of continuous operation under 1 Sun illumination (Figure 3d). There is no obvious change observed in XRD and XPS characterizations of the $\mathrm{Cu}@\mathrm{C}$ after the test (Figures S7 and S14). Moreover, the stability of the $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}$ is further confirmed by the XRD, SEM, and HRTEM characterizations of the sample after five cycles (Figures S15−S17).

- caption: Figure 4. (a) Average surface temperatures of different samples under 1 Sun. (b) Infrared photos of sample surfaces. $t=30~\mathrm{min}$ after illumination. (c) Schematic diagram of a thermal behavior for the $\mathrm{{Cu}}@\mathrm{{C}}/\mathrm{{CLS}}$ structure.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To study the localized heating effect caused by a photothermal property of Cu NPs, the surface temperature change of different devices under 1 Sun illumination was carefully measured with an infrared camera (Figure 4a,b). After $900~s$ of light illumination, the surface temperatures of CLS and $\mathrm{Cu}/\mathrm{CLS}$ were similar, maintained at ${\sim}40~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ in a steady state. In contrast, the temperatures on the surface of $\mathbf{Cu}@\mathbf{C}/$ CLS remarkably increased from 20 to $72^{\circ}C.$ . This result clearly demonstrated the effective localized heating effect of stabilized Cu NPs through a converting LSPR absorption, which can significantly elevate the surface temperature for an efficient steam generation. On the contrary, $\mathrm{{Cu}}/\mathrm{{CLS}}$ is prone to oxidation without a graphene coating, which restricted its plasmonic heating. In addition, the thermal conductivity of NLS, CLS, and $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}$ were also measured. The thermal conductivity of $\mathrm{{Cu@C/CLS}}$ $\zeta k=0.28~\mathrm{W~m^{-1}~K^{-1}}\rangle$ and CLS $\left(k=0.37~\mathrm{W~m^{-1}~K^{-1}}\right)$ is lower than that of NLS ( $\overset{\cdot}{k}=2.94\mathrm{~W~}$ $\mathrm{m}^{-1}\mathrm{K}^{-1},$ ), which might be caused by the formation of graphitized carbon materials with a low thermal conductivity after pyrolysis.4,45 Therefore, the porous structure of $\mathbf{Cu}@\mathbf{C}/$ CLS can effectively restrain the heat loss.4,37 Accordingly, once the illumination is on, a stable $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}$ device with a high optical absorption can achieve an obvious increase of surface temperature via the localized heating effect of LSPR. Then, because of the hydrophilicity and microchannels of CLS, sufficient water is pumped to the interface to achieve efficient water transmission (Figure $4{\mathfrak{c}}$ ).46 The excellent evaporation rate of $\mathrm{{Cu@C/CLS}}$ is attributed to the synergetic actions between the $\operatorname{Cu}(\varpi\mathrm{C}$ and the 3D porous microchannel structures.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): A tentative study in a model sewage purification was performed over $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}$ . As shown in Figure 5a, methylene blue has two distinct characteristic absorption peaks, and the absorbance of the purified water is almost zero after evaporation,47 which means that the purified water contains negligible organic dyes. Similarly a situation also occurs in a methyl orange solution (Figure 5b). The images and water resistance test results of a water-based carbon ink and collected muddy water samples before and after purification are shown in Figure 5c,d. After being purified by a $\mathrm{{Cu@C/CLS}}$ device, clear and transparent water is obtained. Water resistance tests further show that the ohmic values of black ink and muddy water samples were 0.959 and $0.909\mathrm{M}\Omega$ , respectively, which were much higher than that of domestic water samples $\mathrm{(0.679MS2}$ of an urban water-supply system).6 The results of six tests showed a good reproducibility in Figure S18. The SEM and HRTEM characterizations of the device after a sewage purification also show that device has a good stability (Figure S19). In addition, the evaporation rate just slightly decreased from $1.54\mathrm{kg}\mathrm{m}^{-2}\mathrm{h}^{-1}$ of pure water to 1.44 $\mathrm{kg}^{-2}\mathrm{h}^{-1}$ of $3.5\%\mathrm{NaCl}$ solution and to $\hat{1.39~\mathrm{kg~m}}^{-2}\mathrm{~h}^{-1}$ of $10\%$ NaCl solution under 1 Sun illumination, respectively, showing a good prospect of desalination (Figure S20). These results show that a $\mathrm{Cu@C/CLS}$ device could be used for a water purification and a regeneration of various sewage media in the future and has a broad application prospect.
