- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The organic ligand Hcbpp containing one carboxyl group and two nitrogen-atom heterocyclic rings was chosen and reacted with low-cost zinc metal salt to construct 2D-functionalized MOFs through a simple solvothermal method. Formic acids produced by the hydrolysis of the solvent N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) under acidic condition were cleverly used to crosslink zinc ions to generate the 2D framework and simultaneously create more active sites. Acting as a terminal agent, as-prepared Zn-cbpp incorporated in the perovskite precursor solution was expected to react with $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ to form Lewis acid–base adducts. During the spin-coating and calcination of the precursor films, perovskite continuously grew along  PbI2-Zn-cbpp adducts, and resulted in Zn-cbpp-terminated perovskite grains. The 2D $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp also facilitated the connection of adjacent perovskite grains, eliminating the pinholes or cracks that may exist between the perovskite grains and avoiding the accumulation of excessive unreacted $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ at the grain boundaries (Figure  1a). Figure  1b,c shows the crystalline structure of as-prepared Zn-cbpp, which was identified by single crystal X-ray diffraction (Details see Tables S1 and S2, Supporting Information, CCDC: 2040061). From the $a$ -axis perspective, large numbers of oxygen atoms and nitrogen atoms containing lone pairs of electrons are exposed, which provides enough active sites for the interactions with perovskite grains. The ordered structure of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp provides convenience for precise regulation. The layers are stacked by hydrogen bonds between pyrazines in the ligands to produce an ordered supramolecular structure (Figure S1, Supporting Information). From the Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectra, it can be seen that no stretching vibration peak of the carboxyl group $(1708~\mathrm{cm^{-1}})$ in ligands is found in $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp, and instead there are two stretching vibration peaks at 1556 and $1402~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ attributed to the carboxylate group, suggesting the successful formation of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp (Figure S2a, Supporting Information). The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) pattern further confirmed the phase-pure of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp based on the simulated diffraction pattern (Figure S2b, Supporting Information). The weak light absorption in the visible light region and high decomposition temperature of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp both suggest its potential as an additive for perovskite films (Figure S2c,d, Supporting Information). In order to further investigate the stability of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp under the conditions of device application, the XRD measurements were performed on samples before and after soaking in different solvents after $24\mathrm{~h~}$ under air conditions. As shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information, the diffraction peaks matched well with the pristine sample and most of main peaks are maintained, indicating the excellent stability of as-prepared $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp. Electrostatic potential (ESP) was used to analyze the electronic structure of Zn-cbpp asymmetrical cell and it can be found that the highest electron density is mainly distributed on the carboxylate groups, followed by the undercoordinated nitrogen atoms on the pyrazine at both ends (Figure 1d). These electron-rich atoms or groups can form Lewis acid–base adducts through strong coordination bonds with undercoordinated lead ions.[36,37] Furthermore, the main diffraction peaks of (200) and (300) planes of Zn-cbpp powder suggest that the exposed crystal planes in the synthesized sample are mainly along $a$ -axis (Figure  1b), which is beneficial to the interactions with perovskite precursors due to the small steric hindrance (Figure S4, Supporting Information). Hence, we proposed that exposed active sites in the $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp framework may form strong interactions with $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ and thereby passivate the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To further clarify the effects of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp on perovskite films, other characterizations were performed. The triple mixed cation perovskite containing cesium (Cs), formamidinium (FA), and MA was chosen as the light absorber layer due to its preferable stability and the potential high efficiency. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and scanning electron micro­ scopy (SEM) measurements were applied to investigate the distribution of Zn-cbpp in PSCs and the influence on the surface morphology of perovskite films. From the high-resolution TEM image of the $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ -Zn-cbpp powder, it can be found that $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ crystal grains were closely surrounded and cross-linked by Zncbpp (Figure  3a and Figure S10, Supporting Information). In addition, during the calcination process of the films, Zn-cbpp will not volatilize like DMSO and DMF because of its polymer nature and stronger interactions with $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ . Hence, we inferred that the 2D $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp would be mainly distributed on the surface of $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ -terminated perovskite grains and as a new terminated material to stabilize perovskite grains. Top-view SEM images of perovskite films showed that more insulating $\mathrm{PbI}_{2}$ phase existing at the grain boundaries (bright image contrast area) was significantly reduced after $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification and the surface of the $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modified perovskite film turned to regular without sacrificing the grain size (Figure $^{3\mathrm{b},\mathrm{c}^{\prime}}$ ). The atomic force microscope (AFM) images further suggested that the surface of the modified film was flatter for the surface roughness of the perovskite film which was reduced from 9.15 to $7.98~\mathrm{nm}$ after modification (Figure  3d,e). To further analyze the influence of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp on perovskite grain boundaries, we carried out the conductive atomic force microscope (C-AFM) measurement (Figure  3f,g, and Figure S11, Supporting Information). It can be seen that Zn-cbpp passivation can effectively reduce the current “dead areas” at the grain boundaries, which is beneficial to the transport and separation of charge carriers. Furthermore, continuously increasing the amount of Zn-cbpp in perovskite precursor solutions made the perovskite grains more rounded and the spacing between the grains increased (Figure S12, Supporting Information). It can be proposed that the $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ -Zn-cbpp adducts would maintain its original state during the crystallization process, and the crystal growth of perovskite would mainly occur between $\mathrm{PbI}_{2}(\mathrm{DMSO})_{x}$ and amine salt ions until the crystallization was complete ( $\mathrm{PbBr}_{2}$ and CsI present in the solution would also participate in the reactions similarly). Eventually, the perovskite grains would be tightly capped by $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp. Excessive $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp would lead to more nucleation sites and the formation of large numbers of adducts, thereby restraining the growth of perovskite grains. To confirm the distribution of Zn-cbpp in  PSCs, the cross-sectional high angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image of the PSC with $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification was given in Figure 3h. The devices were based on the typical planar structure of FTO/ $\mathrm{TiO}_{2I}$ /PCBM/Perovskite/Spiro-OMeTAD/Au (PCBM refers to phenyl-C61-butyric acid methyl ester) and each functional layer can be clearly distinguished. Combining the TEM images and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping of the device, we clearly demonstrated that Zn-cbpp mainly distributed on the grain boundaries of the polycrystalline perovskite film and no pinholes or cracks existed between the perovskite grains (Figure  3i and Figure S13, Supporting Information). More importantly, different from previous 2D passivation layers based on $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ framework reported in the recent literature,[43] our experimental results show that the Cs, Pb, and halogen are absent at the grain boundaries. Stable $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp as a new terminated layer tightly wraps the surface of perovskite and closely connects adjacent grains.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure  4a shows the XRD patterns of the perovskite films deposited on $\mathrm{FTO/TiO}_{2}/\mathrm{PCBM}$ substrates and the (100) plane diffraction peak intensity of the perovskite film is enhanced after Zn-cbpp modification. Despite the use of excess $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ in the perovskite precursor solutions, no obvious diffraction peaks assigned to $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ were observed at $12.6^{\circ}$ most probably due to the influence of the substrates on the crystallization of perovskite films. Subsequently, XPS was utilized to confirm the interactions between $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp and perovskite films (Figure  4b). The XPS spectrum of Pb 4f for the pristine perovskite film displays two main peaks located at 138.42 and $143.32~\mathrm{eV},$ attributed to $\mathrm{Pb}4\mathrm{f}_{7/2}$ and $\mathrm{Pb}4\mathrm{f}_{5/2}$ , respectively. For the $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modified perovskite film, the peaks shift by $0.2\mathrm{eV}$ to higher binding energies, implying that stronger ionic bonds are formed between the oxygen atoms of Zncbpp and the undercoordinated lead ions.[44,45] In addition, the obvious peaks of $\operatorname{Zn}~2\mathfrak{p}$ in the modified perovskite film indicated that Zn-cbpp also existed on the surface of perovskite films (Figure 4c). After bonding this stable MOFs Zn-cbpp on the surface of perovskite grains, it can effectively passivate the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films. In order to quantify the impact of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp on the defect density of perovskite films, the current density–voltage $(J-V)$ characteristics were obtained from the prepared hole-only devices using the space-charge-limited current method (Figure  4d). The devices were based on the structure of FTO/PEDOT:PSS/Perovskite/ Spiro-OMeTAD/Au and the trap density was determined according to the equation $N_{\mathrm{t}}=2\varepsilon\varepsilon_{0}V_{\mathrm{TFL}}/e d^{2}$ , where $e$ is the elementary charge, $d$ is the thickness of the perovskite film, $\varepsilon$ is the relative dielectric constant, and $\varepsilon_{0}$ is the vacuum permittivity. The trap-filled limit voltages $\left(V_{\mathrm{TFL}}\right)$ of the devices with and without Zn-cbpp modification are 0.47 and $0.56~\mathrm{~V~},$ respectively. The calculated defect density of the perovskite film with Zn-cbpp modification decreases from $1.14\times10^{16}$ to $9.57\times10^{15}~\mathrm{cm}^{-3}$ , indicating that $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp can effectively passivate the grain boundaries of perovskite films. The steady state photoluminescence (PL) and time-resolved photoluminescence (TRPL) were used to further analyze the quality of perovskite films with Zn-cbpp modification. PL intensity of the perovskite film modified with $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp is 7.3 times higher than that of the reference film, and the PL peak position is blue-shifted from 780 to $774~\mathrm{nm}$ (Figure  4e). Based on the TRPL spectra shown in Figure $4\mathrm{f},$ we calculated the average lifetime of the perovskite films and found that the lifetime increased from 5.82 to 9.55  ns after $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification. These results consistently demonstrate that Zn-cbpp modification can improve the optoelectronic properties and reduce the defects of perovskite films.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The photovoltaic performance of PSCs modified with different concentration of Zn-cbpp was studied and photovoltaic parameters statistical distribution box diagrams were given in Figure  5. The PCE of PSCs increased with the added concentration of Zn-cbpp and obtained optimum at $2.0\mathrm{mg}\cdot\mathrm{mL}^{-1}$ . The change of open-circuit voltage $(V_{\mathrm{oc}})$ was mainly responsible for the improvement of PCE due to the excellent crystal quality and low defect density of perovskite films. Excessive accumulation of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp at the grain boundaries would cause the fill factor of PSCs decrease under high doping concentration, resulting in relatively poor performance. Figure  6a shows the $J-V$ curves of the best-performing PSCs with and without Zn-cbpp modification under reverse scan direction. The control device (without $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification) exhibited a $V_{\mathrm{oc}}$ of $1.108\mathrm{~V},$ a short-circuit current density $(J_{\mathrm{sc}})$ of $22.90~\mathrm{mA~cm}^{-2}$ , and a fill factor of $77.2\%$ , for a PCE of $19.59\%$ whereas the $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp-modified device showed a significantly improved PCE of $21.28\%$ , corresponding to a $V_{\mathrm{oc}}$ of $1.155\mathrm{~V},$ a $J_{\mathrm{sc}}$ of $23.32\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}$ , and a fill factor of $79.0\%$ . The integrated $J_{\mathrm{sc}}$ was obtained from the incident photon to current conversion efficiency (IPCE) spectra (Figure  6b) and the values for PSCs with and without $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification were 22.79 and $22.27\mathrm{mAcm}^{-2}$ , respectively, which matched well with the results from the $J-V$ curves $(<3\%$ discrepancy). Specifically, $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modified PSC exhibits higher IPCE value in the range of 650 to $750\mathrm{nm}$ , which may be due to the changes in the composition of the perovskite film at the grain boundaries. In order to further prove the feasibility of Zn-cbpp in improving the properties of perovskite films, the application of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp in another representative perovskite material, ${\mathrm{MAPbI}}_{3}$ , was also investigated. As shown in Figure S14, Supporting Information, the performance of the ${\mathrm{MAPbI}}_{3}$ -based PSCs with Zn-cbpp modification was also significantly improved, which indicated the universality of this material. In order to further explain why the $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification can improve the performance of PSCs, transient photovoltage (TPV) and transient photocurrent (TPC) decay were used to analyze the charge transfer and recombination behaviors in the devices. As shown in Figure 6c, the photovoltage decay time for the PSC with $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification was $56.18~\upmu\mathrm{s}$ while the control device was $35.88~\upmu\mathrm{s}$ . The prolonged decay time indicates that the charge recombination can be efficiently suppressed, which coincides with the higher $V_{\mathrm{oc}}$ and fill factor of $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp-modified PSCs. The photocurrent decay time was reduced from 6.39 to $4.73~\upmu\mathrm{s}$ for the PSCs without and with $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification (Figure 6d). The decrease in photocurrent decay time may be due to the passivation of defects and elimination of voids at grain boundaries, which makes the extraction of charge carriers more favorable. The faster charge carrier extraction and suppressed charge recombination in $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbppmodified PSCs also help to alleviate the hysteresis behavior, and the hysteresis factor is reduced from $10.6\%$ to $7.2\%$ for $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp-modified PSCs (Figure S15, Supporting Information). This simple grain termination strategy does not require secondary operations and can also greatly improve the stability of the perovskite films against moisture and heat, which will be deeply discussed below.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The moisture stability of perovskite films was first investigated and Figure 7a displayed the photos of the perovskite films with and without $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification before and after storage at room temperature with a humidity of ${\approx}85\%$ for 7 days. It can be clearly seen that the $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp-modified film still retained the original black–brown appearance after 7 days of high humidity aging whereas most of the untreated perovskite film turned yellow, indicating that the pristine perovskite structure has changed significantly. One of the reasons for the excellent moisture stability of the perovskite films with Zn-cbpp modification is the increase in water contact angles of the perovskite films after modification (Figure S16, Supporting Information), but more importantly, Zn-cbpp passivates the surface and grain boundaries of the perovskite films. The decrease in defect state density alleviates the moisture-induced decomposition of perovskite films catalyzed by defects and the presence of $\mathrm{Zn}$ - cbpp with organic aromatic structure at the grain boundaries prevents the invasion of water molecules.[19,46] Then we evaluated the stability of unencapsulated PSCs with and without Zn-cbpp modification stored in the surrounding environment $25~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , $\mathrm{RH}\approx40\%$ ). As shown in Figure 7b, Zn-cbpp-modified devices still maintained $81\%$ of the initial efficiency after more than $1000\mathrm{~h~}$ of aging, which greatly surpassed the control device. XRD patterns and UV–vis absorption spectra of perovskite films after $1000\mathrm{~h~}$ of aging under the same conditions further confirm that the modification of Zn-cbpp can inhibit the formation of hydrated perovskites and the rapid decomposition of perovskite films (Figure S17, Supporting Information). $[47]$ The thermal stability of the PSCs with $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp modification revealed obvious improvement. After heating at $85~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ under nitrogen atmosphere for $750\mathrm{~h~}$ , the efficiency loss of the $\mathrm{Zn}$ -cbpp-modified devices and control devices were $12\%$ and $42\%$ , respectively (Figure 7e). During the first $500\mathrm{~h~}$ of heating, the (100) plane diffraction peaks intensity of the perovskite films hardly changed regardless of the Zn-cbpp modification, indicating the good thermal stability of the triple mixed cation perovskite (Figure S18a,b, Supporting Information). However, as the heating time extended, XRD results indicated the intensification of $\mathrm{PbI}_{2}$ -associated diffraction peaks. The deterioration of performance may be rationalized by continuous decomposition of the surface and grain boundaries of perovskite films into $\mathrm{PbI}_{2}$ upon heating. During this process, the more unstable MA in the system decreased and caused local lattice distortion of the CsFAMA perovskite, which partially converted into $\delta\mathrm{FAPbI}_{3}$ . This process resulted in significant changes in the morphology of the perovskite films and continuously decreased the performance (Figure  7c,d). As expected, the modification of Zn-cbpp alleviated the thermal decomposition of perovskite films and restrained the occurrence of phase transition (Figure S18c,d, Supporting Information).
