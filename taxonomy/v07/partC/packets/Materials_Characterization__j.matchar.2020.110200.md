- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Although several researches have been conducted on the orientation dependency of tensile properties of IN718, the governing mechanisms based on which this mechanical anisotropy is appeared, has not been discussed in details. In addition, the above-mentioned investigations were mostly conducted by tensile testing of many relatively large-sized specimens. Due to the high cost of superalloy powders and the processing time that AM machine spends to fabricate an AM component, the idea of miniature testing of the AM parts seems valuable and costeffective. Recently, small punch tensile (SPT) and small punch creep (SPC) tests have been developed to characterize mechanical properties by using a small amount of material which is a cost-benefit method compared to tensile or creep conventional tests [15]. As indicated schematically in Fig. 1(a), in the SPT method, a hemispherical punch makes a bulging deformation in a disc-shaped specimen with a thickness of $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ and load versus displacement is plotted during the test. [16]. A typical SPT load-displacement curve can be divided into 6 zones as shown in Fig. 1(b). Zone I, which appertains to elastic properties of the material, is called elastic bending; II corresponds to plastic bending and zone III belongs to membrane stretching. In zone IV necking and crack initiation occurs while in zone V fracture softening happens due to crack growth and finally zone VI shows final fracture. There may be a slight difference in the borderline of zones IV and V as reported in previous works [15–18].

### F7
- path: /home/aid1/Documents/causalmat/matmech/Materials_Characterization/j.matchar.2020.110200/images/ec685cfb63e0a646645987648b219dc978691100cb617d6a8db383b1d76c0153.jpg
- caption: Fig. 7. Level of mechanical anisotropy in SLMed IN718 at room and $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ temperatures.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): A series of SPT tests were carried out at both room and elevated temperatures to assess the mechanical anisotropy of the SLM-fabricated IN718 samples. Fig. 7 presents the level of anisotropy in the SLMed samples punched at different temperatures. Their data have been compared with the tensile test data in Table 3. The results indicate that there is a significant difference between the mechanical properties of samples extracted from the side and top views of SLM-fabricated IN718 cubes. As can be seen, the side view sample exhibited almost $5 4 \%$ greater “maximum force” (1691 N) than the one for the top view sample (1096 N). In addition, by increasing temperature from room temperature (RT) to $6 5 0 \ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C } ,$ the maximum force was dropped significantly for samples extracted from the side view while such an increase in test temperature seemed to have no influence on the top view sample. The curve shape is also altered based on the testing direction, which is the point of Section 3.3.2.  The room and high temperatures SPT load-displacement curves of samples extracted from the side and top views are shown in Fig. 8. As can be seen, the curves exhibit various zones implying different flow behaviors. In order to identify the boundary of the zones, first and second derivatives of the fitted curves were also plotted. It can be seen that the side view curve exhibits larger elastic displacement as well as greater load at the end of zone I as compared to the top view curve at room temperature (Fig. 8(a and b)), indicating more stored elastic energy in the side view sample. In zone II at room temperature, the side view curve seems to have a much wider plastic area than the one for top. Despite the zones I and II, zone III is wider for the top view curve but has lower load at the end of the zone (Fig. 8(a and b)). Comparing zones IV and V for both views shows that for the side view sample, load decreased gradually at the end of zone IV and continued thorough zone V until the final fracture occurred. However, in the top view, the load dropped with a very steep slope during zone V. In order to verify and clarify the distinction between zones IV and V, fractography analysis of the top and side samples will be discussed in the next sections.  The SPT curves at high temperature are displayed in Fig. 8(c and d). It can be observed that zone I in the side sample is wider which can be attributed to higher elastic properties and higher yield load and elastic energy, as also observed for the room temperature curves in Fig. 8(a). Interestingly, at $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , the top view sample shows wider plastic strain in zone II as compared to the one for side view sample, opposite to the room temperature curves. In addition, the width of zone III is bigger in the side view sample at $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ , again opposite to what observed in Fig. 8(a) for the room temperature curves. This is in good agreement with the shapes of SPT load-displacement curves that were investigated by Hurst et al. [19]. Curve shape differences between room and elevated temperatures are also observed by Davies et al. [8]. Moreover, in both samples, crack grows immediately after initiation as observed from zones IV and V. As a whole, it was observed that the side view sample has better properties at room temperature by having $5 4 \%$ higher loads compared to the one for the top view sample, while the top view shows better properties at high temperature compared to the side one.  At high-temperature conditions, it is well known that lower external force needs to be applied for dislocation movement. Besides, since the side view sample has higher initial dislocation density (during SLM process) and consequently higher driving force for dislocation annihilation, dynamic recovery might have been highly encouraged to occur in the side view sample at high temperatures and causes a lower maximum load. However, no significant decrease was observed in the maximum force of the top view sample due to much less initial residual stresses. Moreover, continuous chains of Laves particles precipitated on the cell's body of the side view sample (Fig. 4(f)), can affect the hightemperature performance of the SLM-fabricated IN718 [23]. Although these brittle particles limited the high-temperature plastic deformation, it seems that the detrimental effect of long columnar grain boundaries which are weaker than inside of the grains at high temperatures, intensify the demolition of the side view sample at $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ . This was evidenced by the lower plastic deformation of the side sample at high temperatures during zone II compared to its room temperature curve (Fig. 8(a and c)). Also, intergranular fracture occurring along the columnar grain boundaries (Fig. 9(f)) and the grain boundary crack which was observed in Fig. 9(e) show that long grain boundaries are seemingly weaker at $6 5 0 ~ ^ { \circ } \mathrm { C }$ and play a detrimental role in the deformation of the side view sample at high temperatures. In contrast, discrete Laves particles (Fig. $4 ( \mathfrak { g } ) )$ , lower residual stresses, and absence of long grain boundaries in the top view sample are probably the main reasons for keeping its mechanical properties almost unchanged during the hightemperature SPT tests of this sample compared to room one.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Another factor which has to be considered in the mechanical properties of SLM-fabricated parts is a large amount of residual stresses that are mainly appeared due to significant thermal gradient induced to the melt pools during the SLM process [28,29]. It was reported that mechanical anisotropy of the SLM-fabricated IN718 is depended on both crystallographic orientation and thermal history [14]. Fig. 12 shows the schematic representation of MPBs in the SPT samples extracted from the side and top cross-section of SLM-fabricated cubes. As indicated in Fig. 12a, an area with $8 . 7 \ \mathrm { m m }$ diameter of SPT specimen has to be considered for counting the layers which contribute in the SPT test of the side sample, while the $5 0 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ thickness of the specimen has to be regarded for the layers participated in the SPT test of the top view sample. This affects the dislocation slip and consequently mechanical anisotropy [30]. It can be seen in Fig. 12(a) that the side view sample has much more track-track and layer-layer MPBs which means much more slip surfaces compared to top one and as explained earlier, it can cause higher work hardening of the side sample too.
