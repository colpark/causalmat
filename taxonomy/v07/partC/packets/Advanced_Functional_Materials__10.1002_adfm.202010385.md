The $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ layer and $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF layer were obtained consequently. Then we spin-coated different concentrations of RbF aqueous solution on the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ layers to fabricate the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}/$ RbF layers. To confirm the existence of RbF on the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ surface, X-ray photoelectron spectra (XPS) were operated on the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ , $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF, and $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}/\mathrm{RbF}$ films (Figure 1a). The characteristic peaks of C 1s were calibrated to $284.8~\mathrm{eV}$ for all XPS measurements. Compared with the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ film, the additional peaks of RbF-treated films at 684.2 and $109.9~\mathrm{eV}$ are assigned to F 1s and Rb 3d (Figures S1 and S2, Supporting Information), respectively, which demonstrates that RbF has been added into/ onto the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ films. In addition, Figure  1b shows that $\mathrm{Sn}3\mathrm{d}$ peak shifts by about $0.3~\mathrm{eV}$ towards a lower binding energy for the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF, indicating a change in electron cloud density around the $\mathrm{Sn}$ atoms because of the strong bonding between F and Sn when RbF is introduced into the bulk $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ .[39,40] However, the $\mathrm{Sn}3\mathrm{d}$ peak of the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}/\mathrm{RbF}$ shows almost no shift compared with the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ film, which implies the weak interaction between $\mathrm{F}$ and Sn atoms. This phenomenon indicates that the $\mathrm{F}{-}\ensuremath{\mathrm{Sn}}$ bond may form before or during the annealing process, after that $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ has crystalized and could hardly interact with F ions.  To explore the surface morphology and roughness of the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ films without/with RbF treatment, atomic force micro­ scopy (AFM) was operated. As shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information, all the films exhibit a smooth surface with a rootmean-square (RMS) roughness of 0.96, 1.98, and $1.61~\mathrm{nm}$ for the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ , $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF, and $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}/\mathrm{RbF}$ films, respectively; indicating that the presence of RbF has slightly changed the topography of the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ . The optical transmission spectra of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ without/with RbF treatment are shown in Figure 1c. The optical transmission of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF is a little higher than the others in the short wavelength, which is favorable for fabricating solar cells. In addition, all these films show high transmittance in the visible region, indicating excellent optical property. Ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) was carried out to explore the energy band structure of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ films with or without RbF modification. The corresponding calculation methods and results are shown in Figures S4–S7 and Table S1, Supporting Information, respectively. Figure  S8, Supporting Information, shows the energy band alignment of different ETLs, obviously the appearance of RbF has little effect on the energy level of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ .

The conductivity of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF is estimated to be $4.26\times10^{-3}\mathrm{mScm^{-1}}$ , higher than that of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}/$ RbF $(3.17\times10^{-3}\mathrm{mScm^{-1}})$ and $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ $(3.01\times10^{-3}\mathrm{mScm^{-1}})$ ). The enhanced electron mobility and conductivity demonstrate the more effective charge transport of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF, which effectively inhibits charge accumulation at the ETL/perovskite interface and improves the $J_{\mathrm{sc}}$ for PSCs.[14,19] The almost unchanged electron mobility of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}/\mathrm{RbF}$ compared with $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ demonstrates that the surface modification by RbF will not change the electrical properties of ETLs. The increased electron mobility of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF could be attributed to the strong bonding between F and Sn observed in XPS. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed to illustrate the properties of the atomic structures and the interaction between $\mathrm{F}$ and $\mathrm{Sn}$ . Generally, $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ crystallizes to form a rutile structure, and the (110) plane is the energetically the most favorable surface, which is composed of flat $\scriptstyle\mathrm{Sn}-\mathrm{O}$ planes connected by O atoms.[42] As shown in Figures 2a and 2b, we placed a $1\times1$ unit cell RbF on the top of a $1\times1$ unit cell $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ (110) slab with a $3\times3$ unit to construct the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF structure. The adsorption energy of $\mathrm{F}$ on the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ (110) surface is $-2.35\mathrm{eV}$ $(-106.2\mathrm{meV}\mathring{\mathrm{A}}^{-2})$ , demonstrating the intense interaction between $\mathrm{F}$ and $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ (110).[8] We speculated that the strong interaction between F and Sn likely originates from the newly formed $\mathrm{F}{-}\ensuremath{\mathrm{Sn}}$ bonds, which may be one of the main reasons for the changes in the electrical properties of $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ . In addition, a negligible interaction between Rb and $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ (110) is found due to a relatively low adsorption energy $-0.48~\mathrm{eV}$ $(-21.7\mathrm{meV}\mathring{\mathrm{A}}^{-2})$ of Rb on the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ (110) surface.  Afterwards, we prepared the perovskite films through a twostep method to study the influence of the ETLs on the perovskite films. What needs to be emphasized is that the mixed solvents $\mathrm{(DMSO~+~DMF}$ ) do not dissolve the RbF. To investigate the effect of the RbF on the interface between the ETL and the perovskite, we performed DFT calculations to evaluate the change in binding energy at the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ (110)/ $\prime{\mathrm{MAPbI}}_{3}$ (110) interface. The ${\mathrm{MAPbI}}_{3}$ (110) lattice plane is the energetically favorable orientation in film crystallization. Figures  2c and  2d display the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ (1 $[10)/\mathrm{MAPbI}_{3}$ (110) heterostructure model without/with the RbF treatment. The calculation's results reveal that the RbF modification leads to a stronger bonding between the ETL and perovskite with a binding energy of $-5.54$ and $-4.38~\mathrm{eV}$ per $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ (110) unit respectively for the interface with and without the RbF interlayer. Note that the DFT calculations for the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ (110)/ $\mathrm{{\sc~FAPbI}}_{3}$ (110) interface gave similar results of the binding energy. $(-5.59\$ and $-4.61\mathrm{eV}$ for the interface with and without the RbF, respectively) It demonstrates that the introduction of RbF energetically helps $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ contact the perovskite and hence significantly improves the affiliation of the interfaces. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images are shown in Figure 3a–c, and RbF modification seems to have no effect on the perovskite films' surface morphologies. All the perovskite films are uniform and full-coverage with similar grain sizes. And we observed that the perovskite film based on the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}\mathrm{-RbF}$ is smoother than the others. In order to verify this phenomenon, AFM measurements were operated to investigate the surface roughness of perovskite films. Figure  S10, Supporting Information, shows that the perovskite film based on the $\mathrm{SnO}_{2}$ -RbF owns the lowest RMS of $27.1~\mathrm{nm}$ , which is consistent with the SEM observation. X-ray diffraction (XRD) was conducted to explore the effect of different ETLs on the perovskite crystallization. As shown in Figure  3d, the characteristic diffraction peaks of all the perovskite films are almost the same, along with a similar peak intensity. Note that the diffraction peak at $12.7^{\circ}$ with a strong peak intensity should be assigned to $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ , and these moderate residues of $\mathrm{Pb}\mathrm{I}_{2}$ could passivate the surface or grain boundary defects of perovskite films.[25,43]
