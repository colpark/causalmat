- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Fig. 3a shows the engineering stress-strain curve for another [001]-oriented pillar that was further deformed up to a nominal engineering strain of $10\%$ (a larger strain than targeted occurred because of the sudden strain burst). Consistent with the results from Fig. 2, a plateau indicating stress-induced transformation commenced at $427\pm28\mathrm{MPa}$ . Upon further loading, the material became stiffer as the phase transformation was exhausted and the material was elastically loaded. At $1177\pm321$ MPa, a large strain burst $(6.3\%$ strain, up to a maximum strain level of $12\%$ suddenly occurred. This stress level is considerably lower than the maximum stress of 1843 MPa seen in Fig. 2 and can likely be attributed to the highly stochastic nature of plasticity at small scales as well as microstructural changes that occurred during the first two superelastic cycles in the pillar shown in Fig. 2. Upon unloading, there is clear superelastic recovery, and a residual strain of $7.3\%$ is observed. Note that the flat punch lost contact with the pillar, and the initial loading on the second cycle (brown arrow in Fig. 3a) shows the true residual strain after the first cycle. This residual strain level is larger than the $6.3\%$ strain burst, suggesting that some stress-induced martensite did not revert to austenite upon unloading (assuming minimal permanent deformation occurred before the strain burst, as suggested by Fig. 2).  In contrast to the [001]-oriented pillars, the [112]-oriented pillars did not exhibit superelasticity (Fig. 3d). During loading, a small strain burst occurs at $463\pm93$ MPa. Upon further loading up to a nominal engineering strain of $10\%$ , a large strain burst was observed at $620\pm36~\mathrm{MPa}$ . Upon unloading, no significant deviation from linearity was observed, leaving behind a permanent strain. As such, it appears that the small strain burst at $463~\mathrm{MPa}$ during loading is more likely attributed to a minor dislocation pop-in event, although it is possible that a small amount of stress-induced martensite formed before its growth was arrested. This will be further discussed in Section 4.1.2.

(a) $\{011\}{<}100{>}$ slip, (b) $\{001\}{<}100{>}$ slip, (c) $\{114\}{<}22\bar{1}>$ deformation twinning, (d) $\{112\}<\bar{1}\bar{1}1>$ deformation twinning, (e) conventional B2-B19/ martensitic transformation (MT) with $[011]_{\mathtt{B19^{\prime}}}$ type II twins, and (f) herringbone $\tt B2-\tt B19^{\prime}$ MT with $(001)_{\mathtt{B19^{\prime}}}$ compound twins. - Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The results for the two primary slip systems $\{001\}{<}100{>}$ and $\{011\}{<}100{>}$ [29,30] are shown in Fig. 7a-b. As expected, the minimum Schmid factor of zero occurs for the [001] orientation for both slip systems, suggesting that slip should not be able to occur. The [112] orientation has a near-maximal Schmid factor of 0.471 for $\{011\}{<}100{>}$ slip, meaning that slip is highly favored in this orientation, and a moderate Schmid factor of 0.333 for $\{001\}{<}100{>}$ slip.  Similar calculations for the reported deformation twinning modes {114} $\scriptstyle\left\{<221>\right.$ and {112} ${<}111{>}$ [21,56,57] are shown in Fig. 7c-d. The [001] orientation has a Schmid factor of 0.314 for the $\{114\}{<}221>$ mode and a near-maximal Schmid factor of 0.471 for the $\{112\}{<}111>$ mode. For the [112] orientation, the results are reversed, with Schmid factors of 0.471 and 0.314 for $\{114\}{<}22\bar{1}>$ and {112} ${}<\bar{1}\bar{1}1>$ , respectively.  Schmid factors for conventional $\tt B2-B19^{\prime}$ transformation with $[011]_{\mathrm{B19^{\prime}}}$ type II twins are shown in Fig. 7e. We find the minimum Schmid factor of 0.253 at [111] and the maximum Schmid factor of 0.513 near [149]. The [001] and [112] orientations have Schmid factors of 0.386 and 0.439, respectively, which are both reasonably large and suggest that transformation should easily be possible at these orientations. However, we have observed $(001)_{\mathtt{B19^{\prime}}}$ compound twins (Fig. 6), which cannot form according to the conventional theory of martensite [22,47]. An alternate transformation mode that can give rise to $(001)_{\mathtt{B19^{\prime}}}$ compound twins is the formation of a herringbone microstructure, and Schmid factor calculations for this mode are shown in Fig. 7f. The maximum Schmid factor of 0.520 is found at [001]. The lowest Schmid factor of 0.169 is found at [011]. The [112] orientation has an intermediate Schmid factor of 0.348. Thus, the herringbone $\tt B2-\tt B19^{\prime}$ transformation can occur in both our [001] and [112] pillars, but the stress to induce martensite should be lower in the [001] pillars.

- caption: Fig. 8. Orientation dependence of phase transformation stress from MD simulations. (a) Phase transformation stress (MPa). - Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The observed orientation dependence of the stress to induce martensite is also supported by the present MD simulations (Figs. 6c, 6d and 8a) under small-scale and/or constraining boundary conditions. While these simulations are limited in that they do not take into account dislocation-induced plasticity and also did not predict deformation twinning or subtle changes in martensite microstructure, they do provide additional support to the present experimental findings. The MD simulations consistently showed $(001)_{\mathtt{B19^{\prime}}}$ compound twins in a herringbone microstructure. Fig. 8a shows the orientation dependence of the critical stresses for MT from these MD simulations, which are consistent with our experimental observations that the stress to induce martensite is higher in the [112] orientation than the [001] orientation. In Fig. 8b and c, we converted the critical stresses into CRSS values assuming either conventional or herringbone $\tt B2-B19^{\prime}$ MT, respectively (using the Schmid factors in Fig. 7e-f). As can be seen, the CRSS values, which should be orientation independent, for conventional B2-B19′ MT vary strongly with orientation (Fig. 8b) whereas there is significantly less variation for herringbone B2-B19' MT, again demonstrating that herringbone $\tt B2-\tt B19^{\prime}$ MT with $(001)_{\mathtt{B19^{\prime}}}$ compound twins can better explain the orientation dependence observed in the MD results.
