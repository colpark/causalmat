- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): After 50 cycles, the half and symmetric cells were disassembled for post-mortem analysis. Cells were also disassembled and studied after 1 and 5 cycles (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Optical images of the LMA after 50 cycles are shown in Figure 1a, in which a film can be seen building up where the anodes were matched against their respective cathodes. This film on the lithium anode is known to consist of a mixture of dead lithium and SEI but will be hereafter referred to only as SEI for simplicity.[33] At higher cycle numbers, the anodes in both the symmetric and half cells show thicker, darker, and more brittle SEI. The SEI on LiLi was generally more fragile as suggested by the optical images, but showed variation between samples.  Further characterization of the lithium SEI was performed with scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Figure 1b shows onaxis images from several representative regions of the lithium SEI after 50 cycles, demonstrating significant spatial inhomogeneity. Within a sample, one region may show relatively compact nodules, while another may show high-surface-area mossy growth. This is true of the lithium from both the symmetric and the half cells, and is also true of the 1 and 5 cycle cells (Figure S2, Supporting Information). Such inhomogeneity is not unusual for LMA in unoptimized conditions, such as with carbonate electrolytes.[34,35] Within the bounds of this inhomogeneity, it is not possible to label one morphology as denser or more compact. To gain further insight, cross-sectional SEM imaging was also performed to measure the thickness of the SEI. During sample preparation, the fragile SEI would conveniently peel away from the underlying lithium metal, providing a clear contrast. While this peeling may not occur perfectly at the boundary between lithium and SEI, the quantitative nature of the thickness measurements allows for meaningful and statistical comparison. Figure 1c shows typical cross-sectional images of the lithium SEI after 50 cycles in both the half and symmetric cells. Figure S2, Supporting Information, shows images after 1 and 5 cycles, while Table S1, Supporting Information, gives the average thicknesses and their standard deviations. The average SEI thickness is lower for LiNMC after 5 and 50 cycles, and is too thin to effectively measure after 1 cycle, in contrast with the SEI on LiLi. While the differences in thickness are within 1–2 standard deviations, the consistency between cycle numbers suggests that these differences are meaningful.  To characterize the composition of the lithium-metal SEI, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed on the cycled lithium anodes, with peak positions indexed in Table S3, Supporting Information. Despite the low detection limit of XPS, negligible transition-metal signal was found for the LMA cycled against NMC (Figure  1d, Figure S3c, Supporting Information). This can be attributed to several factors. First, transition-metal dissolution and crossover in lithium-ion batteries is typically a long-term process, occurring over hundreds of cycles.[30] In addition, the SEI on graphite is typically measured in tens or hundreds of nanometers, while the lithium SEI shown here is tens or hundreds of microns. It is then unsurprising that any crossed-over transition metals are diluted below the detection limit. Meanwhile, the SEI components on the half and symmetric cell lithium anodes show many similarities. As cycling proceeds for both samples, the $\mathrm{Li}_{2}\mathrm{O}$ , Li, and $_\mathrm{C-Li}$ peaks decrease in intensity while the LiF, $\mathrm{Li_{x}P F_{y}},$ $\mathrm{Li_{x}P O_{y}F_{z}}$ , and $\mathrm{C}{-}\mathrm{O}$ peaks increase (Figure S3a,b, Supporting Information). The metallic Li and $\mathrm{Li}_{2}\mathrm{O}$ are associated with the pristine lithium chip and its oxide passivation layer, which logically reduce in intensity as the SEI grows. The CLi carbide is an artifact of the sputtering process and can be directly correlated to the metallic Li.[36] While these peaks decrease on cycling for both LiLi and LiNMC, they decrease more rapidly for LiNMC, which may be an indication of reduced dead lithium content. Meanwhile, the LiF, $\mathrm{Li_{x}P F_{y}}$ $\mathrm{Li_{x}P O_{y}F_{z}},$ and $\mathrm{C}{-}\mathrm{O}$ signals can be attributed to electrolyte degradation and SEI formation.[32,37–41] Phosphorous and fluorine containing compounds are generated through the decomposition of $\mathrm{PF}_{6}$ and its byproducts, such as $\mathrm{PF}_{5}$ , $\mathrm{POF}_{3}$ , $\mathrm{POF}_{2}\mathrm{OH}$ , and $\mathrm{POF}(\mathrm{OH})_{2}$ . Carbon at intermediate binding energies ${\mathrm{'ROCO}}_{2}{\mathrm{Li}}_{\mathrm{{;}}}$ ${\mathrm{C}}{=}0$ , $\mathrm{C}{-}\mathrm{O}$ etc.) and the associated oxygen at high binding energies result from the decomposition and polymerization of the carbonate solvents. Small differences in these peaks, such as greater signals for $\mathrm{Li_{x}P F_{y}}$ and $_{\mathrm{C-O}}$ for the lithium paired with NMC, do suggest some influence from crossover effects.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure 2 shows Nyquist plots of the symmetric, half, and full cells after the 1st, $20\mathrm{th}$ , and 100th cycles. The associated cycling data, as well as Nyquist plots after the 5th and 50th cycles, are available in Figure S6, Supporting Information. A summary of the impedance values is given in Table S3, Supporting Information. Within this data, there are two direct comparisons that can be made: that between the LMA in the symmetric and half cells, and that between the NMC900505 cathodes in the half and full cells. For the LMA, impedance remains relatively flat or slightly decreasing during the first 50 cycles. One explanation is that, because EIS was performed during a charge step, the impedance response at the anode is due only to the freshly plated lithium. However, this flat or declining impedance is also present for the lithium “cathode,” which has been freshly stripped in the symmetric cell, suggesting that it is not an artifact of plating. Instead this behavior may be attributed to the low porosity of the SEI formed in carbonate electrolytes, which is unable to impede plating or stripping despite its bulk resistivity.[42] Interestingly, the general lack of impedance growth in early cycles for the LMA is also evidence of anode-to-cathode crossover. It is well established that capacity fade in coin half cells is significantly greater than that in pouch full cells, as will be demonstrated later. Therefore, if the effective impedance of the lithium anode remains unchanged during cycling, then the greater overpotential growth and capacity loss of half cells must be attributed to the behavior of the cathode. Of course, the LMA cannot maintain a low impedance forever. Between 50 and 100 cycles, the impedance of the lithium anodes in both the symmetric and half coin cells rises sharply. Due to the limited stability of the carbonate electrolyte, even these flooded cells can become subject to dry-out as cycling continues. Notably, the flat impedance with a sharp rise at 100 cycles exists in both the symmetric and half cells, further confirming the limited impact of transition-metal dissolution and crossover relative to the inherent reactivity of the LMA. The moderately higher lithium-anode impedance at 100 cycles for the symmetric cell can be attributed to more severe dry-out induced by the additional lithium electrode.  The other impedance comparison that can be made is between the NMC in the full cells and the NMC in the half cells. If crossover from the LMA occurs, it would be expected to increase the impedance of the cathode in the half cell. This does appear to be true when considering only the first cycle, after which the impedance of NMCLi is twice as high as that of ${\mathrm{NMC}}{\mathrm{-Gr}}.$ This large difference after one cycle can be attributed to the significant reactivity of the pristine electrodes. The LMA will generate large amounts of decomposition species as it undergoes initial SEI formation, while the NMC cathode has not yet formed a CEI and may be particularly vulnerable to attack. Despite this early difference, cathode impedance growth between the 1st and 100th cycles is remarkably similar between the two cell types, being ${\approx}17$ $\Omega~\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ . As with the LMA, this could suggest that no significant crossover from the anode was occuring; however, there is a clear difference in CEI composition and thickness as revealed through XPS (Figure S4, Supporting Information). This could instead mean that the CEI formed under the influence of crossover differs in composition but not impedance. However, there is another theory which relates to cell design itself.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): As expected, full cells in the pouch format exhibit excellent capacity retention, maintaining $91\%$ capacity after 200 cycles (Figure 3a–c). The pouch half cells also show improved capacity retention relative to coin half cells, with $85\%$ capacity remaining after 200 cycles and $92\%$ remaining after 100 cycles, compared to $89\%$ after 100 cycles for the coin half cells. The superior capacity retention of pouch half cells over coin half cells is further evidence of anode-to-cathode crossover. By stabilizing the LMA through increased stack pressure, fewer decomposition products are formed, and the cathode is subject to reduced attack.  XPS spectra of the cathodes from the cycled pouch half and pouch full cells are available in Figure 3d, while the peak positions are indexed in Table S4, Supporting Information. Compared to the XPS data from the coin cell cathodes, there is a clear shift for both samples toward higher binding energies in the O 1s spectra, representing a greater proportion of polymeric carbonate breakdown products. Similarly, both samples demonstrate an additional F 1s peak at intermediate binding energy, which can be attributed to $\mathrm{LiPF_{6}}$ decomposition products such as $\mathrm{Li_{x}P O_{y}F_{z}}$ . While the cathodes paired with lithium and with graphite both see an increase in these peaks, their growth is more pronounced for the cathode paired with lithium. The intensity ratios of $_{\mathrm{Ni-O}}$ to F KLL and of $(\mathrm{LiF+Li_{x}P O_{y}F_{z}})$ to PVDF are also greater for NMCLi, confirming a thicker average CEI.  There is a wide body of literature on the reactivity of lithium metal with carbonate electrolytes.[32,37–41] While this knowledge is rarely extended to the concept of crossover, it stands to reason that the reaction pathways and decomposition products in these reports should mirror that seen here. To confirm this and determine which crossover species caused the change in CEI composition, electrolyte from the cycled pouch half cell was analyzed with nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (NMR, Figure 4). NMR spectra for $^1\mathrm{H}$ , $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ , $^{19}\mathrm{F}$ , and $^{31}\mathrm{P}$ were collected to test for both salt and organic solvent decomposition. The pristine electrolyte is also compared as a baseline. In the $^1\mathrm{H}$ spectrum, the peak at 7.3 ppm is associated with vinylene carbonate (VC), an electrolyte additive present initially as $2\%$ by mass. Signal from the VC is also present in the $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ spectrum at $135.3~\mathrm{ppm}$ . After cycling, the intensities of these signals are reduced by a factor of four, which is unsurprising given the additive’s role as a reactive stabilizer of the LMA.[38,45] Despite clear evidence of electrolyte breakdown, however, there are no meaningful additional peaks in the $^1\mathrm{H}$ or $^{13}\mathrm{C}$ spectra which can be associated with soluble products of carbonate decomposition. Meanwhile, there are peaks for the salt decomposition product $\mathrm{POF}_{2}\mathrm{OH}$ which are visible as a doublet in the $^{19}\mathrm{F}$ and a triplet in the $^{31}\mathrm{P}$ spectra. However, these peaks are less intense for the cycled electrolyte than for the pristine, where $\mathrm{POF}_{2}\mathrm{OH}$ is also present as a trace impurity. For both samples, the intensity is much lower than that of other reports looking at electrolyte after thermal decomposition or after cycling in symmetric cells.[32,37] It was hypothesized that this lack of impurities after extended cycling is itself a result of crossover. Though soluble species may form at a given electrode, they can be consumed at the other electrode such that their steady-state concentration in the electrolyte is low. Optical images of the cycled pouch cell separators are given as evidence for the generation of crossover species despite their absence from NMR (Figure 3e). The separator from the lithium-metal battery has taken on a yellow-brown color, which other reports have associated with polymeric and fluorinated compounds generated by, respectively, solvent and salt breakdown.[32] Furthermore, lithium symmetric pouch cells were cycled with flooded electrolyte for additional NMR analysis. Figure 4 shows that after just 25 cycles, the $\mathrm{POF}_{2}\mathrm{OH}$ signal intensity was markedly higher than that in the pristine electrolyte, in contrast with the electrolyte from the lithiummetal half cell.
