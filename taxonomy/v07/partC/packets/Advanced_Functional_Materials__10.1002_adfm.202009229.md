- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) is widely established as a traumatic brain injury (TBI) biomarker and can be used in early diagnosis. As essential primary human immune cells, leukocytes are recruited to injured cerebral sites during TBI response, where they can interact with and potentially bind to TBI biomarkers. To date, no studies have demonstrated ultra-low GFAP binding enumeration on leukocytes. Herein, a dark-field imaging technique coupled with computational analysis is introduced to quantify GFAP bound to peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Dark-field microscopy (DFM) with a custom-written image acquisition software is developed for rapid 3D PBMC imaging by utilizing specific antiGFAP monoclonal antibody functionalized gold nanoparticles (antiGFAP-AuNPs) as contrast-generating probes. Subsequently, the developed algorithm is utilized in processing thousands of acquired images for rapid visualization and enumeration of bound antiGFAP-AuNP on each leukocyte. The proposed method is demonstrates the specific binding of GFAP to the surface of PBMCs on a healthy donor blood. Thereafter, subpopulations of PBMCs with antiGFAP-AuNP binding are identified with the assistance of fluorescence imaging and DFM imaging, paving a new way to understanding the relationship between TBI and leukocyte classes. Hence, this study offers a rapid and ultra-sensitive strategy for biomarker assessment following TBI.  The collection of blood samples is routine in clinical practice, which is an obvious advantage as these samples can be made available for additional testing such as GFAP detection without altering the standard clinical protocol during the blood drawing process. Especially, small volume blood draws can be readily carried out in austere environments, enabling rapid capture of blood for analysis.[13] Significantly, peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs, mainly containing B cells, monocytes, natural killer (NK) cells, and T cells) are primary human immune cells that are easily isolated from collected blood samples. PBMCs respond to cell signals as they circulate and can infiltrate into brain injury sites due to blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption for injury and inflammatory responses.[14] Subsequently, the recruited PBMCs in injury sites can bind to and carry away numerous TBI biomarkers. GFAP as a type III intermediate filament expressed in neurological tissue, especially in mature astrocytes of the central nervous system, is one of these biomarkers released from cerebral microenvironments after extracranial injuries (Figure 1(i)). Alternatively, directly tracking the binding of GFAP to the surface of PBMCs is anticipated to overcome the background noise or false positives/negatives issues which hinder existing methods, and could therefore be used as a diagnostic as well as a means to better understand TBI mechanisms. However, the interactions between the released GFAP and leukocytes have not yet been investigated to date, much less the enumeration of ultra-low concentration GFAP on leukocyte surface in healthy individuals.  In this work, dark-field microscopy (DFM) equipped with customized image acquisition software was employed for acquiring 3D PBMC images by utilizing highly specific antiGFAP monoclonal antibody-functionalized AuNPs (antiGFAP-AuNPs) as contrast-generating probes to visualize and enumerate GFAP on the surface of each PBMC (Figure 1). Healthy donor blood samples purchased from the RBC corporation were processed for PBMC extraction by density gradient centrifugation (Ficoll-Paque) (Figure  1(ii)). The PBMCs were then incubated with antiGFAP-AuNPs under room temperature for 5 min and washed three times with PBS to remove the unbound antiGFAP-AuNPs for DFM imaging. Owing to the strong LSPR signals of AuNPs, AuNPs bound to the surface of PBMCs could be sensitively recognized by DFM imaging. After the initial readout by the computer (Figure  1(iii)), a machinelearning computational pipeline was utilized to rapidly enumerate bound AuNPs on the surface of PBMCs (Figure  1(iv)). Of note, the mean number of AuNPs bound on each PBMC was ${\approx}27.9\pm6.9$ in antiGFAP-AuNP treated PBMCs. Additionally, the subpopulations of PBMCs can be recognized through staining of lineage markers using different fluorophore-conjugated antibodies. Thus, the combination of fluorescence imaging of cell lineage and DFM imaging of bound AuNPs could help to distinguish B cells and monocytes with high antiGFAP binding, providing significant information on the interactions between TBI biomarkers and primary human immune cells. Although future clinical studies involving TBI patients with differing levels of injury need to be performed, these preliminary results demonstrate a quantitative single-cell approach with the potential for evaluating TBI severity in the future.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To establish plasmonic nanoparticles for the specific detection of GFAP on the surface of PBMCs, the conjugation of antiGFAP antibodies with AuNPs were performed by BBI Solutions (Figure S4, Supporting Information). The size of AuNPs and as-prepared antiGFAP-AuNPs were measured by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), which indicated that the diameters of both AuNPs and antiGFAP-AuNPs were centered at $80\mathrm{nm}$ (Figure S4b,c, Supporting Information). As shown in Figure S4c, Supporting Information, a thin protein coating highlighted by green arrows could be observed on the AuNP surface of antiGFAP-AuNP conjugates due to successful conjugation of antiGFAP antibodies. The hydrodynamic sizes of  AuNPs and antiGFAP-AuNPs determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) were $90.5\pm1.1_{\:\cdot}$ and $106.5\pm1.2\mathrm{nm}$ (Figure S4d, Supporting Information), respectively. The conjugation of antibodies with AuNPs was also confirmed via UV–vis absorption spectroscopy, as the conjugated antibodies on the nanoparticle surface caused a detectable redshift in the absorption spectrum (Figure S4e, Supporting Information).  Before investigating GFAP detection on the surface of PBMCs, the specific in vitro GFAP binding ability of the as-prepared antiGFAP-AuNPs was systematically evaluated (Figure  3). Prior to characterization, the nanoprobes were incubated with different concentrations of GFAP at $37~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ in a water bath for $30\mathrm{min}$ (Figure  3a). Subsequently, the specific binding ability of the antiGFAP-AuNPs was also quantitatively studied via DLS (Figure 3b and Table S1, Supporting Information). In comparison to antiGFAP-AuNPs alone, distinct hydrodynamic size increases could be measured as a function of increasing GFAP concentrations from 0 to $50\mathrm{nM}$ . The measured size on the DLS gradually increased from $106.5\pm1.2$ to $111.6\pm1.6$ , $113.5\pm1.0$ , $127.9\pm3.4$ , $145.4\pm10.7$ , $137.2\pm4.8$ , and $147.1\pm\:3.9\mathrm{nm}$ as the final ratios of antiGFAP-AuNPs:GFAP were increased from 1:0 to 1:250, 1:500, 1:1000, 1:1500, 1:2000, and 1:2500 (Figure  3b and Table S1, Supporting Information), respectively. From these results, we can observe that DLS size reached a plateau when the antiGFAP-AuNPs:GFAP ratio was over 1:1500 (Figure  3b), demonstrating that the binding sites on antiGFAP-AuNPs were saturated. Of particular significance was that the polydispersity index (PDI) was quite low in each GFAP-incubated antiGFAP-AuNP group (Table S1, Supporting Information). The low values of PDI coupled with DLS sizes suggested the formation of uniform monomers instead of nanoparticle dimers or trimers in the mixture, which indicated that each GFAP antigen was captured by only one antiGFAP-AuNP as illustrated in Figure  3a. Remarkably, the size of antiGFAPAuNPs incubated with bovine serum albumin (BSA) at 1:2500 ratio charactered by DLS was $107.5\pm4.5$ nm, which was similar to that of antiGFAP-AuNPs (Figure S5a, Supporting Information). AntiBiotin-AuNPs incubated with GFAP at 1:2500 ratio also showed a similar size with antiBiotin-AuNPs. Thus, nonspecific absorption can be ruled out by the results shown in Figure S5a, Supporting Information. Furthermore, antiGFAPAuNPs and antiGFAP-AuNPs incubated with GFAP at 1:2500 ratio were respectively imaged via TEM with phosphotungstic acid staining (Figure S5b,c, Supporting Information). Compared to antiGFAP-AuNPs, there were almost no differences in the thickness of protein layer on antiGFAP-AuNPs incubated with GFAP at 1:2500 ratio, suggesting no multilayer proteins were formed on AuNPs. In addition, the absorption spectrum was slightly red shifted and broadened in antiGFAP-AuNPs incubated with GFAP at a 1:2500 ratio group (pink) than that of antiGFAP-AuNPs without GFAP incubation (olive green) (Figure 3c). These absorption spectral features further point to binding of GFAP on the surface of the nanoparticles and a lack of nanoparticle aggregation, even at the highest tested concentration of GFAP.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The concept of GFAP detection on the surface of PBMCs via antiGFAP-AuNPs binding is illustrated in Figure  4a. As described in Figure  3, each GFAP is recognized by only one antiGFAP-AuNP, the specific binding of antiGFAP-AuNP to GFAP is expected to appear as well-distributed monomers, dimers, and trimers throughout the surface of PBMCs rather than aggregated nanoparticle clusters. To investigate this specific binding on the PBMC’s surface at nanometer resolution, PBMC samples were prepared for scanning electron microscopy (SEM) via a $5\mathrm{min}$ incubation with antiGFAP-AuNPs (Figure  4b). After incubation, the PBMCs were washed three times with PBS to remove the unbound antiGFAP-AuNPs. The estimated resolution of the SEM was ${\approx}3\mathrm{nm}$ , which is more than sufficient for the visualization of the $80\mathrm{nm}$ diameter AuNPs in this study. As shown in Figure 4b, high numbers of nanoparticles bound on PBMC cell membranes were found in the PBMCs treated with antiGFAP-AuNPs. Since this could be the result of both specific and non-specific binding, blocking experiments were performed to eliminate non-specific binding of antiGFAP-AuNPs on the surface of PBMCs. Briefly, Fc blockers (BD Pharmingen, 564  220) were utilized to treat PBMCs prior to antiGFAP-AuNPs treatment. There is still high number of bound antiGFAP-AuNPs on the surface of PBMCs observed, suggesting those antiGFAP-AuNPs were specifically bound to the GFAP antigens on the surface of PBMCs. The specificity of antiGFAP-AuNPs binding was further investigated by competitive binding experiments. To that end, PBMCs were pre-treated with $5~\upmu\mathrm{L}$ of $0.5~\mathrm{mg~mL^{-1}}$ Clone 273807 antiGFAP monoclonal antibodies. As antiGFAP antibodies were expected to bind to GFAP antigens on the surface of PBMCs, the specific binding of antiGFAP-AuNPs could be blocked by antiGFAP antibodies. As shown in the third column image in Figure 4b, low antiGFAP-AuNPs binding was observed, further demonstrating that the antiGFAP-AuNPs can specifically capture the GFAP antigens on the surface of PBMCs. It is also worth mentioning that several large aggregated antiGFAP-AuNPs clusters throughout the antiGFAPAuNPs treated samples regardless of pre-treatment conditions were found on the surface of PBMCs; these clusters are thought to be an artifact caused by drying process during SEM sample preparation.  In order to rapidly acquire GFAP binding information from a simple benchtop microscope, a customized DFM with high resolution and rapid 3D cell image acquisition software coupled with ML was developed for GFAP enumeration on the surface of PBMCs. It is worth noting that hundreds of 3D PBMC images could be captured in minutes, which was much faster than SEM imaging. The acquired 3D PBMC images were then processed and analyzed automatically using the algorithms described in prior studies.[15] To confirm the reproducibility and consistency, the method was performed on three healthy donor samples, with each measurement carried out in triplicate. As can be seen in montages of DFM images shown in Figure 4c, the binding tendencies were similar to those from SEM imaging (Figure 4b), that is, bright signals could be observed in antiGFAP-AuNP-treated PBMCs, demonstrating high binding of antiGFAP-AuNPs on the surface of PBMCs. Significantly, the DFM signals decreased after both blocking experiments and competitive binding experiments. DFM montages showed a similar AuNP binding trend on PBMCs with the same treatment in SEM images as described in Figure 4b, suggesting our strategy for GFAP numeration on PBMCs is rapid and reliable.  These 3D PBMC images in Figure 4c were further analyzed by a machine-learning-based pipeline to generate binding histograms of the AuNP number on each individual PBMC (Figure 4d). A negligible number of false positive signals were counted on the surface of PBMCs without any treatment, resulting in the blue histogram nearly located at zero counts. In sharp contrast, the counting histograms of antiGFAPAuNP-treated PBMCs (red) show that the bound AuNPs were distinctly distributed at a range from 60 to 80, representing an extremely low level of GFAP on PBMCs, around 80 to a few hundred binding events per cell. This is, therefore, consistent with low serum GFAP in healthy individuals. Moreover, the binding AuNPs in 60–80 range were obviously reduced after blocking experiments and competitive binding experiments, as evidenced in orange and green counting histograms. The corresponding quantification of average AuNP numbers per cell derived from Figure  4d are shown in Figure S8, Supporting Information. The plotted distribution results show that ${\approx}27.9\pm$ 6.9 AuNPs bound onto each PBMC in the antiGFAP-AuNPtreated group (red), which is believed to be caused by both nonspecific absorption and specific binding. Additionally, $18.1~\pm$ 7.5 AuNPs were counted on antiGFAP-AuNP-dosed PBMCs in blocking group (orange), corresponding to the number of GFAP antigens available on each PBMC for binding after blocking non-specific Fc absorption. Based on these numbers, we can deduce that around $10\mathrm{AuNPs}$ were bound to each PBMC via non-specific absorption, which corresponds well with the results from the antiGFAP-AuNP-dosed PBMC in competitive binding group ( $[5~\upmu\mathrm{L}$ of $0.5~\mathrm{mg~mL^{-1}}$ Clone 273807 antiGFAP antibodies, green). There were $12.3\pm4.1$ AuNPs measured on each PBMCs in the latter group.  Based on the above-mentioned blocking experiments and competitive binding experiments, we postulated the binding of antiGFAP-AuNPs on PBMCs could be completely blocked by the combination of Fc blockers and antiGFAP antibodies (Figure 4e). In order to verify our hypothesis, Fc blocker dosed PBMCs were treated with different volumes of Clone 273807 antiGFAP antibodies $(0.5\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{mL^{-1}})$ , rabbit polyclonal antiGFAP antibodies $(0.5~\mathrm{mg~mL^{-1}})$ ), goat polyclonal antiGFAP antibodies $(0.5~\mathrm{mg}~\mathrm{mL^{-1}})$ and Clone SMI25 antiGFAP antibodies $(0.5~\mathrm{mg~mL^{-1}})$ ). Subsequently, the PBMCs were incubated with antiGFAP-AuNPs for GFAP numeration on the surface of PBMCs. As shown on the histograms in Figure  4e, compared to antiGFAP-AuNP-treated PBMCs, the average number of AuNPs per cell decreased from $31.7\pm9.3$ to $23.2\pm7.9$ for the Fc blocker treatment group, due to the reduction of non-specific absorption. In addition, Fc blocker-dosed PBMCs incubated with antiGFAP antibodies showed diminished AuNP binding properties, with the bound AuNP number gradually decreasing from $17.9\pm5.3$ to $13.1\pm\:2.5$ , $6.6\pm2.8$ , and $4.8\pm1.8$ as the amount of Clone 273807 antiGFAP antibodies increased from 0.5 to 1, 2.5, and $5~\upmu\mathrm{L}$ , respectively. These quantitative results suggested that the non-specific absorption and specific binding of antiGFAP-AuNPs can be entirely blocked by Fc blockers coupled with free Clone 273807 antiGFAP antibodies. It is noted that the antiGFAP-AuNP-treated PBMC results in Figure S8, Supporting Information and Figure  4e were obtained in separate, independent experiments. The measured binding values were compared using student’s t test, which found the means are not statistically significantly different $(p=0.21)$ . Additionally, both polyclonal antiGFAP antibodies, but not Clone SMI25 antiGFAP antibodies could block the specific binding of antiGFAP-AuNPs on PBMCs, further confirming Clone SMI25 antiGFAP antibodies cannot block the specific binding of Clone 273807 antiGFAP-AuNPs to GFAP on the surface of PBMCs (Figure S9, Supporting Information). Taken together, we can conclude that antiGFAP-AuNPs could be captured by PBMCs through both non-specific absorption and specific binding, which were consistent with flow cytometry results depicted in Figure 2. Of particular significance was that DFM imaging coupled with computational analysis strategy could rapidly count extremely low, near-single molecule levels of GFAP on the surface of each PBMC, which is a significant advantage when compared to flow cytometry and SEM assays.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): These results clearly demonstrate the ability to visualize and enumerate the extremely low level of GFAP binding using DFM imaging coupled with computational analysis. Equally important, however, is to understand the distribution of GFAP binding to individual subpopulations of leukocytes, as the specific interaction of GFAP and immune cells can shed light on the body’s response to injury. To examine leukocytespecific GFAP binding, the DFM setup was combined with epi-fluorescence imaging (Figure 5). After incubating with different fluorophore-conjugated lineage marker antibodies and antiGFAP-AuNPs, the same field of view of PBMCs could be visualized by both fluorescence and DFM imaging. Validation experiments were carried out to show that fluorescence labeling did not interfere with AuNP enumeration, allowing measurements of surface binding and cell lineage to be carried out orthogonally. Lineage antibody labeling was carried out to identify B cells, monocytes, NK cells, and T cells (Figure  5a). The number of each subtype of PBMCs with high/low binding of antiGFAP-AuNPs is plotted in Figure  5b, demonstrating that $47.4\%$ of B cells and $71.7\%$ of monocytes showed high binding of antiGFAP-AuNPs $(>30\mathrm{AuNPs}$ per cell), while only $8.9\%$ and $6.4\%$ of NK cells and T cells showed high binding, respectively. In order to validate the quantification accuracy of our classification approach, a traditional flow cytometry assay was performed (Figure S10, Supporting Information). The flow cytometry results showed similar PBMC subpopulation trend compared to our fluorescence imaging method, further confirming the validity of our customized DFM and fluorescence micro­ scopy approach. To the best of our knowledge, classification of PBMCs demonstrating high GFAP antigen binding results using a multimodal imaging approach has not been previously reported.
