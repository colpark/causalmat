- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Fig. 1 shows the BSE-SEM images of the Al-Cr-Fe-Ni-Ti alloys that were subjected to the homogenization treatment and subsequent air-cooling. Though the alloy specimens are not subjected to the aging treatment, they exhibit two-phase composite structures comprising dispersed nanoprecipitates in the matrix. Based on the results of the thermodynamic calculations using ThermoCalc software (provided in Supplementary Fig. S1), the matrix and the nanoprecipitate are expected to be BCC phase and $\mathrm{L}2_{1}$ phase, respectively. It has been reported that the $\mathrm{L}2_{1}$ precipitate exists in the same ferritic alloy system even after rapid quenching by a melt-spinning [37,38]. Furthermore, Baik et al. [39] reported that the coarsening of $\mathrm{L}2_{1}$ precipitate in FBB8-4Ti $(\mathrm{wt\%})$ alloy occurs by agglomeration during aging at $700^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for 100 hours, whereas the additional nucleation and growth hardly happen, resulting in a very small change in the volume fraction of precipitate as about ${\sim}1\%$ . Therefore, it is inferred that the precipitation occurs rapidly to ensure the approximate completion of nucleation and growth during air-cooling.  The SEM images show the evolution in the size, number density, volume fraction, and distribution of the precipitates with the change in the Al content of the alloys. As the Al added to the alloys, the shape of precipitates changes from approximately spherical (8Al and 10Al alloys) to cuboidal with a rounded corner (12Al and 14Al). Finally, highly elongated cuboidal precipitates with sharp corners form in the 16Al alloy. The average size of the precipitates decreases, while the number density and volume fraction increase with the increase of Al content, thereby resulting in the decrease in the interprecipitate spacing. The precipitates in the 8Al and 10Al alloys are randomly distributed in the matrix. However, a regular arrangement of the precipitates is observed for the alloys with a higher content of Al $(\geq12\mathrm{at}\%)$ , which is a commonly observed phenomenon in Ni-based superalloys. This microstructural feature is attributed to the elastic interactions between the misfitting precipitates. The interactions originate from the interfacial coherency and vary with the interprecipitate spacing, i.e., a repulsive force for a short distance and attractive force for a long-distance [40, 41]. Therefore, this result implies that the addition of Al not only decrease the interprecipitate spacing but also improves the interfacial coherency, which can be clearly confirmed from the TEM analysis in the following section.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The inhabitation of the dislocation motion by precipitates results in strengthening via four main mechanisms: 1) order strengthening, 2) modulus strengthening, 3) coherency strengthening, and 4) Orowan dislocation looping. In evaluating the precipitation hardening mechanism, we assumed that the shape of the precipitates is spherical to apply to the strengthening models. (In the case of the cuboidal precipitates in 12Al, 14Al, 16Al alloys, it was assumed that the width is the same as the diameter.) When a dislocation passes through the ordered precipitate, an antiphase boundary with a higher energy state as compared to that of the desired bonding can be formed. Furthermore, the difference in shear modulus between the matrix and precipitate induces a local change in the line tension in the segment of the dislocations that pass through the precipitate. Therefore, the controlling mechanisms during the precipitate shearing are order strengthening and modulus strengthening. The occurrence of dislocation shearing depends on the size of the precipitate. When the size of the precipitate is smaller than a specific critical size, dislocation shearing occurs, while above that, dislocation looping is formed [63]. Dislocation looping is generally caused by incoherent particles, and it is difficult for precipitates to maintain lattice coherency. However, if the size of precipitate is large enough, dislocation looping can occur despite the existence of lattice coherency. Brooks [64] proposed a geometric criterion for coherency, which stated that an interface with a length of $\Delta t$ remains coherent when $\Delta t\cdot\delta\leq b$ . Therefore, the critical radius where the transition from dislocation shearing to looping appears is defined as $r_{c r i t.}=b/2\delta$ , where b is the Burgers vector of dislocation. As summarized in Table 4, the critical radius of precipitate is at most ${\sim}16~\mathrm{nm}$ , indicating that the precipitate shearing is not the controlling mechanism at room temperature. The dislocation motion is also hindered by the coherency strain field around precipitates which bends the dislocations toward the low-energy position. The equations for the coherency strengthening, $\sigma_{c o h}$ , are given by [65,66]:  The contribution of precipitation hardening to the yield strength is calculated from the experimental data and strengthening models. The results indicate that there is a gradual increase in the contribution of precipitation hardening with the addition of Al (Table 4). However, the actual extent of coherency strengthening would be significantly lower than that indicated by the calculated value due to the misfit dislocations. Thus, the Orowan dislocation looping is the primary controlling mechanism for precipitate hardening in 8Al and 10Al alloys. Furthermore, the fine secondary precipitate also contributes to the mechanical strength of the alloys. This is attributed to the improvement in lattice coherency and the decrease in the precipitate size.
