- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ nanosheets were prepared by a lithium intercalation chemical method.[35] The exfoliated ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ dispersions $(1.0\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{mL^{-1}})$ are dark gray and can be stable for at least 3 weeks in laboratory conditions (Figure 1a). As shown by atomic force microscopy (AFM) characterization, the lateral size of individual ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ nanosheets mostly ranges from 100 to $500\mathrm{nm}$ , and their thickness is about 1.0 and $1.1\mathrm{nm}$ , respectively (Figure 1a; Figure S1, Supporting Information). The TMD colloidal solutions $(0.1\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{mL^{-1}})$ are negatively charged depending on $\mathrm{pH}$ , and ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ is more negative than ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ (Figure 1b). Two possible origins are responsible for the negative surface charge. First, it may come from the electron transfer from organolithium to the TMDs during the intercalation process.[36] Second, it may come from the formation of negatively charged $\mathrm{WS}_{2}\mathrm{-OH^{-}}$ and $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}\mathrm{-OH}^{-}$ at TMD/water interface via separate chemical equilibrium[24,37]  Heterogeneous TMD multilayer membranes can be fabricated via a two-step process (Figure  1d). First, ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ membranes (termed as $\mathrm{WS}_{2}\mathrm{M}$ and $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}\mathrm{M}$ ) were fabricated separately through a commonly used vacuum filtration method. The mass ratio of the solid content was kept at 1:1. The thicknesses of the as-prepared ${\sf W S}_{2}{\bf M}s$ and $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}\mathrm{Ms}$ are 0.82 and $0.89\upmu\mathrm{m}$ , respectively. Afterwards, two pieces of $\mathrm{WS}_{2}\mathrm{M}$ and $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}\mathrm{M}$ were stuck together, and a subsequent water fusion process was conducted by filtration of pure water through the membrane stack, in order to make the two parts fuse together.  The J-TMDM is hydrophilic on both ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ (contact angle (CA) $=45.4^{\circ}\pm2.5^{\circ})$ ) and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ sides $(\mathrm{CA}=53.0^{\circ}\pm1.6^{\circ})$ , and shows smooth surface morphology in scanning electronic microscope (SEM, Figure  1e,f). SEM characterization on the cross section exhibits uniform laminar microstructure along the entire thickness (Figure  1g). The $\mathrm{WS}_{2}/\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$ heterostructure is not distinguishable in SEM, and no seams or voids are found on submicrometer scale, confirming the two parts are fused together.  Further energy-dispersive X-ray spectrometry (EDS) was carried out in SEM on the cross section of J-TMDMs (Figure 1g). From tungsten element mapping (W, in red), one can see that the ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ multilayers appear merely in the bottom half of the membrane cross section. A clear interface in between (indicated by the arrows) reveals the $\mathrm{WS}_{2}/\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$ heterojunction. Element mapping of sulfur (S) and molybdenum (Mo) is indistinguishable due to peak overlap in energy spectrum (Figure S2, Supporting Information). So, the expected Mo distribution only in the upper half is not observed. Instead, it shows the backbone of the J-TMDM indicated by the content of sulfur element (in green). Even so, the $\mathrm{WS}_{2}/\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$ interface can still be faintly visible (indicated by the arrows), in which the upper part shows relatively deeper color due to the additional signals from Mo, and its position is well in accord with that revealed by tungsten element mapping.  X-ray diffraction (XRD) characterization unveils that both the homogeneous ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}{\mathrm{Ms}}$ and $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}\mathrm{Ms}$ , and the heterogeneous J-TMDMs exhibit very similar patterns in wet state with a (001) diffraction peak found at about $2\theta=7.6^{\circ}$ (Figure 1h). This value corresponds to a calculated interlayer spacing $(d)$ of about $1.16~\mathrm{nm}$ . Considering the thickness of the triple atomic structure $(\approx0.32\mathrm{nm})$ ,[38] the effective channel height in TMD-based membranes is about $0.84~\mathrm{nm}$ , which allows the permeation of most hydrated simple salt ions.[39] The XRD tests also confirm excellent stability of the J-TMDMs. As shown in Figure S3, Supporting Information, without any pretreatment, the laminar microstructure of J-TMDMs remains unchanged, whenever they were dried in air or stored in water. The microstructure can keep stable even after being immersed in water for at least 10 days. These results suggest that the JTMDMs are quite stable upon hydration, in agreement with previous research on homogeneous TMD membranes.[40,41]

Under a scanning voltage, a weak rectified ion-transport behavior is found through the J-TMDMs with an ionic current rectification (ICR) ratio of about 1.6 under $1.0~\mathrm{V}$ (Figure  2b). This result is in contrast to the symmetric current–voltage responses found in homogeneous ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ or ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ membranes (Figure  2c,d). As revealed by the XRD results (Figure 1h), the geometry of the laminar nanochannels in ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ or ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ parts is quite similar. Therefore, we can attribute the observed weak ICR effect to the inhomogeneous surface charge distribution. From zeta potential measurements, ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ is more negative than ${\mathrm{Mo}}S_{2}$ (Figure 1b). In this case, the preferential ion-transport direction can be understood as from the low-surface-charged part $(\mathrm{MoS}_{2})$ to the high-surface-charged part $(\mathsf{W}\mathsf{S}_{2})$ . This explain is supported by previous experimental and theoretical results established on 1D nanofluidic systems by $\mathrm{us}^{[6]}$ and other groups.[42,43]

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): With equivalent ionic concentrations on both sides, under two-side illumination $(94.1\mathrm{mW}\mathrm{cm}^{-2})$ ), we observe a remarkable ionic photocurrent $(I_{\mathrm{ph}})$ and a concomitant photovoltage $(V_{\mathrm{ph}})$ through the J-TMDM, without any external electrical bias (Figure  3a,c). Upon illumination, the recorded ionic current soon arises from zero to about $10.3~\mathrm{nA}$ within $30~\mathrm{s}$ , suggesting a net cationic flow from ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ to ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ . The claimed ion transport is also confirmed by directly measuring the changes in $\mathrm{K^{+}}$ concentration in the two reservoirs after light illumination via inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (Part 5, Supporting Information). Likewise, the photovoltage reaches its peak value of $-31.1\mathrm{mV}$ in the same time period. In contrast to other active-ion-transport membranes with different ionic solutions placed on the two sides, in which the open-circuit potential is largely contributed by liquid-junction potential difference,[22] the photovoltage measured here purely reflects a light-induced ion-transport motive force. Intriguingly, once the illumination was carried out on only one side, either from the ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ side (Figure 3d,f) or from the ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ side (Figure 3g–i), the $I_{\mathrm{ph}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{ph}}$ appear in the same direction, but their magnitudes reduce to roughly one half of that obtained under twoside illumination (Figure 3c).  As control experiments, similar ionic photoresponse cannot be found with nanoporous polycarbonate membranes (Figure S6, Supporting Information). We also checked the photoresponse from homogeneous ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ membranes under two-side illumination. As shown in Figure S7, Supporting Information, only very limited photocurrent and photovoltage signals were recorded, whose magnitudes are generally more than one order lower than using J-TMDMs. According to our previous research, the scarce photoresponse from ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ membranes may result from the intrinsic inhomogeneity during sample fabrication.[45] The above results are summarized in Figure S8, Supporting Information, from which we can come to the conclusion that the J-TMDM is essential for the remarkable ionic photoresponse.  The magnitude of ionic photocurrent and photovoltage grows up with light intensity (Figure 3j). At different KCl concentrations, the photocurrent is positively correlated with the concentration due to increased number of ionic charge carriers, while the photovoltage remarkably falls down at high concentrations (Figure 3k). The declining concentration dependence of photovoltage reflects the nature of active ion transport, that is, to drive ion migration at high concentration needs to overcome a relatively high energy barrier.[23]  The transport mechanism is explained in terms of a photovoltaic effect in $\mathrm{WS}_{2}/\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$ heterostructures. Figure  3b illustrates the relative alignment of electronic bands of ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ based on previous theoretical results.[46] It shows that the $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}/\mathrm{WS}_{2}$ heterostructure forms a type II semiconductor heterojunction, with the conduction band minimum (CBM) present in ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ and the valence band maximum (VBM) stayed in ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ . Upon illumination from both sides (Figure 3a), photoexcited electron–hole pairs generate in both parts. Due to the type II band alignment, the excited electrons in ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ jump to the ${\mathrm{Mo}}{\mathrm{S}}_{2}$ layers, while the holes created in ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ transfer to the ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ layers.[47] This dual process forms an efficient charge separation between the two parts, and consequently generates a photovoltaic motive force for ion transport (Figure 3c).  With separate light transmission tests, we can assure that the light irradiation penetrates the entire thickness of the ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ half-membranes ( $0.8{-}0.9~\upmu\mathrm{m}$ thick). Considering the strong optical absorption of TMD materials, the transmitted light intensity is very weak. Then, in the one-side illumination tests (Figure 3d,g), the majority of the photoexcited charge carriers are generated in the illuminated part, and thus the charge transfer due to type II band alignment takes place merely from the illuminated part to the non-illuminated part. With the electrons transferring from ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ to ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ (Figure 3e)  or the holes transferring from ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ to ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ (Figure  3h), the polarity of the photovoltaic potential difference keeps unchanged, that is, the high-potential area remains in the ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ part. Even so, the magnitude of photocurrent and photovoltage remains roughly one half of that obtained under twoside illumination (Figure 3f,i).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Following this mechanism, the ion-transport direction can be reversed by altering the binary combinations with appropriate band alignment. For example, taking ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ as a reference, both the CBM and the VBM of ${\mathrm{Mo}}{\mathrm{S}}_{2}$ are lower than the counterparts of ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ , thus, in the time trace of the $\mathrm{MoS}_{2}/\mathrm{WS}_{2}$ heterostructure, a downward ionic photocurrent response is observed, suggesting a cationic transport from ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ to ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ (Figure 4a). These results confirm that the remarkable ionic photoresponse are powered by the charge separation in TMD-based heterostructures.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): By means of initiating a net ion transport between equilibrium electrolyte solutions, this transport phenomenon can be used to generate ionic power. Taking the $\mathrm{WS}_{2}/\mathrm{MoS}_{2}$ heterostructures, for example, with equivalent KCl solutions $(10^{-3}\textbf{M})$ placed on two sides, under illumination, the presence of nonzero intercepts on both potential and current axes, relative to that in dark condition, indicates a remarkable light-to-ionic energy conversion (Figure  5a,b). Typical open-circuit voltage $(V_{\mathrm{oc}})$ and short-circuit current $(I_{\mathrm{sc}})$ are on the level of several tens millivolt and microampere per square centimeter, respectively. Under normal conditions (unbuffered KCl solution), both $V_{\mathrm{oc}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{sc}}$ grow up with light intensity (Figure S15, Supporting Information). This trend is in agreement with the measurement shown in Figure  3j. Then, the maximum ionic power density $(P_{\mathrm{max}})$ and the corresponding energy conversion efficiency can be calculated following an established method (Experimental Section).[23,51] $P_{\mathrm{max}}$ is also positively correlated with light intensity, and reaches an uttermost of $0.62\mathrm{mW}\mathrm{m}^{-2}$ (Figure 5c). This is potentially caused by the increased number of photoexcited carriers and enhanced surface charge density under stronger light irradiation.[24] In addition, the energy conversion efficiency remains on the order of $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-4\%}$ (Figure 5c), which is on the same level with existing light-driven ion-transport systems.[23,44,52]  The light-to-ionic power generation can be optimized by varying ionic concentration and $\mathrm{pH}$ . Analogy to the photocurrent and photovoltage measurements in Figure $3\mathrm{k\Omega}$ the $V_{\mathrm{oc}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{sc}}$ also exhibit opposite growing trends with respect to the change in concentration from $10^{-5}$ to $10^{-2}\textbf{M}$ (Figure  5d). This consequently leads to a non-monotonic concentrationdependence in the generated power, whose peak value is found at moderate concentration of about $10^{-3}\textbf{M}$ . Under this optimal concentration, $P_{\mathrm{max}}$ and its efficiency can be further improved by tuning the $\mathrm{pH}$ (Figure  5e). The magnitude of $I_{\mathrm{sc}}$ and $V_{\mathrm{oc}}$ is remarkably promoted in alkaline salt solutions (Figure S16, Supporting Information). At $\mathrm{pH}\mathrm{~\normalfont~{~9~}~}$ , the power density approaches nearly $2~\mathrm{mW}~\mathrm{m}^{-2}$ under the light intensity of $94.1~\mathrm{mW~cm}^{-2}$ , which is equivalent to approximately one sun excitation. The efficiency also grows up to about $2\times10^{-4\%}$ . This pH-dependent enhancement is mainly due to the high surface charge density in ${\mathrm{WS}}_{2}$ and ${\mathrm{MoS}}_{2}$ at alkaline $\mathrm{pH}$ (Figure 1b).  Similar light-to-ionic energy conversion can be found in a variety of electrolyte solutions containing monovalent or divalent salt ions (Figure  5f). With divalent cations in the electrolyte solution, such as ${\mathrm{Mg}}^{2+}$ , $\mathrm{Ca}^{2+}$ , $\mathrm{Cu}^{2+}$ , and $\mathrm{Ba}^{2+}$ , the generated power is even slightly improved. This result also suggests that, besides simple salt solutions, the heterogeneous TMD membranes are capable of capturing ionic power from various electrolyte solutions with complex ionic compositions.
