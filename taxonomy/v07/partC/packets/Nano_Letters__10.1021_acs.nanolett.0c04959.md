- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The interactions of multicomponents in the high-concentration polymeric interlayer have been investigated, as shown in Figure 1. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) indicates that our high-concentration PVC contains more than 50 wt $\%$ bis(trifluoromethane)sulfonimide lithium salt (LiTFSI), which is much higher than the 1:10 molar ratio of a lithium salt (e.g., ${<}30$ wt $\%$ ) in typical PEO electrolyte (Figure 1a).13,14 It can be seen that the PVC decomposes around $300^{\circ}\mathrm{C},$ whereas LiTFSI around $400~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . The weight decrease below $300~^{\circ}C$ may stem from the absorbed moisture or monomers due to the hygroscopic nature of LiTFSI salt during the TGA sample preparation in ambient air. After the different drying processes (i.e., as-prepared, $120~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ heating, and vacuum drying), the samples still show a similar trend, implying that the highconcentration salt interacts strongly with small molecules. For these reasons, we have fabricated the polymeric interlayer in the glovebox. The Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra are shown in Figure 1b, c and display each component’s role in the high-concentration polymer. The concentrations (i.e., 0.5, 1.0, and $2.0\mathrm{~M~}$ ) in Figure 1b, c are calculated on the basis of the amount of LiTFSI added in vinylene carbonate (VC) solution, that may differ after polymerization. The final polymerized products have a higher salt concentration due to the partial evaporation of unreacted VC molecules. From FTIR spectra, it is seen that the ${\mathrm{C}}{=}0$ groups are strongly coordinated with $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ ions and thus promoting salt dissociation into $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ and ${\mathrm{TFSI}}^{-}$ ions (Figure 1b). Also, increasing anion peaks (i.e., ${\mathrm{CF}}_{3},$ $\mathrm{SO}_{2},$ and $\mathrm{S}\mathrm{-N}$ bands) were observed with a higher salt concentration in PVC (1.0 and $2.0\mathrm{~M~}$ in Figure 1c). The overall FTIR comparison can be found in Figure S1, where apparent peak variation can be observed with concentration difference. It should be mentioned that increasing concentration may intensify the salt−polymer interactions, but it also prevents polymerization, leading to unreacted monomers or small-molecule polymers. Therefore, a $1\textbf{M}$ concentration of LiTFSI was chosen as an optimized concentration for the polymeric interlayer since the effect of salt started to appear beyond $1\textbf{M}$ LiTFSI, as shown in Figure 1b, c.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): As a representative application, a high-concentration polymer was introduced as an interlayer on the cathode. The preparation process of PVC coated cathode is indicated in Figure 2a. First, a conventional slurry-cast cathode was prepared before the PVC layer coating. Such a cathode inevitably exhibits a highly porous structure due to the dilute solution casting from microsized cathode particles (NCM or LCO) mixed with nanosized carbon particles connected by a polymeric binder (Figure S2). This porous structure is beneficial for the batteries using liquid electrolytes because hierarchical pores provide sufficient pathways for the liquid electrolyte to diffuse for the desired redox reactions on the electrode surface. However, a solid-state electrolyte cannot access those pores because of the rigid nature, leading to pointto-point contact, limiting charge carrier transport. In our study, we successfully filled these void areas of the composite cathode with $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ ion-conductive polymers. The high-concentration PVC solution (i.e., $1.0\mathrm{~M~}$ LiTFSI in VC) was injected into the slurry-cast-cathode. After thermal cross-linking, the PVC solution was polymerized, which uniformly coats the active materials’ surface and connects discrete particles with an ionconducting polymer. The high-concentration PVC is sufficiently adhesive, which can also form a free-standing pellet (Figure S3).  The polymerized chemical structure is shown in Figure 2b. During thermal cross-linking, a multicomponent mixture will be polymerized into a high-concentration polymer with dissociated $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ and ${\mathrm{TFSI}}^{-}.$ , where $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ ions are coordinated by either functional groups of PVC chains or TFSI anions. The structure of the polymer solution before and after cross-linking was compared by FTIR spectroscopy. As shown in Figure 2c, ${\mathrm{=}}\mathrm{C-H}$ groups were observed in the PVC solution before cross-linking from ${\mathrm{C}}{=}\mathrm{C}$ bonds of VC solvent molecules. The ${\mathrm{C}}{=}\mathrm{C}$ groups are then polymerized, turning into $_{\mathrm{C-C}}$ chains after cross-linking, and thus ${\mathrm{=}}\mathrm{C-H}$ groups disappear while $-\mathrm{C-H}$ groups appear. The FTIR confirmed the complete polymerization of PVC after thermal-heating. The PVC solutions with different LiTFSI concentrations were also investigated, showing well cross-linked polymers (Figure S4). It should be noted that the unreacted VC will be evaporated, which effectively leads to the higher salt concentration, as confirmed by TGA in Figure 1a. The abundant ${\mathrm{C}}{=}0$ groups still exist after cross-linking, which will act as major solvating groups for the dissociation of lithium salt.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The electrochemical properties of the high-concentration PVC will significantly affect the battery performance of the PVC coated cathode. The PVC’s property may determine many aspects, including rate performance, electrochemical stability, and mechanical durability. First, we investigated the electrochemical stability of high-concentration PVC when in contact with a typical cell component, such as stainless steel. Figure 3a shows the schematic illustration of the measurement setups for the linear sweep voltammetry (LSV). Both configurations used Li-metal as a reference/counter electrode (RE/CE), composite polymer electrolyte as a solid-state electrolyte, and stainless steel as a working electrode (WE). Although bare stainless steel was used for the control sample (i.e., bare SSE), we coated one of the stainless steel electrodes with a high-concentration PVC (i.e., PVC-coated SSE) to investigate its effect on oxidative stability. As shown in Figure 3b, bare SSE started showing severe electrolyte decomposition even after $3.5\mathrm{~V~}$ vs $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ . Such poor stability is expected according to the widely known vulnerability of PEO to oxidation. On the other hand, PVC- coated SSE demonstrated much-enhanced anodic stability showing a negligible current density $(<2\mu\mathrm{Acm}^{-2})$ up to $5\mathrm{~V~}$ vs $\mathrm{Li/Li^{+}}$ . It is believed that abundant oxygen groups (especially carbonyl groups) are highly resistive to oxidation leading to improved oxidation stability. The high-concentration PVC also possesses a significantly high amount of TFSI anions, whose highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) level is even lower than most polymers.44 Combining the electrochemical stabilities from both polymers and anions, high oxidative stability could be achieved.  Ion transport was also investigated for the high-concentration PVC layer to compare with PEO, which has been wellknown for its poor conductivity at low temperatures. For the conductivity measurement, the PEO electrolyte was fabricated by the solvent-drying process of PEO and LiTFSI solution. In contrast, the high-concentration PVC electrolyte was prepared by pressing powders into a pellet (See photographs of Figure S3). As demonstrated in Figure 3c, PEO shows a large ohmic resistances of 2680 ohm $\mathrm{cm}^{\breve{2}}$ at $30~^{\circ}C$ and 141 ohm $\bar{\mathrm{cm}^{2}}$ at 60 $^{\circ}{\cal C}.$ . In the case of PVC, much smaller impedances were observed, such as $83~\mathrm{ohm~cm}^{2}$ at $30~^{\circ}C$ and $25\mathrm{ohm~cm}^{2}$ at 60 $^{\circ}{\cal C}.$ . These impedance differences are anticipated to make significantly different results in terms of electrochemical performances. The large impedance limits the full utilization of active materials and thus lowering usable specific capacity. On the basis of the impedances at different temperatures, conductivities have also been calculated, as shown in Figure 3d. It is widely accepted that a minimum conductivity of $\bar{1\times10^{-4}}$ $\mathrm{Scm^{-1}}$ is required for practical battery applications. With this in mind, our PVC shows an excellent conductivity of $1.1~\times$ $10^{-4}~\mathrm{{S}~c m^{-1}}$ at $30~^{\circ}C$ and $4\times10^{-4}\mathrm{Scm^{-1}}$ at $60~^{\circ}C$ that are beyond the required conductivity. It was observed for PEO that the conductivity could not reach $1\times10^{-4}\mathrm{Scm^{-1}}$ even at a high temperature of $60^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . To summarize, our highconcentration PVC has superior electrochemical performances, including improved oxidation stability up to $5\mathrm{~V~}$ vs $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ and a high ionic conductivity over $1\times\mathrm{i}0^{-\bar{4}}\mathrm{Scm}^{-1}$ .

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The Li | NCM battery was tested to investigate the PVC coating layer’s role in battery performance, as shown in Figure $_{4\mathsf{a}-\mathsf{c}}$ . NCM is one of the widely used active materials for practical battery applications. NCM is also known for its high specific capacity and notably high operating voltage. Since the energy/power density is closely related to the specific capacity and operating voltage, the oxidatively vulnerable PEO-based solid electrolytes have limited the employment of high voltage cathode $(>4\mathrm{~V~}$ vs $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li}^{\cdot}$ ). Therefore, we used the NCM cathode as a representative high-voltage cathode ( $4.25\mathrm{~V~}$ vs Li/ $\mathrm{Li}^{+}.$ ) to utilize the advantage of the high-concentration polymeric interlayer.  First, the cell impedances were measured by electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), as shown in Figure 4a. The bare NCM $\left(\operatorname{Li}\mid\operatorname{NCM}\right)$ battery’s impedance became larger after 10 cycles, which possibly stemmed from the increased interfacial resistance due to the PEO decomposition. The impedance of PVC-coated NCM (Li | PVC-NCM) has been slightly increased after 10 cycles but stays almost the same. Besides, Li | PVC-NCM demonstrated much smaller total resistances than the Li | NCM cell before/after the cycling test. The superior transport property and electrochemical stability of the PVC interlayer would show improved kinetics at the interface with NCM cathode. The fitting of the EIS spectra clearly indicates that the reduced impedance of Li | PVC-NCM mainly comes from the interfacial resistance (Figure S5). In good agreement with cell impedances, the Li | PVC-NCM battery also demonstrated improved rate performance (Figure S6). The EIS spectra of PEO-coated and PVC-coated NCM were also compared, where poor ionic conductivity of PEO resulted in larger impedances (Figure S7).  Figure 4b summarizes the cycling stability of Li | NCM battery. The fast capacity fade was observed as the cycle goes by, which later shows almost no extractable capacity after 100 cycles (capacity retention at 100th cycle $=3\%$ ). This inferior cycling stability is well expected because of the PEO electrolyte’s poor oxidative stability at high voltage. Since the cutoff voltage was $4.25\mathrm{~V~}$ vs $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li}^{+}$ , which is much beyond the stable voltage window of PEO $\mathrm{~\zeta~}{<}4.0\mathrm{~V~}$ at most), the PEO electrolyte’s severe decomposition may result in such larger overpotentials and poor cycling stability.  In contrast, the Li | PVC-NCM battery demonstrated muchimproved cycling performance showing around $75\%$ capacity retention after 100 cycles (Figure 4c). The average Coulombic efficiency (CE) of Li | NCM battery is $98.8\%_{;}$ , while Li | PVCNCM battery is over $99.9\%$ . This significant difference in CE also indicates the enhanced stability of Li | PVC-NCM battery, whereas poor stability of the Li | NCM upon contact with polymer electrolyte. The evolution of cell overpotentials was also observed in voltage profiles during cycling (Figure S8).  We also tested LCO and LFP batteries with the PVC coating layer (i.e., Li | PVC-LCO and Li | PVC-LFP, respectively). Figure 4d shows Li | PVC-LCO battery’s voltage profiles, where the overpotential almost does not change, implying stable charge/discharge processes. Interestingly, Li | LCO battery with PVC coating demonstrated superior cycling stability with a capacity retention of $90\%$ after 100 cycles (Figure 4e). It is believed that a slightly lower cutoff voltage $(4.2\mathrm{~V~}$ vs $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li}^{+}$ ) for Li | PVC-LCO battery may result in higher capacity retention compared to the NCM cathode. For this reason, the Li | PVC-LFP battery with a much lower cutoff voltage of $3.8\mathrm{~V~}$ shows almost no capacity fade upon the cycling (Figure S9). One interesting phenomenon was observed in cycling performance. All the cycling tests exhibit an activation process that increases capacity during the initial cycles, such as 10 cycles for NCM (Figure 4c), 3 cycles for LCO (Figure 4e), and ${\sim}80$ cycles for LFP (Figure S9b) to reach the maximum capacity. Such activation processes are affected by various factors, including solid electrolyte interphase (SEI)/cathode electrolyte interphase (CEI) layers, ${{\mathrm{Li}}^{+}}$ ion pathways in the electrolyte, electrode, and the electrode/electrolyte interface, or even physical contact between components.45,46 It is believed that the superior electrochemical property (i.e., high ionic/electronic conductivity) and morphology (i.e., smooth surface with large size) may lead to a fast activation process of LCO particles. In contrast, a very long process was required for LFP due to the sluggish charge transport and large surface area.

On the other hand, a clear difference was observed with the bare NCM cathode, where many pores, as well as large gaps between NCM and other components, were observed (Figure S10).
