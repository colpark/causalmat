- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure  1a shows the high-resolution powder X-ray diffraction (HRPD) patterns of as-synthesized undoped, phosphorusdoped, and boron-doped $\mathrm{LiNi}_{0.92}\mathrm{Co}_{0.04}\mathrm{Mn}_{0.04}\mathrm{O}_{2}$ powders (denoted as U-LNCM, P-LNCM, and B-LNCM, respectively) obtained by synchrotron radiation X-ray beam. All the diffraction patterns were completely indexed based on the hexagonal $\alpha{\mathrm{-NaFeO}}_{2}$ -type layer structure (space group $R{\cdot}3m\}$ without an impurity phase. The clear splitting of (006)/(102) and (018)/(110) diffraction peaks implies a well ordered layer structure with a high crystallinity for all three powders (Figure 1b).[26] With P and  B doping, the diffraction peaks were shifted to lower $2\theta$ values (Figure  1c) and the lattice parameters determined by Rietveld refinement increased (Figure S1 and Table  S1, Supporting Information), indicating that both $\mathrm{P}$ and B were successfully incorporated into the LNCM lattice. Previously, TM octahedral or Li tetrahedral site has been proposed as the possible B and P doping site.[30–33] The incorporation of P and B into the LNCM lattice was further corroborated by Fourier transformation infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR) and X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES). In FT-IR spectra (Figure  S2, Supporting Information), the absorption band at $531~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ , which is generally ascribed to TM–O–TM $(\mathrm{TM}=\mathrm{Ni}$ , Co, and Mn) lattice vibration, was shifted to the lower wavenumber with $\mathrm{P}$ and B doping, while the new weak peaks at 1039 and $1045~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ corresponding to P–O and B–O vibrations, appeared in P-LNCM and B-LNCM powders, respectively.[32] In Mn K-edge and Ni K-edge XANES spectra (Figure S3, Supporting Information), the absorption edges were slightly shifted to the lower energy implying that the oxidation states were slightly reduced with P and B doping to maintain the charge neutrality.[34] The chemical compositions determined by inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectroscopy (ICP-AES) indicated that the molar ratio of Mn:Co:Ni was close to the nominal composition (92:4:4) and the doping concentration of $\mathrm{P}$ and B was estimated to be $\approx1\mathrm{mol\%}$ (Table S2, Supporting Information). Based on the characterization results, it can be inferred that phosphorus and boron were successfully incorporated into the LNCM crystal structure.

All three powders are more or less spherical in shape with an average diameter of ${\approx}10~{\upmu}\mathrm{m}$ (Figure 2a,d,g). - Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To investigate the plate-like primary particles in P-LNCM and B-LNCM powders in detail, the cross-sectional specimens were prepared by focused ion beam (FIB) technique and observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The FIB preparation details and the TEM observation direction are schematically illustrated in Figure S4 in the Supporting Information. FIB specimen of plate-like primary particle should display rod-like morphology. The high magnification TEM images revealed that both primary particles exhibited the similar rod-like cross-sectional morphology as expected, but the primary particle of B-LNCM showed much thinner frontal facets (planes exposed to the surface (Figure  3a,c)). The atomic-resolution high angle annular dark filed (HAADF) scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) images and selected area electron diffraction (SAED) patterns indicated that both primary particles had the identical crystallographic orientation where the lateral plane was (003) and the frontal facets were (102) and (10-4) (Figure  3b,d). Consequently, the $a$ -axes of the primary particles are directed to the surface of the secondary particle and the Li layers in each primary particle are aligned along the radial direction, which can facilitate the Li ion diffusion from surface to center and enhance the rate capability of LNCM cathode.[22] The textured microstructure of P-LNCM and B-LNCM powders was further confirmed by crystal orientation map obtained using ASTAR (automatic crystal orientation and phase mapping package for TEM). To obtain the crystallographic orientation maps (Figure  3e–g), each raw crystal orientation map was combined with its corresponding index map. (This map is built based on the matching values between recorded diffraction pattern and most correlated diffraction pattern that is calculated from the crystal structural model; Figure S5, Supporting Information.)[35] The color code for the crystal orientation map is shown in Figure 3i. The U-LNCM powder showed the randomly distributed color index, indicating the randomly oriented primary particles (Figure 3e). On the contrary, the orientation maps of P-LNCM (Figure 3f) and B-LNCM (Figure 3g) are indexed as green tone, suggesting that most of the primary particles have the similar crystallographic orientation, that is, textured microstructure. The thinner and more elongated morphology of B-LNCM compared to P-LNCM can be further confirmed by ASTAR analysis. The development of radially aligned microstructure in boron-doped LNCM and LNCA cathodes has been attributed to the modification of surface energy through boron-doping, which was supported by the density functional theory (DFT) calculation.[24,25] However, it is well known that the addition of boron or phosphorus to the oxides forms the amorphous or glass phase at the grain boundary and modifies the microstructure during sintering through recrystallization or grain growth inhibition.[36–40] We highly speculate this possibility and further studies are ongoing to verify the detailed underlying mechanism of microstructure development in B-LNCM and P-LNCM powders.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The electrochemical properties of as-synthesized U-LNCM, P-LNCM, and B-LNCM powders as cathodes for LIBs were evaluated using a 2032 coin-type half-cell within a voltage range of $2.5\substack{-4.4\mathrm{~V~}}$ (vs $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}})$ at a current density of 0.1C $(1{\mathrm C}=200~\mathrm{mA}~\mathrm{g}^{-1})$ (Figure  4). All three electrodes exhibited the similar initial charge/discharge voltage profiles, but the discharge capacity of P-LNCM $(221.4\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}})$ and B-LNCM (221.7 mAh $\mathrm{g}^{-1})$ electrodes was slightly smaller than that of U-LNCM $(228.5\mathrm{mAhg^{-1}})$ due to the electrochemically inactive phosphorous and boron incorporation (Figure  4a). However, the cycle performance was significantly improved by P and B doping. After 100 cycles at the current density of $0.3\mathrm{C}$ , the U-LNCM electrode retained $64\%$ of its initial discharge capacity whereas the P-LNCM and B-LNCM electrodes retained $72\%$ and $78\%$ of their initial discharge capacity, respectively (Figure  4b). Furthermore, the P-LNCM and B-LNCM electrodes exhibited much enhanced rate capability compared to the U-LNCM electrode. At a high current density of 2C, the P-LNCM and B-LNCM electrodes still delivered the high discharge capacity of 151.7 and $153.4\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ , respectively, which was much higher than $105.7\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ of the U-LNCM electrode (Figure  4c). To reveal the different cycling behavior of the three electrodes, the differential capacity $(\mathrm{d}Q\mathrm{d}V^{-1})$ profiles were obtained by differentiating the 5th, 10th, 15th, and $30\mathrm{th}$ charge/discharge curves (Figure  4d–f). All three electrodes showed a series of phase transitions during charging from hexagonal to monoclinic $({\mathrm{H}}1{\mathrm{-}}\mathrm{>M})$ ), monoclinic to hexagonal $(\mathrm{M}{\rightarrow}\mathrm{H}2\$ ), and hexagonal to hexagonal $(\mathrm{H}2{\rightarrow}\mathrm{H}3)$ ). The final $_{\mathrm{H}2\to\mathrm{H}3}$ phase transition is reported to be responsible for the abrupt anisotropic lattice contraction in the $c$ -axis direction, which induces the mechanical stress on the particle that leads to the particle cracking and, consequently, the capacity fading.[8,10,11] For the U-LNCM electrode, the intensity of $_{\mathrm{H}2\to\mathrm{H}3}$ peak rapidly decreased and the polarization voltage (potential gap between cathodic peak and anodic peak) significantly increased with cycles, implying the irreversible structural damage during cycling (Figure  4d). However, the irreversibility was reduced in the P-LNCM and B-LNCM electrodes (Figure  4e,f). In particular, the intensity and position of $_{\mathrm{H}2\to\mathrm{H}3}$ peak were relatively well maintained in the B-LNCM electrode, which was consistent with the improved cycle stability as described earlier. To see the consequence of different irreversible phase transition, the after 50th cycled electrodes were examined by synchrotron X-ray beam. The HRPD patterns showed that all three cycled electrodes retained their original $R{\cdot}3m$ layer structure without a new phase formation (Figure  S6, Supporting Information). However, a careful observation revealed that the (003) diffraction peak was shifted to a lower $2\theta$ in all three cycled electrodes. The peak shift is attributed to the different amount of remaining Li in the cycled electrodes, that is, Li deficiency. The Li deficiency of the cycled electrodes is due to the buildup of the electrode polarization that prevents the full-lithiation of the cathodes even at discharged state.[41] The (003) peak shift was more significant in U-LNCM $(0.05^{\circ})$ than P-LNCM $_{(0.04^{\circ})}$ and B-LNCM $(0.02^{\circ})$ electrodes. Thus, the degree of peak shift in the HRPD patterns, extent of polarization in the $\mathrm{d}Q\mathrm{d}V^{-1}$ plots, and capacity decay were in the same order ( $\mathrm{U-LNCM>P{\cdot}L N C M}$ $>$ B-LNCM). The variation of lithium ion mobility $(D_{\mathrm{Li}}^{\phantom{\dagger}})$ and charge transfer resistance $(R_{\mathrm{ct}})$ with cycles further supported the different cycling behavior of the three electrodes (Figure S7, Supporting Information).[34,42] $D_{\mathrm{{Li}}}^{+}$ and $R_{\mathrm{ct}}$ were determined by galvanostatic intermittent titration technique (GITT) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), respectively, and the details are elsewhere.[34,42,43] The GITT measurement was carried out by applying the current pulse of 0.1C for $20\mathrm{min}$ and subsequently resting for $^{1\textrm{h}}$ . $D_{\mathrm{{Li}}}^{+}$ continuously decreased with cycles in all three electrodes, but the reduction was more severe in U-LNCM, followed by P-LNCM and B-LNCM electrode (Figure  S7a, Supporting Information). $R_{\mathrm{ct}},$ obtained by the semicircle in the Nyquist plot of EIS, increased with cycles in all three electrodes, but the rate of resistance increment was different and it was in the order of B-LNCM, P-LNCM, and U-LNCM (Figure S7b, Supporting Information). Consequently, the degradation of lithium ion mobility and charge transfer resistance with cycles was consistent with the observed cycle stability of LNCM electrodes.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To find out the reasons for the improved cycling stability of the P-LNCM and B-LNCM electrodes, in situ XRD analysis was performed to monitor the phase evolution and structural change during the delithiation. The electrodes were charged to $4.4~\mathrm{V}$ (vs $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Li^{+}})$ at a current density of 0.2C using the modified cointype cell with $3\mathrm{mm}$ hole at its center. Figure 5a represents the contour plot of (003) and (104) diffraction peaks for U-LNCM, P-LNCM, and B-LNCM electrodes. All three electrodes exhibited the continuous phase transitions upon charging, which were well matched with the previous reports.[17] Upon first charging, the $(003)_{\mathrm{H}1}$ peak smoothly shifted to a lower $2\theta$ and the $(003)_{\mathrm{H}2}$ peak was evolved at ${\approx}4.1\mathrm{V}.$ The shift of (003) peak to a lower angle indicates the lattice expansion in $c$ -axis direction, which is attributed to the increase of oxygen–oxygen repulsion due to a Li removal within the Li-slab.[44] However, upon further charging, the (003) peak rapidly shifted toward higher $2\theta$ for all three electrodes, which implies the abrupt decrease of interslab distance along $c$ -axis direction. The peak shift during $_{\mathrm{H}2\to\mathrm{H}3}$ phase transition was $1.06^{\circ}$ , $1.04^{\circ}$ , and $1.03^{\circ}$ for U-LNCM, P-LMCM, and B-LNCM, respectively, and thus, the difference was not significant. This result suggests that the incorporation of phosphorous or boron on the crystal lattice did not noticeably mitigate the intrinsic structural change associated with the multiple phase transitions during charging. However, the beneficial effect of P and B doping can be clearly seen in the crosssectional SEM images of the cycled electrodes (Figure 5b,c). In the 50th cycled U-LNCM electrode, the numerous microcracks were formed throughout the secondary particle while some of the cracks crossed the entire particle, indicating that the randomly oriented primary particles in U-LNCM electrode could not relieve the internal stress and avoid the crack nucleation and propagation. It is expected that these microcracks provide a pathway for the electrolyte infiltration and deteriorate the newly exposed interior particles by forming the resistive surface, which results in the rapid capacity fading.[45,46] In contrast, no visible structural damage was found and the structural integrity was well preserved in the cycled P-LNCM and B-LNCM electrodes, showing that the radially aligned textured microstructure had the superior structural stability against the mechanical stress resulting from the anisotropic dimensional change during cycling.[24,22] The structural stability of the P- and B-doped electrodes could be further confirmed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) measurement. The DSC results for the charged electrodes showed that the doped LNCMs reduced the thermal activity compared to U-LNCM by shifting the onset of exothermic reaction to a higher temperature and reducing the amount of heat generated (Figure S8, Supporting  Information). The improved thermal property of P-LNCM and B-LNCM can be attributed to the decrease of microcracks and electrolyte infiltration during charge process.[8] Based on the obtained results, it can be concluded that the structural stability of textured microstructure is responsible for the enhanced cyclability of the P-LNCM and B-LNCM electrodes.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): First, EELS analysis was performed to understand the electronic states of the cycled electrodes (50 cycles at 0.3C) at discharge state (Figure  6; Figure S9, Supporting Information). In EELS spectra, O K-edge arises from exciting an $\mathrm{O}_{\mathrm{1s}}$ shell electron to unoccupied hybridized $\mathrm{TM}_{3\mathrm{d}}{-}\mathrm{O}_{2\mathrm{p}}$ orbitals, and TM L-edge measures the dipole allowed transitions from $\mathrm{TM}_{2\mathrm{p}}$ orbitals to unoccupied $\mathrm{TM}_{3\mathrm{d}}{-}\mathrm{O}_{2\mathrm{p}}$ orbitals.[47–50] While TM L-edge has been successfully utilized as an indicator for the oxidation states of TM ions in the layered cathodes, there are debates on using the O K pre-edge as an evidence of oxygen redox or oxygen vacancy.[47,51] This is because the TM-O hybridization decisively contributes to the O K pre-edge intensity. Furthermore, it has been proposed that the line-shape evolution of O K pre-edge is mainly determined by TM 3d state variation.[52] Nonetheless, as the O K pre-edge evolution always occurs with the TM L-edge variation and the oxygen vacancy accompanies with the TM reduction to maintain the charge balance, we can indirectly probe the oxygen vacancy formation through TM L-edge and O K pre-edge.[49,53] To probe the change of electronic states depending on the particle position, the EELS spectra were acquired by a line scanning from surface to bulk ( $10~\mathrm{nm}$ from particle edge). Among TM L-edges, only Ni L-edge was investigated as Ni constitutes over $92\%$ in this LNCM system and acts as a main culprit for the degradation of Ni-rich LNCM. All Ni L-edge spectra showed two characteristic peaks or white lines $[\mathrm{L}_{3}$ and $\mathrm{L}_{2}$ , and the position of $\mathrm{L}_{3}$ -edge peak gradually shifted to a lower energy when moved from bulk to surface. The Ni L-edge peak recovered the bulk peak at 8 and $4\mathrm{nm}$ for frontal and lateral facets of U-LNCM (Figure  6b,c) and 6 and $3\mathrm{nm}$ for frontal and lateral facets of both P-LNCM (Figure 6e,f) and B-LNCM (Figure 6h,i), respectively. As the shift of $\mathrm{L}_{3}$ -edge peak to the lower energy implies the reduction of valence state, [47,48] the above results indicate that Ni ions at the surface are reduced with an anisotropic behavior depending on the particle orientation. Thus, Ni L-edge peak recovery was strongly dependent on the doping and facet orientation (frontal vs lateral) of the primary particles. Likewise, all O K spectra consisted of a sharp pre-edge peak at ${\approx}540~\mathrm{eV}$ and a broad main-edge peak at ${\approx}550\mathrm{~eV.~}$ The pre-edge peak intensity gradually decreased when moved from bulk to surface and the pre-edge peak became a shoulder of the main peak at the surface. Furthermore, the intensity reduction of O K preedge peak coincides with the position shift of $\mathrm{L}_{3}$ -edge peak in all the cycled electrodes. Therefore, the reduction of Ni valence state and concurrent lattice oxygen loss were smaller in the doped LNCM electrodes, and the lateral facets were less vulnerable to the oxygen loss compared to the frontal facets.  We performed the first-principles calculations to further understand the facet-dependent oxygen vacancy formation (see the Experimental Section). Figure  6j,k represents the lateral (003) and frontal (102) planes in $\mathrm{LiNiO}_{2}$ structure, respectively. Due to high Ni contents (over $90\%$ ), we adopted the $\mathrm{LiNiO}_{2}$ system, which shows the structural and compositional similarity to Ni-rich LNCM according to the previous reports.[24,25,54] The oxygen vacancy formation energy of each surface was calculated as a function of lithium concentration in $\mathrm{Li}_{x}\mathrm{NiO}_{2}$ ( $x=1$ , 0.66, 0.33). From the results (Figure 6l), the frontal (102) plane was calculated to have a lower oxygen vacancy formation energy compared to the lateral (003) plane in the whole range of lithium content, which indicates that the frontal (102) plane is more prone to release the oxygen gas. Furthermore, the oxygen vacancy formation energy monotonically decreased with increasing the lithium extraction. Especially, the frontal (102) plane showed the negative oxygen vacancy formation energy $(-0.13~\mathrm{eV})$ at $x=0.33$ , however, the lateral (003) plane still exhibited the positive oxygen vacancy formation energy value $(0.35~\mathrm{eV})$ . The lower oxygen vacancy formation energy of frontal (102) plane compared to lateral (003) plane is due to the presence of undercoordinated O atoms in (102) plane.[55] Those oxygens are highly unstable to evolve as oxygen gas with a lower oxygen vacancy formation energy. Overall, these calculations show the facet-dependent oxygen evolution and suggest the importance of the particle orientation.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The loss of oxygen in LNCM inevitably accompanies with the structure transformation from layered structure (R-3m) to spinel structure $(F d{\cdot}3m)$ and eventually to rock salt structure $(F m{-}3m)$ , and the phase transformation starts from the surface and migrates toward the inner bulk.[11,16] To confirm the phase transition, the outmost surfaces of the pristine and cycled electrodes were examined using atomic resolution STEM. After 50 cycles at 0.3C, the inner bulk of the LNCM electrodes maintained the original $R{\cdot}3m$ layered structure (Figure S11, Supporting Information), but SRL on the surface significantly propagated toward the inner bulk and exhibited the anisotropic behavior depending on the particle orientation (Figure 7b,d). The fast Fourier transform (FFT) patterns indicated that SRL on the surface was NiO-like rock salt $(F m{-}3m)$ phase. The thickness of SRL was estimated to be 8 and $3{-}4~\mathrm{nm}$ for frontal and lateral facets of U-LNCM and ${\approx}6$ and ${\approx}3$ nm for frontal and lateral facets of both P-LNCM and B-LNCM, respectively (Figure  7; Figure S12, Supporting Information). The obtained results were consistent with the depth of oxygen loss determined by EELS analysis, and thus, the propagation of SRL was influenced by the presence of dopant and/or facet orientation.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To support the EELS and STEM results, operando DEMS experiment was performed to monitor the gas evolution during charge process (Figure  8a–c). The $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ release was monitored by increasing the charging voltage up to $4,8\mathrm{~V~}$ at the current density of 0.1C. For all three cathodes, $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ started to evolve after the H2 phase and the gas evolution rate increased with raising the charge potential and reached its maximum at $4.8~\mathrm{V}.$ This is because the lattice oxygen becomes more vulnerable with increasing the lithium extraction from the crystalline structure (Figure  6l).[14] The accompanying $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ was resulted from the chemical oxidization of the electrolyte by the oxygen radicals that were released from the LNCM.[28,29] As expected, the amount of gases released decreased in the order of U-LNCM, P-LNCM, and B-LNCM. Thus, the B-LNCM electrode showed a minimal amount of $\mathrm{O}_{2}$ and $\mathrm{CO}_{2}$ evolution during charge process, demonstrating the high oxygen stability.  Based on the EELS, STEM, and DEMS analyses, it was found that the oxygen release and cation migration behavior were anisotropic and depended on the facet orientation of the primary particle, that is, the frontal facets have more vulnerable characteristics compared to lateral facets. As described earlier, the primary particles of B-LNCM were much thinner plates and thus, had more lateral facets and less frontal facets (in terms of the area) compared to P-LNCM. This implies that B-LNCM is more resistant to the oxygen loss and phase transformation during cycling, which results in the better cycle performance compared to P-LNCM (Figure 8d).  To prove the above hypothesis, the P-LNCM and B-LNCM electrodes were further examined by sXAS using a fluorescence yield (FY) mode (depth sensitivity of ${\approx}200\mathrm{nm}$ from the particle surface).[56] For the O K-edge sXAS spectra, the pre-edge peak (from 528 to $535~\mathrm{eV}$ ) arises from the excitation of $O_{1\mathrm{s}}$ electron to unoccupied states of hybridized $\mathrm{TM}_{3\mathrm{d}}{-}\mathrm{O}_{2\mathrm{p}}$ orbitals, while the broad peak above $535~\mathrm{eV}$ corresponds to the excitation to unoccupied $\mathrm{TM}_{4\mathrm{sp}}{-\mathrm{O}_{2\mathrm{p}}}$ hybridized orbitals (Figure  8e,f).[57–59] The pre-edge peak was mainly focused in this research as the 3d orbitals of TM contribute to the main redox activity in the Ni-rich layered cathodes. For the pristine electrodes, only lattice oxygen peak of layered cathodes was detected at $529.5~\mathrm{eV.}$ However, after multiple cycles, the intensity of layered lattice oxygen peak at $529.5~\mathrm{eV}$ decreased and a new peak at $533~\mathrm{eV}$ appeared in the cycled P-LNCM electrode, which can be attributed to the presence of NiO-type rock-salt (Figure  8e).[58,59] On the contrary, the depression of layered lattice oxygen peak was rather small and the peak at $533.2~\mathrm{eV}$ was hardly observed in the cycled B-LNCM electrode (Figure  8f), indicating that the B-LNCM electrode was more resistant against the phase transition during cycling. In case of Ni L-edge, due to the spin–orbit coupling of the core hole, Ni L-edge sXAS spectrum is divided into two separated energy bands, namely, $\mathrm{L}_{3}$ -edge $(\mathrm{Ni}~2\mathrm{p}_{3/2})$ at lower energy and $\mathrm{L}_{2}$ -edge (Ni $2{\tt p}_{1/2})$ at higher energy (Figure $_{8\mathrm{g,h}}$ ).[58] In addition, the $\mathrm{2p}\mathrm{-}3\mathrm{d}$ interaction and crystal field effect further split the $\mathrm{L}_{3}$ -edge into a low energy band $\left(\mathrm{L}_{3\mathrm{L}}\right)$ at ${\approx}855~\mathrm{eV}$ and a high energy band $(\mathrm{L}_{3\mathrm{H}})$ at ${\approx}856.5~\mathrm{eV}.$ In case of the P-LNCM electrode (Figure $8\mathrm{g}.$ ), compared to the pristine electrode, the $\mathrm{L}_{3\mathrm{L}}/\mathrm{L}_{3\mathrm{H}}$ ratio increased and the $\mathrm{L}_{2}$ -edge shifted to the lowed energy after cycling. This indicates an evolution of transition metal 3d bands to higher occupancies, that is, reduced oxidation states.[59] On the other hand, the Ni L-edge spectrum of the cycled B-LNCM electrode was almost identical to that of the pristine electrode, implying that the B-LNCM electrode effectively maintained its original Ni valence state and layered structure during cycling. Overall, the sXAS results showed that B-LNCM effectively suppressed the phase transformation during cycling compared to P-LNCM electrode. Therefore, by conducting the various measurements, this research has revealed three findings; 1) origin of excellent cyclability of B-LNCM to be suppression of microcrack and alleviation of oxygen evolution, 2) importance of facet development to minimize the oxygen release, and 3) significance of oxygen evolution control for the battery performance improvement.
