- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The results of investigation of the laser irradiation influence on PL properties of erbium in silicon matrix are presented in Figure $_{2\mathrm{a-c}}$ . It is clearly seen that the increase in the laser processing fluence leads to the growth of the amplitude both for PL and Raman signals (Figure 2a,b).  Figure  2a presents the Raman spectra, while Figure  2b shows an erbium PL at $1530~\mathrm{nm}$ corresponding to the transition $^4\mathrm{I}_{13/2}\rightarrow^{4}\mathrm{I}_{15/2}$ . The Raman spectra in Figure  2a showed the appearance of a peak at ${\approx}518~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ after processing, which corresponds to the transverse optical (TO) mode of lattice vibrations of crystalline silicon phase.[56] This indicates the formation of silicon crystalline grains in the film.[57] The reason for the deviation of the position from $520~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ corresponding to the TO vibrational mode of a bulk silicon crystal is associated with the finite small sizes of crystal grains in the silicon film after heating. This effect was experimentally[58]  and theoretically[59] studied. It is also worth noting that the absorption of laser radiation with subsequent heating of the material can influence the position of the peak.[60]  The increase in the PL signal can be explained by the rise of erbium concentration in silicon matrix (for lower exposure intensities), and a change in the type of the surrounding media (for higher exposure intensities). Indeed, erbium ions surrounded by oxygen atoms glow brighter than those in the environment of silicon.[51] In our experiments, the formation of an oxygen-rich (oxide) environment can be attributed to the oxidation process of silicon during laser exposure in the air atmosphere.[66] The reason for the subsequent decrease of the PL signal is mainly due to the evaporation of the material when the laser intensity exceeds the damage threshold marked by the right dashed line in Figure 2c. The Raman signal increase can be explained through laser-induced heating of silicon with its subsequent crystallization. However, the volume of the silicon crystal phase at high power densities decreases. This effect can be explained by the destruction of the silicon lattice caused by the reaction of silicon with the air at high temperatures and accompanied by oxygen absorption, additionally creating a matrix of $\alpha\mathrm{SiO}_{2}$ . In favor of this proposition, one can see a peculiarity of the PL spectrum curve, which has noticeable changes: the observed fine structure of the peaks is blurred, forming a single wide peak. Such a situation is typical for erbium luminescence in alkali-silicate glass matrices based on silicon oxide $\left[67\right]$ (see Figure 2b).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Figure  3 presents the results of the comprehensive characterization of spots on the surface of bilayer Er/Si film fabricated with different laser intensities and distance between laser affected zones. The difference between laser-affected zones and initial film surface can be easily detected both on SEM image (Figure 3a) and optical (Raman and PL) responses (Figure 3b,c). As can be seen from the SEM image (Figure 3a) at sufficiently high intensities, partial removal of the material occurs at the center of the irradiated area resulting in the donut-like shape. However, it cannot be seen from the PL mapping image represented in Figure 3c due to limited optical resolution. Thus from the analysis of these data, one can see that laser irradiation at fluences higher than $4.8\mathrm{~m~}$ J $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ of the bilayer $\mathrm{Er}/\mathrm{Si}$ film surface can provide Si crystallization and surface modification, but laser-assisted doping of silicon by erbium provide PL at fluence exceeding $5.5\mathrm{~m~}$ $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ . It is important that at fluences exceeding this threshold, the surface modification is distinctive. Therefore the label protection can be achieved through luminescent properties of an image or creation of visually similar but non-luminescent FaS in close proximity with LuS. However, the addition of such FaS can have a bad influence on Er photoluminescent properties in the silicon film. Indeed, erbium diffusion due to the heat created during the recording of neighboring spots can result in blurring and intensity decrease. In turn, this effect reduces the speed and reliability during the point-by-point reading of the final image. Therefore, it is necessary to reveal the minimum distance between points.  Figure 3d shows a PL map with different positions among laser affected areas, which is made to find how close the FaS can be located without distortion of the PL signal from LuS. In order to do it, LuS were recorded at $4.4\mathrm{-}6.6\mathrm{~m~}$ J $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ (see Figure  3d, left). After the erbium layer removing by etching in hydrochloric acid with subsequent distilled water rising, the FaS were fabricated at different distances from LuS, starting from $2\upmu\mathrm{m}$ with a step of $0.5~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ with the same laser fluence. From the Figure  3d (right image) one can see, that for the case of the distance between FaS and LuS lower than $2~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ the PL signal coming from LuS crosses the dashed line corresponding to an initial position of the signal on the PL map after LuS recording. This fact can indicate about erbium diffusion in silicon film during FaS formation. Finally, during this experiment, it was found that the optimal laser fluence to record a luminescent secure label equals to $6.6\mathrm{~m~}$ J $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ with a minimal distance between LuS and FaS of $2.5~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ . We utilize this distance for subsequent experiments to reduce the reading time and fabricate a compact security label. It should be noted that the distance between neighboring spots can be increased. We carry out point-by-point scanning of the label area by the exciting laser beam and the Er luminescent signal collection by a confocal spectrometer equipped with a CCD camera (see Experimental Section for more details). In this case, the rise of a distance between neighboring spots can influence on the QR code reading time and subsequent data processing.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The scheme of the security labels fabrication process, as well as the results of the proof of concept experiment, are presented in Figure  4. Local laser heating of a bilayer Er/Si film allows forming a luminescent label that can be read in the PL map. To demonstrate it, a standard QR-code with dimensions of $42\times42$ pixels (spots) was generated to encode the “ITMO University” line. In general, the size of luminescent spots is determined by optical resolution, which for a diffraction-limited system is a function of operating wavelength and objective numerical aperture. For our experimental conditions, it can be estimated as $1.4~{\upmu\mathrm{m}}$ $\lambda=800\mathrm{nm}$ , $\mathrm{NA}=0.7)$ ). This value can be reduced using shorter wavelengths (e.g., $405~\mathrm{nm}$ ) and objectives with higher NA. Another critical point is the spot readout. Since photoluminescence wavelength is $1530~\mathrm{nm}$ , it results in the detection resolution of $2.7~\upmu\mathrm{m}$ for $\mathrm{NA}=0.7.$ The density of spots comes from the need to prevent the erbium migration during to the label writing stage. Taking into account the determined distance between neighboring points $(2.5~{\upmu\mathrm{m}})$ , the density of pixels in our case is $0.16~\upmu\mathrm{m}^{-2}$ . The selected pattern was directly recorded on the surface of a sample in five steps.  The first step is the fabrication of a bilayer $\mathrm{Er}/\mathrm{Si}$ film (Figure  4a). The detailed description of the film fabrication processes is presented in the Experimental Section. At the second stage, an array of LuS was made through point-bypoint local laser exposure on a preliminarily determined pattern (Figure  4b). The exposure intensity was carefully selected to ensure the maximum PL signal as it was described in the discussion of Figure 3. This irradiation results in the formation of the desired image on the film surface, which optically looks like a series of holes on the film surface. This image can be used as a security label. Indeed, the label created in this way can be directly read by optical microscopy due to partial material removal during processing. Data protection, in this case, is achieved by the creation of a PL image at the wavelength of $1530~\mathrm{nm}$ during surface mapping by the beam of a green laser.  The second type of security label possesses a higher degree of data protection through the fabrication of FaS pattern near initial LuS, creating a pattern that looks like an array of holes in an optical or SEM microscope. Before recording an array of FaS, it is necessary to etch the bilayer film with a solution of hydrochloric acid, which actively reacts with erbium only in areas where laser irradiation was not applied and does not affect the silicon layer. During the incorporation of erbium in the silicon matrix, the creation of optically active complexes Er:O takes place predominantly.[65] Thus Er:O complexes in the silicon matrix are surrounded by silicon atoms. This feature limits the possible chemical effect on $\mathrm{Er}{\cdot}\mathrm{O}$ clusters from a $10\%$ aqueous HCl solution used in our experiments. Such a solution does not provide etching of silicon and silicon oxide layers as well as incorporated Er photoluminescence.[68] We have done experiments on treating the fabricated label surface with the $10\%$ HCl aqueous solution to check this idea. These experiments demonstrate that such treatment has no influence on the label photoluminescent properties (see Supporting Information, Section Chemical treatment). The etching products are eliminated by distilled water (see Figure 4c) Since the erbium layer was removed, spots recorded at the fourth step are non-luminescent but look like LuS (see Figure 4d and inset with AFM profile cross-section), therefore securing the stored QR-code from direct optical reading. The conducted AFM experiments demonstrate that the depths of the laser annealed spots are $97\pm13\mathrm{nm}$ (FaS) and $94\pm16~\mathrm{nm}$ (LuS); the widths of the spots are $494\pm100\mathrm{nm}$ (FaS) and $488\pm90$ nm (LuS) (for more details see Supporting Information. Security Label Description). Therefore they are indistinguishable in AFM scans.
