- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The fabrication of PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li anode is schematically shown in Figure 1a. Cationic PDDA–TFSI was prepared by anion exchange of hydrophilic $\mathrm{Cl^{-}}$ with hydrophobic $\mathrm{TFSI^{-}}$ by mixing PDDA–Cl with an equivalent mole of LiTFSI (Figure S1, Supporting Information).[16] A large PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li foil was formed by tape casting solution of PDDA–TFSI in anhydrous NMP on Li strip followed by solvent evaporation (Figure S2, Supporting Information). The morphology of the bare Li strip and PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The microscopic roughness of bare Li strip unavoidably causes uneven distribution of electrical fields at the electrode surface and consequently the inhomogeneous $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ flux during plating (Figure S3, Supporting Information). By contrast, the PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li exhibits a smooth and even surface, which is beneficial for homogeneous $\operatorname{Li^{+}}$ nucleation and distribution (Figure 1b). The PDDA–TFSI layer is around $3\upmu\mathrm{m}$ thick with evenly distributed C, N, F, and S elements (Figure  1c,d). The hydrophobic PDDA–TFSI coating adheres closely to Li strip to provide long-term protection and improve humid resistance. To visually demonstrate the water suppressing ability of the PDDA–TFSI layer, a drop of water was placed on the Li foil with or without PDDA–TFSI protection. The bare Li foil reacted vigorously and released a large amount of heat and gas as soon as contacting with water, leaving a black and destroyed surface (Figure S4 and Movie S1, Supporting Information). On the contrary, the water-droplet kept quite steady on PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li over $2\mathrm{min}$ , and then slowly percolated through the PDDA– TFSI layer without any violent reaction (Figure  1e; Movie S2, Supporting Information). This demonstrates that PDDA–TFSI layer can retard the diffusion of water molecules to Li surface and effectively suppress the humid attack.  The stability of the anodes in ambient air was also studied (Figure 1f ). After exposure to air with ${\approx}60\%$ RH, the color of Li strip changed within a few minutes and completely turned to black in $^{1\textrm{h}}$ . By contrast, PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li remained shiny and showed no obvious change in color or volume for $^{1\textrm{h}}$ , revealing that PDDA–TFSI can prevent gas permeation and stabilize Li anode in ambient air. The hydrophobic TFSI anions and the conformal coverage of polymer coating on the Li metal surface are the main reasons for the water suppressing capability of PDDA–TFSI layer. The contact angles of bare Cu and PDDA–TFSI@Cu with water were measured to provide evidence of the water-suppressing effect (Figure S5, Supporting Information). An increased contact angle of PDDA–TFSI $@\mathrm{Cu}$ with water $(72^{\circ})$ compared to that of bare Cu $(32^{\circ})$ indicates the enhanced hydrophobicity and better sealability of the PDDA–TFSI layer. Furthermore, the electrochemical stability was investigated by measuring linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) of PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li and Li anode in a carbonate-based electrolyte (Figure  1g). PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li shows very stable electrochemical window up to $5.28\mathrm{~V~}$ that is compatible with high-voltage NMC cells.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The plating morphology of Li metal on different electrodes was captured by SEM. Before cycling, the surface of Cu foil was quite bumpy (Figure S6, Supporting Information), causing inhomogeneous Li nucleation during Li plating. $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ ions tend to gather on the tips of the bumps with stronger electrical fields compared to the edges, leading to gradual formation of dendrites. As shown in Figure 2a,b, Li deposition on the bare Cu gave dendrite morphology with whisker-like cross-section structure after plating with a capacity of 3 mAh $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ . By contrast, with Li deposit on the PDDA–TFSI protected Cu foil, PIL top layer swelled in the electrolyte but kept even and intact after Li plating, and the middle layer of deposited Li was relatively dense, revealing homogenous Li deposition without dendrite structure (Figure 2c,d).  In situ optical microscopy characterization was also carried out to investigate the Li deposition morphology on Cu foil with and without PDDA–TFSI layer. As seen in Figure  2e, direct plating $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ on bare Cu led to an inhomogeneous Li nucleation and continuous growth of a large amount of Li whiskers for $30\mathrm{min}$ at a current density of $1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ . Quite different from the porous and loose Li deposition on the bare Cu electrode, a dense and dendrite-free Li layer was formed on the PDDA– $\mathrm{TFSI@Cu}$ electrode at the same current density (Figure  2f). It is obvious that whisker-like Li deposits are completely eliminated, which is consistent with the morphology observed by SEM.  We simulated current density distribution to understand the mechanisms of Li deposition in the presence of the polycationic layer using COMSOL multiphysics (Figure  2h–k). Starting with the same Li nuclei morphology, the introduction of the polycationic layer with the electrostatic shielding nature reduces the electric field, which is beneficial for stabilizing deposition of Li. The surface charge gradient is also alleviated by the ionic PDDA–TFSI layer. Especially, the valleys on the polycationic-film-coated Li have higher current density compared to the counterpart without PDDA–TFSI. This will lead to a more homogeneous incoming $\operatorname{Li^{+}}$ flux and a smoother Li deposition layer. The electrostatic shielding effect is schematically illustrated in Figure  2g. During the initial stage of deposition, Li plates on the electrode and unavoidably forms some protrusions due to system fluctuations. The protuberant tips or sharp edges display a stronger electrical field than the valleys, and $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ ions preferably deposit around the tip area.[17] This self-amplification behavior leads to the formation of dendritic Li. When the current collector is covered with a layer of polycationic polymer, the abundant positive charges even out the electric field. Although the diffusion rate of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ near the electrode is slower due to the reduced electric field, the distribution of incoming $\operatorname{Li^{+}}$ is more even compared to the bare electrode, as evidenced by the COMSOL simulation.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): We first studied chemical composition of the SEI layers formed on the bare Cu electrode and PIL-coated Cu electrodes using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). Electrolyte used in $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Cu}$ half cells was $1\mathrm{~M~}\mathrm{LiPF_{6}}$ in 1:1 v/v EC/DEC with $5\mathrm{~wt}\%$ FEC. In N 1s spectra, PIL-induced SEI layers show two peaks: the one at $401.7{-}402.3~\mathrm{eV}$ corresponding to $\mathrm{N^{+}}$ on the side chains of the PIL, and the other at $398.9\mathrm{-}399.1\mathrm{eV}$ attributed to $\mathrm{Li-N}$ bond in $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ (Figure 3a).[12a,19] $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ is a decomposed product of TFSI anion, and no $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ is observed in the SEI formed on the bare Cu due to the lack of TFSI anions. The accumulated TFSI anions in the polycationic film likely undergo reduction reaction and the decomposed $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ constitutes the SEI layer, which is also observed in the SEI layer formed in the electrolyte with IL additive.[12a] Similar redox reaction is reported lately of the polymer trapped $\mathrm{PF_{6}}$ anions.[20] $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ is detected in all PIL-induced SEI layers, indicating that all three types of PILs undergo a similar reaction. Using the elemental quantification derived from the survey scans together with fitting of the high-resolution N 1s spectra, the atomic concentration of $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ can be calculated (Tables S1 and S2, Supporting Information). For instance, a total atomic fraction of $3.19\%$ N is present on the surface of PDDA–TFSI induced SEI, $11.4\%$ of which is attributed to $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ . Therefore, the concentration of N from $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ in the PDDA–TFSI induced SEI is $0.36\%$ . Similarly, $0.41\%$ and $0.34\%$ of $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ are observed in poly(EVIm– TFSI) and PDMA–TFSI induced SEI layers, respectively. So the SEI layers induced by the three PILs are composed of a similar amount of $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ . In $\mathrm{F}$ 1s spectra, the PIL-coated electrodes do not differ much from the control sample, with LiF concentration of $6.78\%$ , $6.14\%$ , $5.35\%$ and $5.89\%$ on the surface of PDDA–TFSI $@\mathrm{Cu}$ , poly(EVIm–TFSI) $@\mathrm{Cu}$ , PDMA–TFSI $@\mathrm{Cu}$ and bare Cu, respectively (Figure 3b). The higher concentration of $\mathrm{C-F}$ on the PIL-coated electrodes is associated with the accumulated TFSI anions. From the above analysis, we conclude that those three PILs with positively charged nitrogen provide a similar effect of SEI layer formation. The presence of highly ionic conductive and insoluble $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ in the SEI layer facilitates fast $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transport, alleviates $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ deficiency on the anode surface, and assists the formation of stable SEI layer, which are vitally important for long-term cyclability of Li metal anode.  $\mathrm{Li}/\mathrm{Cu}$ cells paired with different Cu electrodes were fabricated to examine the efficiency of Li deposition. The PILcoated electrodes show similar Li nucleation overpotentials of 25, 26, and $24~\mathrm{mV}$ for PDDA–TFSI@Cu, poly(EVIm–TFSI) $@$ Cu and PDMA–TFSI $@$ Cu, respectively, much lower than that of the bare Cu electrode $(115~\mathrm{mV})$ ), revealing that the PIL layer can dramatically reduce the deposition barrier of $\operatorname{Li^{+}}$ on the electrode (Figure  3c). This could be originated from the existence of $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ in the PIL-induced SEI layer since highly conductive $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ can increase the surface mobility of $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ and promote the reduction step $(\mathrm{Li^{+}}+\mathrm{e}=\mathrm{Li})$ . CE and cycling stability of different electrodes are evaluated in Figure  3d. At a current density of $0.5\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ with an areal capacity of $1\mathrm{mAh~cm^{-2}}$ , the bare Cu electrode exhibits an initial CE of $95.1\%$ which keeps at $94.9\%$ for 60 cycles before the abrupt drop. The brittle SEI on the bare $\mathrm{Cu}$ electrode undergoes constant break and repair which consumes a large amount of electrolyte and Li source, leading to decreased CE as well as the growth of Li dendrites.  All PIL-coated Cu electrodes show improved CEs compared to the bare Cu electrode, with initial CEs of $95.7\%$ , $94.6\%$ , and $94.6\%$ , and average CEs of $96.9\%$ , $96.5\%$ , and $96.6\%$ for PDDA–TFSI $@$ Cu, poly(EVIm–TFSI) $@\mathrm{Cu}$ and PDMA–TFSI $@$ Cu, respectively. The relatively low initial CEs of the $\mathrm{PIL@Cu}$ electrodes are probably due to the consumption of Li metal to form $\mathrm{Li}_{3}\mathrm{N}$ in the SEI. The potential profiles at the $50\mathrm{th}$ and the 100th cycle show constant results in the discharge–charge reversibility. For the three PIL-coated Cu electrodes, the overpotentials are ${\approx}21~\mathrm{mV}$ at 50th cycle and then slowly increase to ${\approx}27~\mathrm{mV}$ at 100th cycle, lower than that of the bare Cu electrode ( $34~\mathrm{mV}$ at the 50th cycle) (Figure  3e,f). The result suggests that the three types of PILs have a similar protection effect on Li metal anode. PIL protective layer assists the formation of a stable SEI layer, unifies $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ distribution, and mitigates side reactions between electrolyte and Li anode, all of which contribute to the improved Li utilization and stability. We used PDDA–TFSI protected Li anode for the following electrochemical analysis due to a relatively simpler synthesis procedure.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The electrochemical performance of PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li was further investigated using symmetric Li/Li cells. At a current density of $1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ with an areal capacity of 1 mAh $\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li delivers cycling stability for over $1000\mathrm{~h~}$ (Figure 4a). Steady, flat, and smooth voltage profiles are observed from the enlarged pictures, with overpotential as low as ${\approx}23~\mathrm{mV}$ during the entire Li plating/stripping process, indicating a stable electrode/electrolyte interface and a fast $\mathrm{Li^{+}}$ transport to anode surface. Although Li/Li symmetric cell without polycationic layer exhibits similar voltage plateaus to that of PDDA–TFSI $\textcircled{a}\mathrm{Li}/$ PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li cell during the initial cycles, significantly higher overpotentials were observed at the beginning and end of each plating/stripping process (Figure  4b), which reveals the consumption of the previously unused fresh Li beneath the surface. Moreover, the overpotential of Li/Li symmetric cell increases dramatically after $200\mathrm{~h~}$ , manifesting the structural failure of electrode and the exhaustion of electrolyte.  The areal capacity of Li plating/stripping is substantially increased to $10\mathrm{mAh~cm}^{-2}$ , which is well above the target value for commercial Li-based batteries, i.e., $3\mathrm{mAhcm^{-2}}$ . PDDA– $\mathrm{TFSI@Li/PDDA}\mathrm{-TFSI@Li}$ still accomplishes long-term stability of over $700\mathrm{~h~}$ , greatly outperforming the bare Li anode (Figure 4c). Considering that the capacity of the $100\upmu\mathrm{m}$ thick Li strip is measured to be $19.39\mathrm{mAh~cm}^{-2}$ (Figure S9, Supporting Information), the utilization of Li anode, defined as the ratio of the reversible Li stripping/plating capacity to the Li anode capacity, is as high as $51.6\%$ . Cycling at an elevated current density of $5\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ , PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li exhibits much more stable and smaller voltage hysteresis compared to the bare Li anode (Figure  4d). Such a high areal capacity and long-term stability are among the best of the reported Li anodes cycled in carbonate-based electrolytes, where highly reactive solvents deteriorate anode stability especially at high areal capacity (Figure 4e). The superiority of the PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li for long-term stability at high areal capacity is derived from the synergistic effect of its electrostatic shielding nature to reduce the electrical field gradient and promote a uniform $\operatorname{Li^{+}}$ flux at the anode, as well as the formation of a stable SEI that provides fast $\operatorname{Li^{+}}$ transport channel.  Electrochemical impedance spectra (EIS) analysis was carried out to provide more insights into the interfacial stability of Li anodes. Figure S11 in the Supporting Information shows EIS curves and the corresponding equivalent circuit models before and after cycling at $1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ with an areal capacity of $1\mathrm{mAh~cm}^{-2}$ for $100~\mathrm{h}$ . The lower values of various resistances relating to ion transport in bulk electrolyte $\left(R_{\mathrm{o}}\right)$ , surface film $(R_{\mathrm{sf}})$ , and charge transfer $(R_{\mathrm{ct}})$ were observed for the PDDA– $\mathrm{TFSI}(\varpi\mathrm{Li}$ cell both before and after cycling (Table S3, Supporting Information).[21] The results evidently support that the PDDA–TFSI layer could assist the formation of a stable SEI, prevent side reactions between Li metal anode and electrolyte, and suppress the exhaustion of electrolyte and the accumulation of “dead Li” that loses electrical contact with the substrate and becomes electronically inactive. The distinct morphology of Li anodes was investigated by SEM after $100\mathrm{~h~}$ of cycling at $1\mathrm{mA}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ and $1\mathrm{mAh~cm^{-2}}$ . The bare Li exhibits highly porous and dendrite morphology, implying poor SEI stability (Figure  3f). A thick layer of “dead $\operatorname{Li}^{p}$ is also observed from the side-view, which significantly impedes $\operatorname{Li^{+}}$ transport and increases internal resistance (Figure $4g)$ . By sharp contrast, the surface of $\mathrm{PIL@Li}$ remains smooth and compact without visible “dead Li,” which verifies the advantages of PIL to stabilize SEI and enable dendrite-free Li anode (Figure  4h,i). In addition, even after cycling for $200{\mathrm h}$ , the PDDA–TFSI layer can keep structural integrity (Figure S12, Supporting Information). The presence of N and S elements confirms the existence of the PDDA–TFSI film.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Gas generation as a result of parasitic reactions between electrolyte and Li anode is one of major issues of high-energy LMBs.[25] To investigate the gassing behavior of LMBs, we carried out ultrasonic transmission mapping using the ultrasonic imaging technique developed by our group.[26] LFP/Li pouch cell was used for scanning since LFP cathode is usually very stable with no gas generation. The gassing mainly arises from the decomposition of electrolyte on Li anode, and it can be observed from the change in ultrasonic transmissivity. The large difference in impedance between gas and solid/liquid phase causes a large reflection and thus significantly reduces ultrasonic transmissivity. High-to-low transmissivity is transformed to a red-to-blue heat map, where the low transmissivity, blue color, indicates gas generation. As shown in Figure 5a, in pouch cell containing bare Li anode, sporadic gas bubbles start to appear at $25\mathrm{th}$ cycle and transmission drops to almost zero for the majority of the electrode at 45th cycle, indicating side reactions accompanied by gassing. By contrast, the pouch cell containing PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li anode shows no sign of gas generation at 45th cycle, confirming the suppressed gassing behavior due to stable SEI and homogeneous Li deposition (Figure 5b).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To demonstrate the potential of the PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li in practical LMBs, the electrochemical performances of Li/NMC811 and Li/LFP cells were investigated. As expected, PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li demonstrates better cycling performance than the bare Li anode. Specifically, PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li/NMC811 cell cycled at 0.5C exhibits almost unchanged voltage hysteresis during 200 cycles compared to the massively increased hysteresis for the bare Li cell (Figure 6a). PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li/NMC811 cell maintains $90.1\%$ of the initial capacity after 200 cycles, better than $74.9\%$ of the bare Li cell (Figure  6b). Moreover, since the PDDA–TFSI layer reduces the internal resistance, enhanced rate performance is observed with reversible capacities of 192.6, 182.0, 167.1, 154.9, 144.5, 136.0, and $115.5\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ under current densities of 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 8C, respectively, superior to the performance of the bare Li cell which only delivers a specific capacity of $48.2\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ at 8C (Figure  6c). When paired with high-loading LFP cathode ( $20\mathrm{mg}\mathrm{cm}^{-2},$ ), PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li maintains excellent stability with $97.5\%$ capacity retention after  200 cycles and stable rate capacity of 141.2, 137.0, 124.2, and $91.8~\mathrm{mAh}$ ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ at 0.2, 0.5, 1, and 2C, respectively (Figure  6d–f). On the contrary, bare Li cell suffers rapid capacity decay when cycled at $0.5\mathrm{C}$ and delivers a much lower specific capacity of 67.6 mAh ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ at 2C. The PDDA–TFSI $@$ Li accomplishes lower voltage hysteresis compared to bare Li for both NMC and LFP full cells, especially at high current densities, which is attributed to the stable SEI and fast $\operatorname{Li^{+}}$ transport pathway provided by the PDDA–TFSI layer (Figures S13 and S14, Supporting Information).  Constructing air-stable Li anodes that can be easily handled in ambient air is highly desirable for improving safety and reducing cost of LMBs.[27] The air/water-stability of the PDDA–TFSI $@\mathrm{Li}$ anode was tested by galvanostatic charging/ discharging process. After exposure to air $60\%$ RH) for $^{1\mathrm{h}}$ , the PDDA–TFSI $@\mathrm{Li}$ anode delivers the unaffected performance of 143.3 and 136.7 mAh ${\boldsymbol{\mathrm{g}}}^{-1}$ at 0.2 and 0.5C, respectively, with steady voltage profiles and capacity retention of $132.1\mathrm{mAhg}^{-1}$ after 100 cycles (Figure  6g; Figure S15, Supporting Information). Moreover, the water-treated PDDA–TFSI $@\mathrm{Li}$ after drying still shows low and steady hysteresis and long-term stability of $300~\mathrm{h}$ at an areal capacity of $3\mathrm{mAh}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ (Figure 6h), whereas the bare Li could not run at all. These results provide solid evidence that the hydrophobic PDDA–TFSI protective layer suppresses the corrosion of Li metal by air and water, which offers the possibility of assembling LMBs without the extremely inert atmosphere.
