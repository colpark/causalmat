- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The presence of Ce layer on the surface of aluminium nanoparticles was studied through EDS analysis. The results obtained from EDS analysis are given in Fig. 1 and Table 3. According to Table 3, it can be seen that more than $0 . 5 \%$ w/w Ce element was detected on the surface of all Ce-Al samples indicating the Ce film deposition. As clear in Fig. 1, with increasing the pH of the modification solution, the Ce content precipitated on the surface of the nanoparticles increased. The greatest amount of Ce was observed on the surface of the samples modified in a solution with pH of 3.8. When the pH reached 4.2, the Ce content on the samples reduced slightly. Hence, a pH of 3.8 was considered as the best condition for surface modification of aluminium nanoparticles.  EDS analysis can only reveal the presence of elements on the surface of the particles but the bonds formed on the surface cannot be studied by this technique. Therefore, the chemical composition and different states of cerium oxides/hydroxides were characterized by XPS analysis.  The chemical composition of Ce-Al samples has been studied by XPS analysis. The overall XPS survey spectrum of Ce-Al samples (Fig. 2a) showed C 1s, O 1s, Ce 3d and Al 2p as the main peaks. The results of high resolution analysis are given in Fig. 2b, c and Table 4. High resolution Al 2p scan was fitted into two Gaussian–Lorentzian peaks of 75.41 and 78.17 eV associated with the presence of aluminium oxide and aluminium metal on the surface of the sample, respectively (Fig. 2b) [44–47]. The oxygen core lines are more sensitive to chemical changes. Thus, more information about the state of Ce oxide/hydroxide layer was obtained from the O1s high resolution spectrum, which is shown in Fig. 2c. Here, a clear distinction could be made between cerium oxides, cerium hydroxides and aluminium oxide. Cerium oxide in the structure was characterised by two binding energies at 529.06 and $5 2 9 . 9 7 \mathrm { e V }$ associated to $\mathsf { C e O } _ { 2 }$ and ${ \mathrm { C e } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ , respectively. Hydroxyl groups, Ce-OH, which were presented on the surface of nanoparticles exhibited binding energies around $5 3 2 . 0 2 \mathrm { e V }$ , while aluminium oxides on the surface showed binding energies around $5 3 0 . 9 9 \mathrm { e V }$ [44–46]. This result is in good agreement with EDS test results indicating that cerium is available in small quantities on the surface of aluminum nano-particles. In particular, the contribution of cerium oxides in the O 1 s line is much less intense than cerium hydroxides and occurs at lower binding energies (Table 4). In addition, the intensity ratio of Ce-O/Al-O is about 35.6:34.1. All of these features indicate that the Ce layer precipitated on the nanoparticles surface was not homogeneous and nanoparticles were partly coated with cerium oxides/hydroxides. Considering figures in Table 4, it can be found that various forms of cerium compounds in order of an increase in signal intensity are Ce-OH, ${ \mathrm { C e } } _ { 2 } { \mathrm { O } } _ { 3 }$ and $\mathrm { C e O } _ { 2 }$ .

As a result, the amino silane compounds were self-polymerized in the bulk solution rather than being grafted on the nanoparticles surface. This resulted in the smallest mass loss of Si-Al-3 sample among the Si-modified compounds. Likewise, Si-Al-1 reacted with a smaller share of APTES during modification and had the second smallest mass loss among other samples during thermal decomposition.

Hansen solubility parameters of these two solvents are given in Table 1.  As mentioned previously, the selected solvents have almost similar non-polar and hydrogen parameters but different polar parameters. The sedimentation rates of the modified and unmodified samples in each of the solvents were evaluated by turbidity test method. The results are displayed in Figs. 6 and 7. The less NTU changes over $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ period indicate that particles are stable in the desired solvent [55].  It was expected that after cerium modification, the surface polarity of the Ce-Al nanoparticles would not be changed significantly compared to the Al samples. That is why the test was done with two solvents having different polarity parameters to verify the above prediction. As can be seen in Fig. 7, both Ce-Al and Al particles have hydrophilic surface structure, which is evident from their good stability in polar solvent of methyl ethyl ketone.  Compatibility of Si-Al, Si-Ce-Al and Al nanoparticles in methyl ethyl ketone and butyl acetate was also assessed using turbidity test and the results are also given in Figs. 6 and 7. The presence of hydroxyl groups on the surface of unmodified Al particle is responsible for its polar nature. However, surface modification of nanoparticles with APTES altered the nature of the surface from polar to non-polar. Comparing Al curve with that of Si-modified samples in Fig. 7, it can be concluded that the Al nanoparticles in methyl ethyl ketone exhibited better compatibility and lower deposition rate compared to the modified particles. The solution remained opaque even after $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . But for the modified nanoparticles the deposition rate was higher. This conclusion is in agreement with the results obtained from FTIR analysis. The silane compound, which reacted with the surface of the nanoparticles, led to an increase in hydrophobic nature of the surface. Deposition behaviour of nanoparticles in butyl acetate showed that Al nanoparticles deposited quickly in the solvent leaving a transparent solution at the top. As for the Si-Al and Si-Ce-Al nanoparticles, they were well dispersed in butyl acetate and the solution remained opaque after $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ of test period.  Comparing Al, Si-Al and Si-Ce-Al nanoparticles, lower changes and higher NTU values were recorded for the Al particles dispersed in methyl ethyl ketone over $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ . In contrast, a significant drop of NTU was observed for the Si-Al and Si-Ce-Al samples and after $6 0 \mathrm { { m i n } }$ the NTU value of these samples was much less than that of the unmodified Al particles. These results suggest that the unmodified nanoparticles possess better compatibility in methyl ethyl ketone than the silanized samples. Methyl ethyl ketone has a higher polar solubility parameter than butyl acetate. Hence, the Al particles having more polar structure were more stable in MEK than Si-Al and Si-Ce-Als with the nonpolar surface nature. Fig. 6 reflects the higher stability of the Si-Al and Si-Ce-Al nanoparticles in butyl acetate compared to the Al particles.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The efficiency of cerium oxides/hydroxides and amino silane coating as corrosion inhibitors for aluminium nanoparticles was evaluated by a gas volumetric technique during $1 5 \mathrm { h }$ exposure to an acidic solution with $\mathrm { p H } = 1 . 0$ (adjusted with nitric acid). The results obtained from this study are shown in Fig. 8, which indicate that cerium and silane layers inhibited the corrosion process of aluminium nanoparticles by increasing the latency period of all modified samples far beyond that of the unmodified ones. Considering Fig. 8(a), it can be understood that the pH of the modification solution did not affect the volume of evolved hydrogen gas significantly, thus the inhibition efficiency obtained for Ce-Al samples cannot be assumed as pH dependent. However, particles, which were modified in a solution containing cerium ions with $\mathrm { p H } = 3 . 8$ , had the lowest volume of evolved hydrogen (Fig. 8(a)). Table 6 shows the inhibition efficiency of the modified nanoparticles.  During surface modification of nanoparticle by silane coupling agents, the crosslinking between silane molecules is favoured to obtain a dense Si O Si network. It is assumed that a dense layer of silane would be able to restrict the access of aggressive species to the underlying aluminium nanoparticle surface. In terms of silanized samples, during the first $1 4 0 \mathrm { m i n }$ of the testing period, no evolution of hydrogen was observed (Fig. 8(b)). After this period, the corrosion reactions initiated with a considerable rate. For SiAl-2 sample, a longer latency period was observed than for other samples, but after initiation of the corrosion reaction the rates for Si-Al-1, Si-Al-2 and Si-Ce-Al samples were approximately the same (Fig. 8(b)). The Si-Al-3 sample had the highest evolved hydrogen volume after the unmodified particles. Furthermore, Fig. 8(c) shows the hydrogen evolution of best samples in each category of Ce-Al, Si-Al and Si-Ce-Al nanoparticles. According to Fig. 8(c), the Si-Al-2 sample showed the greatest corrosion resistance in acidic environment but the longest latency period was observed for the $\mathrm { C e } { \mathrm { - } } \mathrm { A l / p H } = 3 . 8$ . This can be attributed to the better barrier effect that cerium layer provides for the nanoparticles, which limited the accessibility of corrosive species to the underlying aluminium surface. However, it seems that the gassing reaction was capable of being initiated at even the very few uncoated sites on the $\mathrm { C e - A l / p H } = 3 . 8$ sample. When it comes to Si-modified samples, i.e. Si-Al-2 and Si-Ce-Al the nanoparticles were oxidized more rapidly resulting in white powder of oxidized aluminium particles deposited at the bottom of the burette.  One of the most important features of the chemistry and structure of silanols that should be taken into account is the high acidity of silanol group, which means that they tend to form strong hydrogen bonds both to themselves and to organic molecules containing suitable hydrogen bonding sites. So, the less volume of evolved hydrogen gas from Si-Al-2 and Si-Ce-Al samples may not be for the better corrosion protection that silane coupling agents provided, but for the hydrogen molecules being trapped and/or consumed by hydrogen bonding sites, e.g. $\mathsf { N H } _ { 2 }$ and OH groups, on the samples.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Epoxy coatings filled with 1, 2 and $3 \%$ of modified and unmodified aluminium nanoparticles were applied on the steel substrates to provide protection against corrosion. After $3 6 0 \mathrm { h }$ salt spray test, the coating was peeled from steel surface. According to Fig. 10(a), after removing the coating from the surface of the substrate, there were widespread corrosion products on the surface after the salt spray test. This demonstrates the extent of electrolyte penetration through the coating. Film destruction and the formation of corrosion products for blank sample are greater than the coatings containing unmodified and modified nanoparticles, so that after $3 6 0 \mathrm { h }$ of exposure to salt spray chamber, it has been completely destroyed.  For the samples containing Ce-Al particles, from the trend of corrosion products expansion, it can be said that the electrolyte was able to penetrate easily into the coating, thus causing coating detachment from the metal surface (Fig. 10(b)). This could be due to the accumulation of nanoparticles in the coating, the presence of which causes voids and empty spaces that provide a path in the coating for corrosive species. However, coatings containing Ce-Al particles showed higher degree of protection against corrosion compared to the samples with Al nanoparticles. Cerium can reduce the rate of degradation and dissolution of aluminum by two mechanisms. The first mechanism is by blocking the corrosive species and acting as a physical barrier. The second one is by acting as an active inhibitor. Compounds containing $\mathsf { C e } ( \mathrm { I I I } ) / \mathsf { C e } ( \mathrm { I V } )$ are metastable, meaning that in the presence of $\mathrm { C l ^ { - } }$ ions, they can convert to each other through redox reactions. In this way, at the intersection of aluminum/cerium coating, where cracks exist, they are able to form Ce O Al bonds [56,57].  A much extended corroded site was observed for the M1 and M3 nanocomposites, reflecting high permeation of water and aggressive species through the coating film to the steel surface (Fig. 10(b)). This high permeation of electrolyte into the M3/epoxy sample may be due to the poorly dispersed Ce-Al nanoparticles in the polymeric coating matrix. While for the M1/epoxy specimen, this feature can be attributed to the inadequate loading of nanoparticles. When the free volume, which can be attributed to the presence of aggregations, increases in the coating film, the permeation would be increased and thus the corrosion inhibitive species will be depleted more rapidly. So, the less damaged sites was observed for the M2 nanocomposite can be attributed to the less aggregation of nanoparticles in the coating providing effective barrier performance.  For the S2 and S4 samples, the surface beneath the coating was almost undamaged and bright, but for the latter a slight brownish appearance was also noticed upon visual observation, as compared to S1, S3 and blank samples (Fig. 10(c) and (d)). Salt spray examination of the Ce-Al epoxy composite coatings showed more damaged sites on the surface, while Si-Al containing coatings showed fewer corroded sites, as shown in Fig. 10(b) and (c).  For an effective barrier against corrosion, a well dispersion of nanoparticles in the epoxy coating is necessary to reduce the penetration of corrosive species. For this purpose, strong interactions should be established between nanoparticles and epoxy resin. This strong interaction can be achieved by a reaction between $\mathsf { N H } _ { 2 }$ terminal groups in the siloxane chains and epoxy functional groups. However, considering turbidity test results, the unmodified and cerium modified particles did not exhibit good compatibility with butyl acetate, so they might lack the proper interaction with epoxy matrix as well. The small amount of corrosion products, which are present on the surface reflect negligible coating defects and the excellent film compactness. This would suggest a well dispersion of nanoparticles through the coating film, probably due to an active interaction of $\mathsf { N H } _ { 2 }$ groups on the aluminium nanoparticles with the epoxy matrix.

EIS measurements  Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were performed to characterize the corrosion protection properties of the coatings prepared from unmodified and modified aluminium nanoparticles. Epoxy coatings were prepared using 1, 2 and 3 wt.% of Al and Ce-Al nanoparticles and $2 \mathrm { w t } \%$ of Si-Al and Si-Ce-Al nanoparticles. Representative Bode plots are shown in Fig. 11.  Electrochemical characterization of the Ce-Al containing coatings showed that the corrosion protection degree increased with increasing the concentration of Ce-Al particles in the coating.  Generally, Ce-Al containing coatings showed better corrosion resistance than the Al containing coatings. In fact, surface treatment of Al nanoparticles via Ce film reduces their electrochemical activity in exposure to alkaline condition. In other words, the Ce film shows very high resistance against alkaline condition that can be provided during cathodic reactions on the steel surface as a result of $\mathrm { O H ^ { - } }$ ions creation. This reduces the Al nanoparticle dissolution rate in alkaline condition and the particles keep their barrier performance at longer exposure times but for the unmodified particles this feature cannot be seen.  Sample M2 exhibited the highest impedance $( \sim 1 0 ^ { 7 } \ : \mathrm { o h m } \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ ) values when compared to the samples containing Al and Ce-Al nanoparticles (with impedance values lower than $1 0 ^ { 6 } 0 \mathrm { h m } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ ), probably due to the adequate load of nanoparticles and less intercoating voids in these samples. This can be attributed to the fact that the nanoparticles were better dispersed in sample M2 and more aggregated in other samples. The lower impedance value at the lowest frequency observed after 57 days of immersion in $3 . 5 \%$  NaCl solution for sample M1 $( \sim 1 0 ^ { 5 } \ : \mathrm { o h m } \ : \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ ) can be attributed to the insufficient load of nanoparticles in the polymeric matrix, indicating the short term barrier protection provided by this coating.  When evaluating the electrochemical characteristics of the SiAl and Si-Ce-Al containing samples, high impedance values were obtained (higher than $1 0 ^ { 1 0 } \mathrm { o h m } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ ) for S2 and S4 samples, and only one time constant can be observed after 57 days exposure to $3 . 5 \mathrm { w t } . \% \mathrm { N a C l }$ solution. This very capacitive behaviour can be explained by a very low number of porosities presented in the coatings. The APTES grafted on the surface of nanoparticles could also contribute to the isolation of nanoparticles as a protective barrier which reduces the corrosion rate of aluminium particles as well as the coating porosity. This result is quite different from those obtained for Ce-Al containing samples. In the latter case, low impedance values were obtained (lower than $1 0 ^ { 6 } 0 \mathrm { h m } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ ) and two time constants were observed indicating that the coating delamination from the surface of the steel substrate was initiated. Therefore, it seems that the Ce-Al nanoparticles do not increase the corrosion resistance in the coating because most of them are not in a well dispersed form.  For the blank samples, two time constants could be clearly observed after 57 days of immersion, one in the high frequency range and a second one at lower frequencies and impedance values were around $1 0 ^ { 4 } 0 \mathrm { h m } \mathrm { c m } ^ { 2 }$ . The high impedance values of the blank samples $( 1 0 ^ { 8 } \thinspace \mathrm { o h m c m } ^ { 2 }$ ) in first days of immersion could be related to the epoxy coating barrier property. These results are similar to those obtained from the coatings reinforced with unmodified nanoparticles, which showed two loops at the 57th days of the exposure.
