- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): A lotus stem is mainly made up by the pith protected by the cortex (Figure 1a,b). While the former is in the form of vascular bundles that transports photosynthesis products from leaves to other organs and brings water and salts from underground, the latter is composed of layered arranged cells that not only strengthens and protects the former but also assists its transportation function (Figure 1d). A leaf is largely composed of mesophyll tissues that produce organics utilizing photosynthesis (Figure 1b,c). The biomass materials contain a decent amount of naturally embedded alkali or alkaline earth elements at the molecular level, which could activate the biomass during carbonization and create porosity in the resulting carbon. Since the stems are the path for water and ions from underground to the leaves, the content of carbohydrate and protein should be higher in leaves while the ion content should be higher in stems. To determine the amount of metal ions in the organs, the freeze-dried organs are thus dispersed in aqua regia at $80~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ to leach the metals. Indeed, the inductively coupled plasma (ICP) measurement of selected eight common metals shows the overall metal content is higher in the stems and K is dominant (Figure 1e; Table S1, Supporting Information).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): To turn the biomass into porous carbon, high temperature heating is usually used. The lotus stems and leaves are washed, freeze-dried, and then treated at $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ under $\Nu_{2}$ atmosphere for $3\mathrm{~h~}$ followed by washing. The obtained carbon materials from stems and leaves are thus named as S-carbon and L-carbon, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images show both carbon materials principally inherit the morphology of the organs, which is not unexpected since pyrolysis primarily generates micropores (Figure 2a,c). It is interesting to note that the transportation channels between the organ flakes are preserved after carbonization, providing an interconnected continuous pore network for gas and electrolyte. Under transmission electron microscopy (TEM), micropores and highly disordered texture in the flakes are observed (Figure 2b,d). The amorphous carbon-like nature of the obtained carbon is further confirmed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and Raman spectroscopy. Two broad peaks around $2\theta=24^{\circ}$ and $44^{\circ}$ corresponding to the (002) and (101) plane of graphite indicate the amorphous characteristics for both carbon materials (Figure 2e). The high $I_{\mathrm{D}}{:}I_{\mathrm{G}}$ ratios from the Raman peaks around $1340~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ (D band, associated with defect) and $1585~\mathrm{cm}^{-1}$ (G band, associated with graphitic) agree with the XRD results (Figure 2e). X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) spectra show that the L-carbon and S-carbon are dominantly composed of carbon and oxygen (Figure $2\mathrm{g};$ Figure S1, Supporting Information). As a result, the obtained amorphous S-carbon and L-carbon have not much difference in the chemical composition and degree of graphitization.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): However, significant difference is observed in the surface area of two carbon materials: the S-carbon presents a specific surface area of $1610~\mathrm{m}^{2}~\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , which is much higher than $1039~\mathrm{m}^{2}~\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ for the L-carbon calculated by the Brunauer– Emmett–Teller (BET) method. Figure 3a shows the $\Nu_{2}$ adsorption–desorption isotherms of the S-carbon and L-carbon, and the derived pore size distribution calculated using density functional theory (DFT) method on the adsorption isotherm is presented in Figure 3b. A bimodal distribution of micropores around $0.9\mathrm{nm}$ and mesopores around $3.5~\mathrm{nm}$ is observed for both carbon materials. Why would the carbon materials from different organs of a plant have apparently different surface area? It is generally believed the naturally contained alkalis in the biomass could very effectively activate the biomass during carbonization and create porosity in the resulted carbon.[33,36–41] In this case, the K content is ${\approx}2.5\mathrm{wt}\%$ in the freeze-dried stems, and $0.9\mathrm{wt}\%$ in the leaves, respectively (Figure 1e). Compared to a conventional chemical activation process in which a large amount of etching agents are mechanically mixed with or diffused into the carbon precursors, the relatively low content of alkalis in the biomass are homogeneously distributed at the molecular level, leading to much higher efficiency of activation. Thus it is speculated that the difference in the specific surface area of S-carbon and L-carbon is due to the discrepancy in the alkalis content in the organs. To elucidate this, the freeze-dried organs are washed with $1\mathrm{~M~HCl~}$ solution to remove the metals before carbonization. ICP measurement confirms that the metals are largely removed (Table S2, Supporting Information). The derived carbons from washed stems and leaves are thus named as WS-carbon and WL-carbon. Both carbon materials have specific surface area around $800{-}900~\mathrm{m}^{2}~\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ and the mesopores in both carbons are almost eliminated. Interestingly, the WS-carbon and WL-carbon have almost identical $\Nu_{2}$ adsorption–desorption isotherms and the derived pore size distribution. The slightly higher metal content in washed leaves could contribute to the higher surface area of WL-carbon than WS-carbon.  In the biomass carbonization process, the specific surface area of the resulted carbon varies significantly with the biomass precursors, depending on their plant cells. With respect to lotus, a specific surface area of over $800\mathrm{m}^{2}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ can still be achieved even from $1\mathrm{~\boldmath~\cal~M~}$ HCl-washed stems and leaves. Undoubtedly, the freeze-dried organs and the HCl-washed organs before carbonization all have negligible surface area (Figure S2, Supporting Information). What happens during the carbonization process of lotus organs? To shed light on this, thermal analysis is performed on the dried stems. Figure 3c shows the thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential thermogravimetry (DTG) curves of the stems and HCl-washed stems. The weight loss below $200~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ is mainly attributed to the removal of remaining water in the cells. Between 200 and $500~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , it is the decomposition of carbohydrates, proteins, lipids, cellulose, alkali containing organics, etc. that occurs.[36] Beyond $500~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ is the densification/graphitization of carbonaceous matter along with the elimination of loosely bonded carbon atoms and heteroatoms such as oxygen and nitrogen. Comparing the DTG curves of unwashed and HCl-washed stems, obviously, a few reactions between 200 and $500~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and one around $600~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ disappear for the washed sample. This can be explained by the leaching of acid-soluble organic matter in the stem including the alkali compounds, which could decompose between 200 and $500~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and also etch carbon around $600~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ It is interesting to note that the pyrolysis yield of the stems is about $13\%$ higher than that of the washed stems (W-stem) at $500~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , likely due to the involatile nature of the decomposed alkali compounds that stay in the pyrolyzed product. Considering there is ${\approx}3.5\%$ of metals in the stems, the corresponding salt content is ${\approx}7\%$ (using KCl as an example), which is consistent with the difference in pyrolysis yield. Although $62.2\%$ weight loss takes place before $500~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ and $7\%$ weight loss occurs between 500 and $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for the stems, the specific surface area of the S-carbon is only $\approx220\mathrm{m}^{2}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ when pyrolyzed at $500~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , and the specific surface area significantly increases when the pyrolysis temperature rises to $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (Figure 3d). This illustrates that the increase in surface area is largely correlated to the process of carbonaceous matter densification and leaving of loosely bonded carbon atoms and heteroatoms. Between 500 and $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ , concurrently, the activation/ etching happens. The $2.2\%$ high in the weight loss of the stems than in washed stems should be induced by the salt etching in stems. This may thus explain the high etching efficiency of homogeneous embedded alkalis at low content. As a result, the highly effective embedded salt activation is the cause for large surface area difference between washed and unwashed stems pyrolyzed at $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{~}}$ and also between S-carbon and L-carbon pyrolyzed at different temperatures (Figure 3d) and even when withered (Figure S3, Supporting Information).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): The electrochemical capacitive behavior of the S-carbon pyrolyzed at $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ is evaluated in $6\mathrm{~M~}\mathrm{~KOH}$ aqueous electrolyte with a two-electrode system. Almost rectangular cyclic voltammograms (CV) are presented at a scan rate from 5 to $500\mathrm{mV}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ without any redox peak (Figure 4a), and no plateau in galvanostatic charge–discharge curves at different current rates (Figure 4b), indicating the electrochemical double layer capacitive behavior and pseudocapacitance from the surface oxygen functional groups. The specific capacitance at a scan rate of $5\mathrm{mV}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ calculated from the typical CV curve shown in Figure 4a is $174\mathrm{Fg^{-1}}$ . Charge–discharge measurement and CV test in three-electrode system give similar results (Figures S4 and S5, Supporting Information). Due to the 3D interconnected porous structure, the S-carbon can deliver $72\%$ of the capacitance even at a high scan rate of $500\mathrm{mV}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ (Figure 4c). The S-carbon also exhibits high cycling performance, having no capacitance fading after $10~000$ charge–discharge cycles at $1\mathrm{~A~}\mathrm{~g~}^{-1}$ (Figure 4d). The L-carbon has similar capacitive behavior and cycling stability as the S-carbon (Figure S6, Supporting Information), but due to its lower specific surface, the L-carbon has lower specific capacitance than the S-carbon (Figure 4c). The rate performance of L-carbon is also worse. At $500\mathrm{mV}\mathrm{s}^{-1}$ , the capacitance retention of the L-carbon is $64\%$ . It is well accepted in porous carbon materials that only a portion of the pore surface is utilized for ion adsorption/desorption during electrochemical charge/discharge.[46–49] At higher charge–discharge rate, mesopores and macropores are more useful for ion adsorption/desorption. Analyzing the pore structure based on the $\Nu_{2}$ adsorption–desorption isotherms reveals that the mesopore and macropore volume for the S-carbon is only slightly higher than that of L-carbon pyrolyzed at $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ (Figure S7, Supporting Information), which is consistent with the moderately increased rate performance of the S-carbon. The areal capacitances of S-carbon and L-carbon are 10.8 and $14.6~\upmu\mathrm{F~cm}^{-2}$ , respectively, at a scan rate of $5\mathrm{mV~s}^{-1}$ . It is suspected that pseudocapacitance from the surface oxygen functional groups contributes to the overall capacitance, although its ratio is not easily distinguished. Indeed, the S-carbon pyrolyzed at $900^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ with less oxygen content could only deliver $66\%$ of the capacitance of the S-carbon pyrolyzed at $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}_{\mathrm{~\iota~}}$ although they have similar surface area (Figure S8, Supporting Information).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): For other plants with wide disparity morphology in the stems and leaves, the difference in microstructure and especially in porosity of the pyrolyzed carbon should also be observed as in the lotus. Indeed, the amount of metal ions in the celery and asparagus lettuce stems are higher than in their leaves, as confirmed by ICP, and the corresponding carbon from celery and asparagus lettuce stems have higher surface area carbon than those from the leaves (Figures 5a–d and 6a–d). The relatively high content of metal ions and especially K in the celery stem (Figure 5d; Table S1, Supporting Information) intrigue us to study the effect of pyrolyzing conditions such as temperature and time on the porosity of carbon products. Specific surface area up to around $2200\mathrm{m}^{2}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ could be obtained when pyrolyzed at $1100~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $3\textrm{h}$ or at $1000~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for $^{7\textrm{h}}$ (Figure 5e; Figures S9 and S10, Supporting Information). As far as we know, this is the highest value reported for carbon from biomass carbonization without introducing extra activation agents and comparable to the high end of chemical activation.[49] Further increasing the pyrolyzing temperature or time results in collapse of the micropores and thus lower surface area (Figures S9 and S10, Supporting Information). The pyrolyzed celery leaves (CL-carbon), unexpectedly, have specific surface area less than $500~\mathrm{m}^{2}~\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ at the same pyrolyzing conditions as celery stems (CS-carbon), although their metal ions contents are not low (Figure 5e; Figure S9, Supporting Information). This is largely due to the much lower micropore/ total pore volume ratio of CL-carbon $(8\%$ at $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$ than that of CS-carbon ( $35\%$ at $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C})$ . This indicates that the metal ions etch the celery leaves to form mesopores and macropores. As a result, the surface area of CL-carbon is much lower than the CS-carbon because micropores are more efficient to contribute to surface area. In spite of several times a difference of the surface area of the carbon materials from celery stems and leaves, CL-carbon shows capacitance of $130\mathrm{~F~}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , close to CS-carbon $(156\mathrm{~F~}\mathrm{g}^{-1})$ . As discussed earlier, only a portion of the pore surface is utilized for ion adsorption/desorption, especially for micropores. As the CL-carbon lacks micropores, the surface and near surface can be much easier accessed by electrolyte, leading to much higher surface utilization. The CL-carbon pyrolyzed at $800~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ has $85\%$ of the specific capacitance of CS-carbon at a scan rate of $5\mathrm{mV~s^{-1}}$ (Figure 5f,g). In other words, the areal capacitance of CL-carbon is $39.4\upmu\mathrm{F}\mathrm{cm}^{-2}$ which is about two times the theoretical double-layer capacitance of carbon materials $(\approx21~\upmu\mathrm{F}~\mathrm{cm}^{-2})$ and among the high end of area capacitance for carbon-based materials (Table S3, Supporting Information).[6,9,30,31,34–36,48,50–56] The abnormal high areal pseudocapacitance, most likely arising from the surface oxygen functional groups, however, is stable for thousands of cycles. This is definitely worthy of further study in a separate work. Although increasing the pyrolyzing temperature to $1100~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ leads to surface area up to $2200\mathrm{m}^{2}\mathrm{g}^{-1}$ , the capacitive performance does not have much improvement, confirming the pseudocapacitance contribution from surface functional groups (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Regarding asparagus lettuce, the specific surface area of the carbon materials from pyrolyzed stems (AS-carbon) and leaves (AL-carbon) and their corresponding capacitance are more or less proportional to the K contents in the stems and leaves (Figure 6).
