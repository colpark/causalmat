- path: /home/aid1/Documents/causalmat/matmech/Materials_Characterization/j.matchar.2015.10.016/images/88a4de0049d264cf163adf9234428e4f00c1732412e4daa53f8d340995f19dcd.jpg
- caption: Fig. 2. (a) Relative density and (b) Vickers hardness vs. amount of AlN additive in $\mathrm { Z r B } _ { 2 }$ – 30 vol% SiC composites sintered via different processes.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Measured relative densities of HP and PS samples are presented in Fig. 2a. As it can clearly be seen, fully dense composites were produced via hot pressing of the samples containing 1 and 5 wt.% AlN, whereas maximum relative density of PS samples was achieved in additive free (PS0) sample. Therefore, it appears that although the addition of AlN can promote densification in HP samples, it has an inverse effect on densification in PS samples. Also, it is obvious that the gap between relative densities of HP and PS samples fabricated with the same sintering temperatures increases with more AlN content.  The relationships between AlN content and Vickers hardness of both hot pressed and pressureless sintered $\mathrm { Z r B } _ { 2 }$ –SiC composites are shown in Fig. 2b. As in can be clearly seen, the hardness of the sample was increased by adding 1 wt.% AlN in the hot pressing process. The improvement in relative density caused by the addition of AlN was imputable to this enhancement. The addition of further AlN in HP samples caused the hardness to drop slightly, maybe due to the formation of soft graphite phase at the interfaces (this phenomenon will be discussed later). In comparison with HP samples, which have approached the theoretical density, the addition of AlN in PS samples caused the hardness to decrease. This phenomenon can be attributed to the sinking in relative density of PS samples due to the formation and entrapment of more gaseous products.

- path: /home/aid1/Documents/causalmat/matmech/Materials_Characterization/j.matchar.2015.10.016/images/0efdeb445cbcd05be17b0277a17edf881379143a6d2456232ad340e8875a782d.jpg
- caption: Fig. 3. SEM micrograph of the fracture surface of additive free (a) hot pressed and (b) pressureless sintered samples.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): SEM micrographs of the fracture surfaces of additive-free samples fabricated via hot pressing (HP0) and pressureless sintering (PS0) are presented in Fig. 3. As it can clearly be seen in HP0 sample, several formed contact areas between $\mathrm { Z r B } _ { 2 }$ and SiC particles provided multidirection diffusion paths which are accompanied with high atomic diffusion rate (promoted by approximately elevated temperatures and applied pressures) and finally led to progression of the sintering phenomenon. Albeit, high atomic diffusion rate resulted in the removal of most of the porosities and cavities, few of them remained in the microstructure, while some grains got coarsened (marked areas with thick markers in Fig. 3. a). The mixed fracture mode (inter/intra granular) of the fracture surface, which is indicated by narrow markers can also be considered as another sign of the sintering progress, as it implicates that there is no significant difference between the strength of the grain boundaries compared to the grains. Such a fracture mode endorses the occurrence of sintering via diffusion mechanism which is promoted by elevated temperatures and high applied pressure.  Fig. 3b which presents the fracture surface of the PS0 sample also indicates the occurrence of sintering phenomenon between the particles. But, comparatively, the sintering progress of PS0 sample is lower than that of HP0 sample. Features such as neck formation and the remaining effects of the departed particles which are marked by thick markers on Fig. 3b and considered as the visual characteristics of the sintered particles, are clearly smaller in size and lower in amount than those seen at HP0 sample. 
### F4
- path: /home/aid1/Documents/causalmat/matmech/Materials_Characterization/j.matchar.2015.10.016/images/eba9da129e6403fc5fcb8fb847e088f75bcdbee3e23e1b0d52e2154f8b9c8f2a.jpg
- caption: Fig. 4. SEM micrograph of the fracture surface and related EDS spectrum of the (a) point A in HP1 and (b) point B in PS1 samples.
- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): SEM micrographs and related EDS patterns of fracture surfaces of PS1 and HP1 samples are presented in Fig. 4. Compared to additive free samples, a fully dense composite was achieved by hot pressing the 1 wt.% AlN-doped mixture. In comparison with the reported results by Monteverde and Bellosi [19], in this work a fully-dense material obtained at significantly lower amount of AlN and applied pressure. The fracture surface includes thin layers of an apparently glassy phase. EDS pattern of such a phase (point A) is presented in Fig. 4a, and indicates the presence of several elements, mainly boron, carbon, silicon and zirconium with small amounts of nitrogen and aluminum. The fracture surface also shows the occurrence of wide grain growth in composite, whereas some grains have grown to sizes more than $1 0 \mu \mathrm { m }$ in diameter. This phenomenon is commonly caused by long heating times as well as the formation of liquid phases through liquid phase sintering (LPS) process. Aside from the formation of small amounts of aforementioned glassy phase which promotes the intergranular fracture, the dominant intragranular fracture mode of this sample can be considered as a sign of sintering progress, as discussed before. Contrary to HP1 sample, the amount of porosities increases at PS1 sample by adding $1 \mathrm { \ : w t } \%$ AlN as the sintering aid, compared to PS0 sample. In addition, the size of porosities is increased, although more necks formed between adjacent particles.  SEM micrograph of the fracture surface of the PS1 sample also includes some regions in which particles are joined via a glassy phase. Although the mentioned phase provides a good joining and keeps particles together and is expected to result in wide diffusion paths, it appears that the amount of sintering aid is not enough to cover all the particles of the composite. In a way that, in most regions, formed necks are similar to those existing in additive-free sample (PS0). However, the relative density of PS1 is approximately $8 \%$ less than that of PS0, and it may be due to the formation of some gaseous products as a result of AlN addition which cannot escape from the microstructure in absence of the applied external pressure. Also, EDS spectrum of formed glassy phase (Point B) in PS1 sample, which is presented in Fig. 4b, shows different chemical compositions from that observed in HP1 sample (Fig. 4a). These differences may occur due to different chemical reactions and the influences of the applied pressure, and will be discussed later.
