### M2  Structure → Performance
- cause: Atomic vacancies and rippled morphology providing active centers for conjugation
- effect: Cytocompatibility and cell encapsulation for biomedical applications
- experiment: Cell viability and proliferation assays | MTT assay, flow cytometry | params: MoS₂ concentrations from 0 to 10 mg/mL | result: High cell viability (>85%) at lower concentrations (<100 μg/mL); decreased viability (~60%) at higher concentrations (100 μg/mL to 10 mg/mL)
  - [experimental result] MoS₂ nanoassemblies interact strongly with PEG–SH via chemisorption at vacancy sites, forming covalent Mo–S bonds.
  - [experimental result] Raman spectra show disappearance of thiol peaks at 670 and 2570 cm⁻¹ in crosslinked hydrogels, indicating bond formation with MoS₂.
  - [experimental result] XPS analysis reveals shift in C 1s and Mo 3d core level peaks, confirming formation of C–S–Mo bonds.
  - [experimental result] Hydrogels exhibit compressive modulus increasing from 8.6 kPa to 27.7 kPa with MoS₂ concentration, enabling mechanical resilience.
  - [image description] Confocal microscopy shows >85% cell viability after 48 h encapsulation in PEG–SH/2% MoS₂ hydrogels.
  - [experimental result] Cells maintain normal spindle shape phenotype and show no adverse toxicity at macroscopic level with low MoS₂ concentrations.
  - [referenced knowledge] IC₅₀ was not observed even at 10 mg/mL MoS₂ concentration, demonstrating superior cytocompatibility compared to other 2D nanomaterials.
  - [deductive reasoning] Therefore, the presence of atomic vacancies enables formation of mechanically robust, cytocompatible hydrogels suitable for cell encapsulation and regenerative medicine.
