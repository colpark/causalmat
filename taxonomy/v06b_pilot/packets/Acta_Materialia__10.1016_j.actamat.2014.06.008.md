- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Au has been chosen here because of its high scattering power (the atomic scattering factors for $\mathrm{_X}$ -rays are proportional to the atomic number), large elastic anisotropy [32] and resistance to oxidation. Its elasticity anisotropy factor is around: C 2C4C4  2:9. Hence strong inter-grain and intra-grain stress heterogeneities are expected even in the absence of plastic events. The Au polycrystalline thin film, $475\mathrm{nm}$ thick, has been deposited by physical vapor deposition on a fused silica substrate. After deposition, lateral growth of the grains is induced by annealing in a flame for $30~\mathrm{s}$ at $800{-}900^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . The in-plane grain size, as revealed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) observations ranges between 0.3 and $3\upmu\mathrm{m}$ . A large square area of $150\times150~\upmu\mathrm{m}^{2}$ is etched by focused ion beam to remove the Au film except in the central area where a $15\times15\upmu\mathrm{m}^{2}$ large island is preserved. Thus one can easily locate the selected area of the polycrystalline thin film and perform different analysis, e.g. SEM, CXD or electron backscattering diffraction (EBSD), on the same area (Fig. 1). Mechanical loading was performed via thermal cycling between room temperature and $250~^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . Since silica and gold have different thermal expansion coefficients (TECs) a temperature excursion $\Delta T$ induces an in-plane strain in the film:  Grains are not isolated in direct space, but their diffraction peaks are spread in angular space because of the distribution of grain orientations (see Fig. 2(a)). According to laboratory texture measurements, the Au thin film exhibits a $\{111\}$ fiber texture. The dispersion of orientations around the surface normal is $5.03^{\circ}$ FWHM (mosaicity). In the synchrotron measurements the detector intercepts a section of the Debye ring. Because the beam size is only a few times larger than the grain size, the Debye ring is granular (spotty). To select a diffraction pattern isolated in the reciprocal space, a high-resolution f111g pole figure is recorded by performing a large rocking curve over a selected part of the polycrystalline film. The corresponding map is composed of several tens of diffraction peaks. One of the most isolated ones is chosen for high-resolution reciprocal space measurements (see Fig. 1(b)).  Grain identification is essential not only for mechanical interpretations but also for studying the phase-retrieval procedure. Localizing in real space the grain which corresponds to the isolated diffraction peak is difficult, especially since the grain size is of the order of a few hundreds of nanometers. Complex triangulation procedures are used in the so-called 3DXRD technique developed a decade ago [35,36]. Here an alternative approach is proposed to solve this issue. In a first step a crystallographic orientation map in a small square area $(\sim15\times15\upmu\mathrm{m})$ containing around 450 grains is recorded by EBSD. Since the grain structure is columnar, this surface analysis reflects the orientations throughout the thickness. These orientations are then compared with a $\{111\}$ high-resolution pole figure measured with X-rays where individual grain diffraction peaks are separated. This comparison allows the diffracting grain to be identified. The single grain recorded in this study is the one shown in Fig. 2(b).

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): In order to keep the high-resolution diffraction pattern of the grain in the detector during heating or cooling, small $25^{\circ}$ temperature increments were used. These increments are equivalent to $\sim0.03\%$ strain application in compression (heating up) or in tension (cooling down). For each step, after a time delay to allow the temperature to stabilize, the furnace vertical position was corrected and alignments were checked. The sample holder expansion along $z$ was calibrated beforehand. Finally the detector was slightly moved in order to keep the peak center in the vicinity of the detector center. Indeed, the peak center of gravity moves in $2\theta$ because of the strain evolution and the thermal expansion in the single grain. Roughly $^{1\textrm{h}}$ per temperature step is required to align and measure the 3-D maps. Because the beam size is around two times larger than the studied object, a small drift of few tens of nanometers has no consequence on the diffraction signal. From our experience, working with a nanobeam $(\sim100\mathrm{nm})$ is not reliable during in situ loading because of stability issues. - Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): As mentioned above, the inverse Fourier transform of the diffracted amplitude provides the displacement field within the grain. Only the square of the complex amplitude $I(\vec{q})\stackrel{_{-}}{=}|A(\vec{q})|^{2}$ is, however, experimentally measurable. This is the well-known problem of phase loss. Under certain conditions, however, it is possible to retrieve this lost phase. The technique is based on an extension of the Shannon theorem proposed by Sayre [39]. If a signal is sufficiently oversampled (i.e. diffracted intensity is measured with a sufficiently high resolution), there is sufficient information in the signal to recover the wavefield phase. Knowing the phase in the reciprocal space (i.e. the complex value of the diffracted amplitude), the electronic density and the displacement field of the object can be then deduced directly with an inverse Fourier transform. In practice, specific numeric computations are used, namely phase-retrieval algorithms [40–42]. In this part a quantitative reconstruction of the displacement field is obtained via these algorithms.  The temperature evolutions of two perpendicular direct space maps are shown in Fig. 6. At the beginning more heterogeneities are observed in the $y$ direction. As temperature increases, a large heterogeneity appears in the $x$ direction. As a consequence phase jumps are more uniformly distributed at $T=240{}^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ . During cooling down, these heterogeneities in $x$ decrease and exhibit a quasi-reversible behavior.

- Linked text (the paper's own sentences, as linked by the PDF parser): Along the film thickness, no phase jump is observed during the loading. Displacement heterogeneities are and remain weak in that direction. EBSD mapping enables the orientation of the five neighboring grains to be determined (see Fig. 7). All the five grains belong to the (111) texture film component. The misorientations between the grains are reported in Table 2. The angle $\Delta\psi_{\langle111\rangle}$ indicates the misorientation of the 111 directions which are normal to the film plane. This implies a difference in the biaxial modulus. Thus for the same applied strain, grains exhibit different stress values, and strain gradients arise from stress accommodation at the grain boundaries. Nevertheless because of the low $\Delta\psi_{\langle111\rangle}$ values, the elastic properties of the grains are almost identical. This cannot explain the increase in strain heterogeneities mainly in one direction during thermal loading. The $\Delta\phi_{\langle110\rangle}$ angle report the inplane misorientation of the grains. The grains $\#g2$ , #g4 and $\#g5$ exhibit an orientation very close to $\#g1$ , the grain which has been analyzed by CXD. These three grains are located on each side of $\#g1$ along the $y$ axis. On the other hand, $\#g3$ and $\#g6$ are more misoriented from $\#g1$ (see Fig. 7). The large strain heterogeneities observed during loading are directly correlated with the presence of misoriented grains. Moreover, the grain boundaries between $\#g1$ and $\#g6$ or $\#g3$ appear highly grooved on the SEM image. It can be hypothesized that defects cannot penetrate these particular boundaries. The storage of defects such as dislocations along this direction $(x)$ induces strain heterogeneities. In such a scenario the reversible behavior of the displacement field as a function of temperature could be a result of the reversible motion of defects within the grain.
